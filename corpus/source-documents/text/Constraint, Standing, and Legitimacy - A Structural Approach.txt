Constraint, Standing, and Legitimacy: A Structural Account of Pluralism

Jonathan Rademacher

March 21,2026

jonathandrademacher@yahoo.com

Abstract

Pluralist societies lack a shared final authority, rendering accounts of legitimacy grounded in consensus, procedure, or normative agreement structurally insufficient. This paper advances a structural alternative. It argues that legitimacy is not a function of agreement among agents, but of whether the system within which they interact composes consistently under its own constraints.

The analysis derives standing, capacity, and personal warrant as necessary conditions for participation, and identifies inclusion, symmetry, and directed repair as invariants required to preserve compositional consistency. It further shows that capacity and autonomy are functions of viable exit—defined as the existence of at least one path that preserves an agent’s continued participation—and that domination consists in the reduction of such viable exits.

These results are then formalized within a constraint algebra (Σᴿ), developed by the author in independent work, in which admissible configurations are those that remain closed under composition. The paper demonstrates that the pluralist invariants are equivalent to the conditions for constraint closure.

The central claim is that legitimacy in pluralist systems is identical with structural admissibility: only those configurations that preserve viable exit and remain consistent under composition can produce binding outcomes.

Keywords: Pluralism; Legitimacy; Non-Domination; Autonomy; Constraint Systems; Structural Consistency; Political Philosophy; Analytic Philosophy; Social Ontology; Agency; Coordination; Constraint Algebra (Σᴿ)

1. Introduction

1.1 The Problem of Pluralism

Pluralist societies are characterized by the persistent coexistence of agents who hold fundamentally divergent moral, religious, and epistemic commitments. This diversity is not merely contingent or temporary; it is structurally irreducible. That is, no mechanism internal to such a society guarantees convergence to a single, shared framework of ultimate justification. This claim is justified by observation of long-standing, stable disagreement across cultures, traditions, and philosophical systems, none of which has demonstrated the capacity to eliminate all others under conditions of free interaction.

From this, it follows that no universally accepted final authority—moral, epistemic, or metaphysical—can be presupposed as the basis for coordination. If such an authority existed and were universally recognized, pluralism would collapse into uniformity. Since pluralism persists, the absence of such authority must be treated as a structural condition of the system. The central problem of political and social philosophy under these conditions is therefore:

This problem is not reducible to preference aggregation or procedural design. It is a problem of how agents can coordinate in a way that produces binding structure without presupposing agreement on the grounds of that structure.

1.2 Limits of Existing Theories

Prominent approaches within the analytic tradition attempt to resolve this problem by appealing to mechanisms that mediate disagreement.

Rawlsian political liberalism grounds legitimacy in the idea of overlapping consensus and public reason. The claim is that, despite deep disagreement, agents can converge on a shared set of political principles that are justifiable to all under conditions of reasonableness. However, this approach presupposes that agents are already situated within a framework that allows such convergence to occur. The conditions under which public reason itself becomes binding are not derived from first principles but assumed.

Habermasian discourse ethics similarly grounds legitimacy in the procedural conditions of ideal communication. Legitimacy arises when norms are the outcome of free and equal participation in rational discourse. Yet the binding force of discourse presupposes that agents recognize one another as participants whose claims must be taken into account. This recognition is not generated by the procedure; it is a precondition for the procedure’s validity.

Pettit’s republican account of non-domination defines legitimacy in terms of the absence of arbitrary interference. Institutions are legitimate when they track the interests of those subject to them in a manner that prevents domination. However, the condition under which “tracking” is recognized as non-arbitrary again presupposes a structural relation between agents that is not itself derived within the theory.

In each case, the theory introduces a mechanism—consensus, discourse, or non-domination—that is intended to ground legitimacy. However, each mechanism operates within a space of relations whose structural properties are not themselves fully specified. This yields the following general claim:

For any of the above theories to function, agents must already satisfy minimal conditions such as recognizability, participation, and mutual relevance. These conditions are not outputs of the theories; they are necessary inputs. Since they are required for the theory to operate but are not derived within it, they constitute an implicit structural framework.

1.3 Structural Claim

The argument advanced in this paper is that legitimacy cannot be grounded in agreement, whether substantive or procedural. Agreement is contingent and may fail under conditions of persistent disagreement. Any account of legitimacy that depends on agreement therefore fails to apply in the general case of pluralism. Instead, legitimacy must be grounded in a property that is necessary for the system to function at all, independent of the particular beliefs or preferences of its agents. The proposed claim is:

Consider any system of interacting agents. For the system to be mathematically or logically valid, its operations must compose without contradiction. That is, if agents interact through a series of relations or transformations, the outcome must be well-defined and invariant under admissible compositions. If this condition fails—if different paths of interaction yield incompatible results—then the system cannot be relied upon to produce stable or coherent outcomes. In such a case, no structure generated by the system can be considered binding, since its results are not determinate.

Therefore, consistency under composition is a necessary condition for the existence of any valid structure. Since legitimacy is a property of structures that bind agents, and binding requires determinacy, it follows that legitimacy must be grounded in the consistency of the system that generates it.

Agreement may occur within such a system, but it is neither necessary nor sufficient for legitimacy. A system may exhibit agreement while remaining structurally inconsistent, and thus unstable. Conversely, a system may sustain disagreement while remaining consistent, and thus capable of producing binding outcomes.

1.4 Contribution

This paper develops a formal account of pluralist legitimacy by treating it as a constraint system and analyzing the conditions under which that system is closed under composition. The central contribution is the following:

A constraint-complete system is one in which all necessary conditions for consistent composition are explicitly represented. In such a system, any configuration that violates these conditions is inadmissible, and any admissible configuration is guaranteed to compose without contradiction.

The method employed does not introduce new normative principles. Instead, it extracts the minimal structural invariants required for a pluralist system to remain coherent. These invariants are then expressed in a form that allows evaluation of whether a given system satisfies them. The result is a framework in which legitimacy is not selected, imposed, or derived from agreement, but emerges as the only admissible structure under complete constraint enforcement.

2. Standing as a Structural Primitive

2.1 What is Standing?

The concept of standing is introduced here as a primitive structural relation within a system of interacting agents. It is necessary to define this concept with precision in order to avoid conflation with existing normative or institutional notions.

Standing is not equivalent to moral worth. Moral worth refers to evaluative judgments concerning the value of an agent under a given moral framework. Such judgments presuppose the existence of a shared evaluative standard. Under conditions of pluralism, no such universally accepted standard exists. Therefore, any definition of standing that depends on moral worth would fail to apply in the general case, as it would require prior agreement on evaluative criteria.

Standing is also not equivalent to authority. Authority refers to the capacity of an agent to impose binding directives on others. Authority is typically derivative: it arises from institutional arrangements, procedures, or recognized hierarchies. These arrangements themselves presuppose a prior structure within which agents are recognized as participants. Therefore, authority cannot serve as the foundational concept for that structure. Standing is instead defined as:

Consider a system consisting of a set of entities and a set of relations defined over those entities. For any entity to participate in the system in a meaningful way, it must be possible to form relations involving that entity that compose with other relations in the system. If no such relations exist, the entity cannot enter into any sequence of operations whose outcomes are defined within the system. In that case, the entity is structurally indistinguishable from absence with respect to the system’s operations.

Therefore, participation is not a matter of value or authority, but of the existence of admissible relations. The minimal condition for such participation is the existence of at least one relation involving the entity that composes consistently with the rest of the system. This condition is what is here termed standing.

2.3 Why Standing Matters

The necessity of standing follows from the requirement that any system capable of generating binding structure must support consistent composition among its elements. First, coordination requires standing.

Coordination consists in the formation of relations among agents whose outcomes depend on the composition of those relations. If an agent lacks standing, it cannot participate in any relation. Therefore, it cannot be part of any coordinated structure. A system that includes agents without standing is one in which those agents are structurally excluded from coordination. Such exclusion is not merely descriptive but has formal consequences: the system’s relational structure is incomplete with respect to those agents. Second, legitimacy depends on standing.

Legitimacy is a property of structures that generate outcomes binding on agents. For a structure to be binding on an agent, the agent must be included in the set of relations that define that structure. If an agent is in nullity, no such relations exist, and the structure has no defined operation with respect to that agent. In that case, the structure cannot be said to bind the agent in any meaningful sense, since binding presupposes the existence of a relation through which the binding occurs.

Therefore, any claim to legitimacy over an agent requires that the agent possess standing. Without standing, there is no relation, and without relation, there is no binding. It follows that standing is a necessary condition for legitimacy.

2.4 Section Conclusion

Standing, as defined here, is the minimal structural condition for participation in a system of composable relations. It is neither evaluative nor hierarchical, but purely relational. Its absence corresponds to nullity, and its emergence is captured by the successor operation. Coordination and legitimacy both depend on standing, not as a normative premise, but as a structural necessity.

3. Structural Invariants of Pluralism

3.1 Standing, Capacity, and the Conditions of Participation

The account of standing developed in the preceding section must now be refined, because as it stands, it admits an ambiguity that cannot be left unresolved without compromising the argument that follows. Standing was initially introduced as the condition under which an agent may participate in relations. However, this formulation leaves open the question of what it means, in structural terms, for such participation to occur.

It is possible, at least in abstraction, to describe a system in which an agent is formally included within a relation while exerting no influence over the structure or outcome of that relation. In such a case, the agent is present in the description of the system but absent from its operation. This distinction between formal inclusion and effective participation is not merely terminological; it has direct consequences for the coherence of the system.

To see this, consider a system of relations whose outcomes are determined by the composition of those relations. Suppose that for some agent, the inclusion or exclusion of that agent makes no difference to any possible composition within the system. That is, for every admissible sequence of relations, the outcome remains invariant regardless of whether the agent is included. Under such conditions, the system does not register the presence of the agent at all. The agent is, in effect, invisible to the structure of the system.

This invisibility is not a contingent defect but a structural equivalence. If the system cannot distinguish between the presence and absence of an agent in any of its operations, then, for all structural purposes, the agent does not exist within that system. What appears as inclusion collapses into nullity.

At this point, a possible objection arises. One might argue that formal inclusion should be treated as sufficient, on the grounds that participation need not always be causally efficacious in order to be recognized. That is, one might claim that the mere availability of participation, even if not exercised or not impactful, is enough to ground standing.

This objection fails on structural grounds. The issue is not whether participation is exercised, but whether it is capable of affecting outcomes. A system in which the presence of an agent never alters any compositional result is indistinguishable from a system in which that agent is absent. Since structural analysis is concerned precisely with the behavior of compositions, any distinction that does not register at that level cannot be sustained.

Thus, standing cannot be identified with mere inclusion. If standing is to mark the possibility of participation in a meaningful sense, then it must require that the agent’s presence has consequence within the system’s compositional structure. This condition is what will be termed capacity.

Capacity is not an additional property that an agent may or may not possess alongside standing. Rather, it is constitutive of standing itself. To say that an agent has standing is to say that the agent’s participation can affect the outcome of relations in which it is involved. Without such capacity, there is no participation, and without participation, there is no standing.

It follows that any system that purports to include agents without granting them capacity is not merely unjust or undesirable; it is structurally incoherent, because it fails to distinguish between presence and absence within its own operational logic.

3.2 Personal Warrant and the Internal Condition of Agents

If standing requires capacity, and capacity requires that an agent’s participation have determinate consequences, then the argument must proceed one step further. It must be possible to explain how agents are able to participate in relations in a manner that preserves determinacy. This requires that the internal structure of agents themselves be brought into view.

An agent does not encounter relations as raw data. Rather, relations are interpreted through a structured perceptual and evaluative framework. This framework determines how the agent distinguishes between different states of affairs, how those states are assessed, and how responses are generated. The coherence of this framework is therefore a precondition for any meaningful participation in a system of relations. This internal coherence is what will be termed personal warrant.

To say that an agent possesses personal warrant is to say that the agent’s perceptual and evaluative operations are internally consistent. Identical inputs must be capable of producing identical interpretations, and those interpretations must give rise to responses that are stable across instances. If this condition is not met, then the agent’s participation becomes indeterminate: the same relation may produce different outcomes depending on arbitrary variation within the agent’s internal structure.

At this point, an objection may be raised. It may be argued that agents frequently exhibit inconsistency in practice, and yet coordination still occurs. If this is so, then internal consistency cannot be necessary in the strong sense being claimed.

This objection rests on a conflation between empirical approximation and structural requirement. It is true that agents may behave inconsistently in practice. However, insofar as coordination succeeds, it does so only to the extent that agents approximate consistency. Where inconsistency becomes sufficiently pronounced, coordination breaks down. Thus, empirical success presupposes structural approximation to consistency, even if that consistency is not perfectly realized.

To make this explicit: if an agent’s responses to identical relational inputs vary without constraint, then no system-level regularity can be maintained. The system cannot rely on that agent’s participation to produce stable outcomes. Such an agent introduces noise rather than structure. A system composed entirely of such agents would be incapable of sustaining any consistent pattern of coordination. Thus, internal consistency is not an idealization imposed from outside; it is a condition that must be satisfied, at least approximately, for participation to be meaningful at all.

A further question now arises. If each agent possesses its own internally coherent framework, how are these frameworks to be related to one another? Specifically, can one agent’s framework be treated as structurally superior to another’s? Under pluralism, the answer must be negative.

To privilege one framework over another requires a justification that is binding across all agents. Such a justification would have to be expressed in terms that all agents must accept, regardless of their own frameworks. However, any such justification is itself articulated within a particular framework. It therefore cannot achieve universal binding force without presupposing the very authority whose absence defines pluralism.

This is not merely a sociological observation, but a structural constraint. Any attempt to rank frameworks presupposes a meta-framework that is itself subject to the same problem. This produces an infinite regress unless a final authority is assumed. Since pluralism is defined precisely by the absence of such an authority, no such ranking can be structurally sustained.

It follows that all internally consistent perceptual frameworks must be treated as structurally symmetrical. This symmetry is not a claim about truth or correctness. It is a claim about admissibility. Any internally consistent agent must be admitted as a participant without prior evaluation of its internal content.

3.3 Axiom 1 — Pluralistic Inclusion

Every internally consistent agent must be capable of being integrated into the system as a participant whose presence has compositional consequence. This is the Axiom of Pluralistic Inclusion.

The necessity of this axiom follows from the conditions already established. Internally consistent agents are structurally admissible. To exclude such an agent is therefore to exclude an element that satisfies all internal conditions for participation.

An objection might be raised at this point: one might argue that exclusion is sometimes necessary in order to preserve coherence, for example by excluding agents whose participation would disrupt the system. This objection misunderstands the level at which the argument is operating. The claim is not that all agents must be included in all relations, nor that no constraints may be imposed on participation. The claim is that the system must admit at least one admissible path through which any internally consistent agent may participate.

To deny even this minimal condition is to assert that the system contains elements that it cannot incorporate under any circumstances. Such a system is not merely restrictive; it is incomplete. It fails to define relations for elements that it nonetheless contains. A system that fails to define relations for its own elements cannot be closed under composition. Since closure is required for consistency, and consistency is required for determinacy, it follows that permanent exclusion is structurally inadmissible. Thus, inclusion is not a normative demand for fairness, but a logical requirement for completeness.

3.4 Axiom 2 — Symmetry of Standing and Capacity

If agents enter into relations with equal standing and equal capacity, then no admissible sequence of relations may transform that equality into persistent inequality. This is the Axiom of Symmetry of Standing and Capacity. The force of this axiom lies in the requirement that compositional systems treat identical inputs identically. If two agents are indistinguishable at the level of standing and capacity, then any distinction in outcomes must arise from the structure of the relations themselves.

If such distinctions are permitted, then the system’s behavior becomes dependent on the particular sequence of relations applied. Identical initial conditions no longer guarantee identical outcomes. This is the defining feature of path dependence. An objection may be raised here as well. It might be argued that real systems frequently produce unequal outcomes from equal starting points, and that such inequality does not necessarily imply inconsistency.

Again, the response depends on distinguishing empirical observation from structural requirement. The claim is not that inequality never occurs, but that a system that systematically produces unequal outcomes from equal inputs cannot maintain invariant behavior under composition. Where such inequality persists, the system’s outcomes become contingent on the path taken rather than the state itself.

This undermines determinacy. A system that cannot guarantee consistent outcomes from identical conditions cannot serve as the basis for binding relations, because it cannot specify what those relations will produce. Thus, symmetry is not an ethical commitment but a structural constraint: identical conditions must yield identical outcomes if the system is to remain coherent.

3.5 Axiom 3 — Directed Repair

The preceding axioms establish conditions that must hold at any given moment. However, plural systems are dynamic. They evolve through sequences of relations, and these sequences may produce deviations from symmetry even if symmetry is required.

For this reason, a third invariant is necessary. Whenever a coordination produces a deviation from symmetry, the system must admit further relations through which that deviation can be corrected. This is the Axiom of Directed Repair.

The necessity of this axiom can be seen by considering what would occur in its absence. If asymmetries arise and cannot be corrected, then over time those asymmetries accumulate. Accumulated asymmetry produces precisely the path dependence that symmetry is intended to prevent. An objection might be raised that not all asymmetries are problematic, and that some degree of inequality may be compatible with stability.

The response is that the issue is not inequality as such, but uncorrectable inequality. Temporary deviations do not violate structural consistency so long as they remain within a system that can restore symmetry. It is the persistence of asymmetry without the possibility of correction that generates inconsistency. Thus, Directed Repair does not require that all inequalities be eliminated immediately. It requires that the system contain the means by which such inequalities can be resolved.

3.6 The Conditional Emergence of Evaluation

The foregoing analysis makes it possible to clarify the role of evaluation within plural systems. It is often assumed that evaluation—of beliefs, claims, or agents—is the primary mechanism through which coordination is achieved. On this view, agents must assess one another’s positions and arrive at judgments that determine how coordination proceeds. The present account suggests a reversal of this assumption.

So long as agents possess internal consistency, and so long as the system satisfies the invariants of inclusion, symmetry, and repair, coordination can proceed without any need to evaluate the internal warrants of agents. The system operates by enforcing structural conditions, not by adjudicating content.

Evaluation becomes necessary only when these conditions fail. When relations do not compose, when asymmetries cannot be resolved, or when outcomes diverge, the system encounters inconsistency. At that point, evaluation serves as a diagnostic and corrective process. Thus, evaluation is not foundational. It is conditional. It arises only in response to structural failure.

3.7 Final Interpretation

The invariants identified in this section—Pluralistic Inclusion, Symmetry of Standing and Capacity, and Directed Repair—do not prescribe what agents ought to believe or value. They do not determine outcomes in advance. Rather, they define the conditions under which any system of plural agents can sustain consistent interaction.

These conditions operate at a level prior to agreement. They do not require consensus, shared morality, or procedural convergence. Instead, they ensure that the system is capable of producing determinate outcomes regardless of the diversity of its participants.

Under these conditions, legitimacy is not constructed through agreement or imposed by authority. It is identical with the persistence of compositional consistency across a system of agents whose participation is both effective and structurally integrated. In this sense, legitimacy is not something that must be justified in advance. It is what remains once all configurations that fail to compose consistently have been excluded.

4. From Philosophy to Constraint Systems

4.1 Structure as Constraint

The argument developed thus far has proceeded entirely in philosophical terms. It has shown that pluralist systems are governed not by agreement, but by structural conditions that determine whether relations among agents can compose without contradiction. In particular, it has been established that standing, capacity, and personal warrant define the admissibility of agents, and that inclusion, symmetry, and repair define the invariants necessary for maintaining consistency.

The present task is not to introduce a new domain, but to clarify the form that these structural conditions already take. In order to do so, it is necessary to reconsider what is meant by “structure” itself.

In ordinary usage, structure is often understood as an arrangement of elements or relations. However, this description is incomplete. To say that a system has a structure is not merely to say that relations exist, but that not all relations are possible. Some relations are permitted, while others are excluded. The structure of the system is therefore defined not only by what is present, but by what is ruled out.

This observation can be made precise by considering even the simplest mathematical expressions. To assert that “2 + 2 = 4” is not merely to identify a result, but to exclude all alternative results. It is to assert that “2 + 2 ≠ 5,” “2 + 2 ≠ 3,” and so on. The expression functions by delimiting a space of admissible outcomes.

This is not an accidental feature of arithmetic; it is intrinsic to any system capable of producing determinate results. A relation that did not exclude alternatives would not determine anything at all. It would leave all outcomes equally possible, and thus fail to produce a result.

From this it follows that what is ordinarily described as a function or relation is, at a more fundamental level, a constraint. It does not merely generate outcomes; it restricts the space of possible outcomes to those that satisfy its condition.

At this point, an objection might be raised. One might insist that functions are essentially generative, in that they produce outputs from inputs, and that to describe them as constraints is to mischaracterize their primary role.

This objection rests on a false separation between generation and restriction. To produce a determinate output is necessarily to exclude all alternative outputs. If a function did not exclude alternatives, it would not produce a unique result. Thus, generation presupposes restriction. The generative aspect of a function is intelligible only because the space of possibilities has already been constrained.

Accordingly, it is more precise to say that a function determines its output by constraining the admissible relations between inputs and outputs. Once this point is established, it can be generalized. Any system that produces determinate outcomes—whether mathematical, logical, or social—must do so by imposing constraints on what is admissible within that system. The structure of the system is therefore identical with the set of constraints that define its admissible configurations.

4.2 Consistency and the Determinacy of Structure

If structure is understood as a system of constraints, then the next question is how such a system can be said to be valid. The answer must be derived from the requirement that the system produce determinate outcomes.

A system of relations is used to generate results through composition. Relations are combined, transformed, and extended in order to produce outcomes that are meant to be binding or meaningful. For this to be possible, the system must ensure that these compositions yield well-defined results.

To say that a result is well-defined is to say that it does not depend on arbitrary variation in the sequence of operations used to produce it. If two sequences of admissible relations begin from the same initial condition, they must yield the same outcome. Otherwise, the system fails to determine what the outcome is. This condition can be restated as follows: a system is valid only if it is consistent under composition.

This statement does not introduce a new criterion; it makes explicit what is already required for any system to function. To see this, suppose that consistency fails. Then there exist two admissible sequences of relations, each permitted by the system, which produce different outcomes from the same initial state. In such a case, the system cannot determine which outcome is correct, because both are generated by admissible operations.

The system therefore fails to produce a determinate result. Any outcome it yields can be contradicted by another admissible sequence. This undermines the possibility of binding structure, since no result can be taken as definitive.

An objection might be raised that some systems tolerate local inconsistencies without collapsing entirely. However, this objection fails upon closer examination. A local inconsistency, if it is genuinely part of the system, will propagate through composition. Any relation that depends on the inconsistent component will inherit its indeterminacy. Over time, this indeterminacy spreads, undermining the system’s ability to produce stable results.

Thus, the distinction between local and global consistency is not fundamental. A system is either consistent throughout or not consistent at all. Partial consistency is not sufficient to sustain determinacy. At this point, the notation introduced earlier can be brought into alignment with this argument.

Given a set of constraints , let the set of admissible relations be understood as those relations that satisfy all constraints in . These relations may be composed to produce outcomes. The system is consistent if and only if all such compositions yield invariant results. Again, the notation adds nothing new. It expresses, in formal terms, the philosophical requirement that a system must produce determinate outcomes in order to be valid.

4.3 Constraint Systems as the Formal Expression of Plural Structure

The argument can now return to the philosophical concepts developed earlier and restate them in the language of constraints. Standing was defined as the condition under which an agent’s presence has consequence within composable relations. This can now be understood as the requirement that there exist admissible relations in that include the agent and whose outcomes depend on that inclusion.

Capacity was defined as the condition that participation affects outcomes. In constraint terms, this means that the admissibility of relations depends on the presence of the agent. The agent is not merely included; it enters into the conditions that define admissibility.

Personal warrant was defined as the internal consistency of an agent’s perceptual framework. In constraint terms, this corresponds to the requirement that the transformations contributed by the agent do not introduce contradictions into the system. An agent whose internal operations are inconsistent would violate the constraints that define admissible composition.

The invariants identified in Section 3 can now be expressed as elements of the constraint set :

Pluralistic Inclusion corresponds to constraints requiring that all admissible agents can be integrated into the system

Symmetry corresponds to constraints requiring that equal conditions yield equal outcomes under composition

Directed Repair corresponds to constraints requiring that deviations from admissibility can be corrected

This translation does not alter the content of the argument. It shows that the philosophical structure already developed is equivalent to a constraint system.

4.4 Closure and the Condition of Legitimacy

The final step is to identify the condition under which a constraint system can be said to be complete. A system of constraints defines a set of admissible relations. These relations are combined through composition. For the system to remain valid, it must be the case that the composition of admissible relations is itself admissible.

If this condition fails, then the system permits operations that generate outcomes outside its own constraints. In such a case, the system undermines itself. It allows the production of relations that it does not recognize as valid. This condition is known as closure.

Closure is not an additional requirement imposed on the system; it is the condition under which the system’s constraints can be maintained across compositions.

To see this, suppose that closure fails. Then there exist admissible relations whose composition produces an inadmissible result. This means that the system permits operations that violate its own defining conditions. Such a system cannot maintain consistency, because it generates contradictions through its own operations.

Conversely, if the system is closed, then all compositions of admissible relations remain admissible. The system preserves its constraints across all operations. It is therefore consistent. At this point, the connection to the philosophical account of legitimacy becomes explicit.

Legitimacy was defined as the condition under which a plural system produces binding outcomes. Binding outcomes require determinacy, and determinacy requires consistency under composition. Consistency, in turn, requires closure under constraints. Thus, legitimacy is not an independent property that must be added to a system. It is identical with the condition that the system is closed under the constraints that define its structure.

The invariants of inclusion, symmetry, and repair can now be understood as precisely those constraints required to ensure closure. Inclusion ensures that all elements can be incorporated into admissible relations. Symmetry ensures that composition preserves equivalence. Repair ensures that deviations do not accumulate into inconsistency.

Taken together, these constraints define a system in which all admissible compositions remain within the space of admissibility. Within such a system, no further justification is required. Legitimacy is not derived from agreement or imposed by authority. It emerges as the only possible condition under which the system can remain coherent.

5. Constraint Algebra (Σᴿ)

5.1 From Constraint Systems to Constraint Algebra

The previous section established that any system capable of producing determinate outcomes must be understood as a system of constraints. It further showed that validity consists in consistency under composition and that such consistency requires closure. At that point, the analysis remained at the level of describing what a system must satisfy in order to be structurally coherent.

The present task is to move from that descriptive level to an operational one. The question is no longer merely what constraints exist, but how those constraints themselves may be treated as objects of analysis.

To see why this step is necessary, consider the following. In any system defined by constraints, one does not merely encounter individual constraints in isolation. One encounters collections of constraints that interact. These interactions determine whether a given configuration is admissible, whether a proposed relation can be introduced, and whether a sequence of operations will remain consistent.

Thus, it is not sufficient to describe constraints; it is necessary to consider how constraints themselves may be combined, compared, and evaluated. This requires treating constraints not merely as conditions, but as elements within a structure. This motivates the introduction of what will be called a constraint algebra.

This definition must be understood carefully. It does not assert that values or states are irrelevant. Rather, it asserts that the admissibility of values and states is entirely determined by the constraints that govern them. 5.2 Operation on Constraints Rather Than Values

The defining feature of the constraint algebra is that it operates directly on constraints rather than on values.

This statement requires justification, as it departs from the usual interpretation of mathematical systems. In standard formulations, operations are defined on values: numbers are added, functions are applied, sets are combined. Constraints appear as conditions that these operations must satisfy, but they are not themselves treated as primary objects.

However, as established in Section 4, any operation on values presupposes a constraint structure that determines which operations are admissible. The value-level operations are therefore derivative: they occur within a space already defined by constraints.

5.4 The Diagnostic Role of Constraint Algebra

Once constraints are treated as objects within an algebra, it becomes possible to analyze not only whether a given relation is admissible, but whether the constraint system itself is complete. This gives rise to the diagnostic function of the constraint algebra.

This claim must be understood carefully. To say that a constraint system is incomplete is to say that it admits configurations that cannot be consistently composed, or that it fails to exclude configurations that lead to inconsistency. In either case, the system does not fully specify the conditions required for consistency.

The constraint algebra detects such incompleteness by examining whether closure holds. If there exist admissible relations whose composition yields an inadmissible result, then the constraint system is incomplete: it has failed to account for all necessary conditions.

Conversely, if closure holds, then the constraint system is complete with respect to the operations it admits. This diagnostic role is not an external addition to the system. It is inherent in the definition of the algebra. To operate on constraints is to make explicit whether those constraints suffice to maintain consistency.

5.5 Non-Constructive Character of Constraint Algebra

A final point must be made in order to distinguish the present framework from more familiar approaches. Constraint algebra does not operate by selecting solutions from a space of possibilities. It operates by eliminating configurations that violate constraints.

This distinction is essential. In optimization-based systems, one begins with a set of possible states and selects among them according to some criterion. In such systems, the question is which solution is best. In a constraint system, by contrast, the question is which configurations are admissible at all. There is no ranking among admissible configurations within the algebra itself. The algebra determines only whether a configuration can exist without contradiction.

An objection might be raised that elimination is itself a form of selection. However, this objection confuses two distinct operations. Selection requires comparison among admissible alternatives according to some criterion. Elimination requires only the identification of inconsistency. It does not compare admissible states; it excludes inadmissible ones.

Thus, the constraint algebra is non-constructive in the following sense: it does not construct solutions by choosing among alternatives but reveals the structure within which any solution must reside.

6. Main Result: Structural Equivalence and the Derivation of Non-Domination

6.1 Capacity Reconsidered: From Participation to Preservation

The concept of capacity, as introduced in earlier sections, was defined as the condition under which an agent’s participation has consequence within a system of relations. This definition was sufficient to distinguish standing from mere formal inclusion. However, it remains incomplete for present purposes, because it does not yet explain what it means for participation to be sustained.

The issue can be stated as follows. An agent may participate in a relation and affect its outcome, and yet the result of that participation may eliminate the agent’s ability to participate in any subsequent relation. In such a case, the agent’s participation is real but self-terminating. The agent acts once, and in so doing forfeits the possibility of acting again.

If such cases are admitted without qualification, then capacity becomes momentary rather than structural. It attaches to isolated acts rather than to the ongoing condition of participation. This is incompatible with the role that capacity must play in a system intended to generate stable, composable outcomes over time. It follows that capacity must be defined not merely in terms of immediate consequence, but in terms of the preservation of the conditions under which consequence remains possible. This requires the introduction of a further distinction.

6.2 Formal Options and Viable Options

At first approximation, one might attempt to define capacity in terms of the number of options available to an agent. An agent appears to possess greater capacity if it can choose among many alternatives, and less capacity if it can choose among few. However, this interpretation fails under closer examination, because it does not distinguish between formal options and viable options.

A formal option is any action that is presented as available to an agent within the system. A viable option, by contrast, is an action whose consequences preserve the agent’s continued capacity to participate. The distinction becomes unavoidable when one considers cases in which multiple formal options exist, but none of them preserve the agent’s capacity.

To make this explicit, consider an agent confronted with a set of alternatives. Each alternative leads to an outcome that eliminates the agent’s ability to participate in any future relation. In such a case, the agent is formally presented with a choice, but structurally, no path preserves its participation.

The multiplicity of options is therefore illusory. The agent is not choosing among different futures; it is selecting among different paths to the same terminal condition.

This demonstrates that the existence of formal options does not suffice to establish capacity. Capacity depends on whether at least one option preserves the conditions under which participation remains possible.

6.3 Viable Exit as the Ground of Capacity

The preceding analysis makes it possible to define a concept that captures the condition just described. A viable exit is a path of action available to an agent such that, if taken, the agent remains capable of participating in subsequent relations.

This definition must be understood as structural rather than instrumental. A viable exit is not defined by whether it achieves a desired outcome, but by whether it preserves the conditions under which the agent remains a participant in the system. At this point, the earlier definition of capacity can be refined. An agent possesses capacity if and only if there exists at least one viable exit available to it.

6.4 Autonomy as Structured Viability

The concept of autonomy can now be derived from the structure of viable exit. Autonomy is often defined in terms of freedom of choice. However, as the preceding analysis has shown, the presence of choice is not sufficient. What matters is whether choice preserves the agent’s capacity. Accordingly, autonomy must be defined in terms of viability rather than multiplicity. An agent is autonomous if it possesses at least one viable exit.

This definition may appear minimal, but it is precisely calibrated to the structural conditions of the system. Autonomy does not require that all options be viable, nor that multiple viable options exist. It requires only that the agent not be forced into a position in which all available paths eliminate its capacity.

An objection might be raised that this definition is too weak, in that it allows for cases in which an agent has only a single viable option. However, the objection misunderstands the level of analysis. The present concern is not to maximize freedom, but to identify the minimal condition under which participation remains meaningful. That condition is satisfied so long as at least one viable path remains.

If capacity depends on the existence of viable exits, and autonomy depends on the preservation of such exits, then any process that reduces the set of viable exits reduces capacity and autonomy.

7. Consequences

7.1 Reframing Legitimacy as Structural Admissibility

The analysis conducted in the preceding sections establishes that legitimacy cannot be understood as a product of agreement among agents, nor as the outcome of any particular procedural arrangement. This conclusion follows directly from the structural conditions of pluralism itself.

Pluralism, as defined earlier, entails the absence of a shared final authority. Under such conditions, no substantive framework—moral, epistemic, or otherwise—can claim universal validity across all agents. Any attempt to ground legitimacy in agreement must therefore presuppose what pluralism denies: that there exists some common basis upon which agreement can be achieved and recognized as binding.

The failure of this presupposition does not merely weaken consensus-based accounts of legitimacy; it renders them structurally insufficient. Agreement may occur, but it cannot serve as the condition that makes outcomes binding, because the validity of agreement itself cannot be secured without appeal to a prior structure.

The same limitation applies to procedural accounts. Procedures may specify how decisions are to be made, but they cannot by themselves establish why those decisions should be binding on all participants. Any procedure presupposes conditions under which participation is meaningful, outcomes are determinate, and results are stable under repetition. These conditions are not generated by the procedure; they are required for the procedure to function at all. It follows that legitimacy must be grounded at a level prior to both agreement and procedure.

The preceding sections have shown that this level is structural. Legitimacy arises not from what agents agree upon, but from whether the system in which they operate satisfies the conditions required for compositional consistency. Those conditions were identified as the preservation of viable exit, the maintenance of symmetry, and the integrability of all admissible agents. From this it follows that legitimacy is most precisely understood as structural admissibility.

This statement requires clarification. To say that a configuration is structurally admissible is to say that it satisfies all constraints necessary for consistent composition. Such a configuration does not produce contradictions under any admissible sequence of relations. It preserves the conditions under which agents remain participants, maintains symmetry where it is required, and admits correction where deviations occur.

An objection may be raised that this definition appears to reduce legitimacy to a purely formal property, thereby excluding considerations of justice, fairness, or value. However, this objection misunderstands the level at which the present analysis operates. The claim is not that all morally desirable outcomes are captured by structural admissibility, but that no outcome can be legitimate if it fails to satisfy structural admissibility. Normative considerations may operate within the space of admissible configurations, but they cannot override the constraints that define that space.

Thus, legitimacy is not exhausted by structural admissibility, but it is bounded by it. Anything that falls outside the space of admissible configurations cannot be rendered legitimate by appeal to agreement or procedure, because it fails at the level of consistency.

7.2 The Displacement of Final Authority

A second consequence follows directly from the identification of legitimacy with structural admissibility. If legitimacy is determined by whether a system satisfies the conditions for closure under its constraints, then the role traditionally assigned to authority must be reconsidered.

In classical accounts, authority functions as the source of legitimacy. Decisions are binding because they are issued by an authority recognized as legitimate. The problem of pluralism arises precisely because no such authority can be universally recognized. The analysis developed here shows that this problem can be dissolved rather than solved.

Authority is not required if the conditions for legitimacy are structural rather than personal or institutional. If a system is closed under its constraints—if it preserves viable exit, maintains symmetry, and admits repair—then the outcomes it produces are binding not because they are endorsed by an authority, but because they are the only outcomes that do not violate the system’s own conditions of coherence. In this sense, closure replaces authority.

To make this explicit: where authority would ordinarily provide justification for why a decision must be accepted, closure provides a structural reason why no alternative can be consistently maintained. The binding force of outcomes arises not from endorsement, but from the impossibility of sustaining contradictory alternatives within the system. This leads to a corresponding shift in the role of justification.

In traditional accounts, justification consists in providing reasons that agents can accept. In the present framework, justification is replaced by consistency. A configuration is not justified because it is accepted; it is justified because any attempt to reject it would produce inconsistency within the system. This can be stated succinctly:

An objection might be raised that this account eliminates the need for reasons altogether, reducing legitimacy to a purely mechanical process. However, this objection again misidentifies the level of analysis. Reasons remain relevant within the space of admissible configurations, where agents must still interpret, deliberate, and act. What is displaced is not reasoning itself, but the idea that legitimacy depends on the successful provision of universally acceptable reasons. Instead, legitimacy depends on whether the outcomes of reasoning can be sustained within a system that preserves the conditions of participation for all agents.

7.3 Stability and the Persistence of Constraint-Consistent Systems

A third consequence concerns the stability of systems over time. The analysis of Section 6 established that domination corresponds to the reduction of viable exit, and that such reduction leads to the collapse of capacity and, ultimately, to the breakdown of compositional consistency. Systems that admit domination therefore undermine their own structural conditions.

From this it follows that systems which fail to preserve viable exit are not merely unjust or undesirable; they are unstable. To see this, consider a system in which the set of viable exits available to some agents is progressively reduced. As this reduction proceeds, those agents lose the capacity to participate in relations in a meaningful way. Their actions cease to affect outcomes, and their presence becomes structurally negligible.

At this point, two possibilities arise. First, the system may continue to operate as if those agents remain participants. In this case, it produces outcomes that no longer reflect the structure of participation it purports to maintain. The system becomes internally inconsistent: it represents itself as including agents whose participation has, in fact, been eliminated.

Second, the system may adapt by excluding those agents entirely. In this case, it violates the condition of inclusion and reduces its own domain. If this process continues, the system collapses into a degenerate form in which only a subset of agents remain capable of participation. In either case, the system fails to maintain the conditions required for closure.

By contrast, a system that preserves viable exit for all agents maintains capacity, symmetry, and the possibility of repair. Such a system remains closed under its constraints, and therefore consistent. This leads to the following conclusion:

This statement should not be understood as an empirical generalization about which systems survive in practice. It is a structural claim about what it means for a system to persist as a coherent system at all.

A system that fails to preserve its constraints may continue to exist in a descriptive sense, but it does not persist as the kind of system it claims to be. It loses the properties that define its structure. Persistence, in the relevant sense, is therefore identical with the preservation of constraint consistency.

7.4 Synthesis

The consequences derived in this section follow directly from the equivalence established in Section 6 between pluralist invariants and constraint closure. If legitimacy is identical with structural admissibility, then it cannot depend on agreement or procedure. If closure replaces authority, then binding force arises from consistency rather than endorsement. If domination undermines viable exit, then it undermines stability itself.

Taken together, these results transform the problem of legitimacy. It is no longer a question of how agreement can be achieved or how authority can be justified. It is a question of whether the structural conditions required for consistent composition are satisfied.

Within this framework, legitimacy is neither constructed nor imposed. It is the condition that remains when all inconsistent configurations—those that eliminate viable exit, disrupt symmetry, or prevent repair—are excluded.

An institution can be modeled as a constraint system that defines the admissibility of actions and relations. The functioning of the institution depends on whether this constraint system is complete and closed under composition.

Thus, institutional stability depends not only on the existence of constraints, but on the system’s ability to maintain closure in the face of evolving interactions. This requires both sufficiently complete constraints and sufficiently rapid mechanisms of repair.

8.3 Communication and Systemic Coordination

The framework also applies to communication systems, understood as structures that map expressions to interpretations across agents. A communication system can be modeled as a set of transformations that relate signals to meanings. For such a system to function, it must satisfy a condition analogous to compositional consistency: identical signals must produce compatible interpretations across agents, or at least interpretations that can be reconciled through admissible transformations.

From Section 3, each agent operates with an internally consistent perceptual framework. From Section 4, these frameworks must be treated as structurally symmetrical. Communication therefore requires that there exist mappings between these frameworks that preserve consistency.

Constraint failure in communication occurs when such mappings break down. That is, when a signal produces interpretations and for agents and such that no admissible sequence of transformations can reconcile them. In such cases, the system fails to compose. Communication does not merely produce disagreement; it produces structural incompatibility.

This can be understood in terms of viable exit. If an agent cannot reinterpret or respond to a signal in a way that preserves its capacity to participate in further communication, then the signal eliminates viable exit within the communicative domain. Thus, communication failure is a specific instance of domination: the reduction of viable interpretive paths available to agents.

An objection might be raised that communication frequently involves disagreement without collapse. The response, consistent with earlier sections, is that disagreement is compatible with consistency so long as the system admits paths through which interpretations can be related without contradiction. It is only when such paths are eliminated that communication fails structurally.

9. Objections and Clarifications

9.1 Objection: The Argument Illicitly Derives Normativity from Structural Constraints

A serious objection to the preceding argument is that it appears to derive normative conclusions—specifically, claims about legitimacy and non-domination—from what are presented as purely structural conditions. The objection can be stated as follows.

The analysis begins by describing conditions under which systems remain consistent under composition. From this, it proceeds to claims about legitimacy, autonomy, and domination. However, consistency is a formal property, while legitimacy is a normative one. To move from one to the other appears to commit a category error, or at minimum to rely on an implicit normative premise that has not been made explicit.

More sharply: even if it is granted that systems must be consistent in order to function, it does not follow that consistency confers legitimacy. A system might be perfectly consistent and yet unjust. Therefore, the identification of legitimacy with structural consistency appears unwarranted.

Response

The objection rests on an equivocation concerning the role of legitimacy in the present argument. The argument does not claim that consistency is sufficient for all forms of normative evaluation. It does not claim that consistency guarantees justice, fairness, or moral desirability. What it claims is more limited and more precise: that consistency is a necessary condition for any structure to generate binding outcomes at all.

To clarify this, it is necessary to distinguish between two questions:

Under what conditions can a system produce determinate, binding outcomes?

Among such outcomes, which are morally preferable?

The present argument addresses only the first question. Legitimacy, as defined in this paper, is not a comprehensive moral evaluation. It is the condition under which outcomes can function as binding within a plural system. Bindingness requires determinacy, and determinacy requires consistency under composition.

If a system is inconsistent, then any outcome it produces can be contradicted by an alternative admissible sequence. In such a case, no outcome can be binding, because no outcome is uniquely determined. Thus, consistency does not make outcomes good. It makes them possible as outcomes.

The objection therefore fails because it attributes to the argument a stronger claim than it makes. The argument does not derive normativity from structure; it identifies the structural conditions under which normative claims can be meaningfully applied.

9.2 Objection: The Framework Presupposes What It Claims to Derive

A second, stronger objection concerns circularity. The argument claims to derive conditions such as inclusion, symmetry, and repair from the requirement of compositional consistency. However, it may be argued that these conditions are already implicitly normative, and that the argument merely redescribes them in formal language.

In particular, the requirement that agents not be excluded, that equality be preserved, and that asymmetries be corrected may appear to be moral commitments smuggled into the structure under the guise of necessity.

If this is correct, then the argument does not derive these invariants from first principles, but assumes them.

Response

This objection can only succeed if it can be shown that the invariants are independent of the requirement of consistency. That is, it must be possible to construct a system that:

is consistent under composition

yet violates inclusion, symmetry, or repair

If such a system exists, then the invariants are not necessary, and the argument fails. The analysis of Sections 3–6 shows that this is not possible. Exclusion violates inclusion by removing agents from the domain of admissible relations. This produces a system that cannot define compositions involving those agents. The system is therefore not closed, and thus not consistent.

Persistent asymmetry violates symmetry by allowing identical initial conditions to produce divergent outcomes. This introduces path dependence, which is incompatible with compositional consistency. Failure of repair violates closure over time by allowing deviations to accumulate. This produces eventual inconsistency even if the system appears locally stable. In each case, the invariant is not introduced as a normative commitment but derived as a condition required to prevent a specific form of inconsistency. Thus, the invariants are not presupposed; they are forced by the requirement of compositional consistency.

9.3 Objection: The Definition of Domination Is Too Strong

A third objection targets the definition of domination as the reduction of viable exit options. The objection may be stated as follows. Many real-world systems restrict the options available to agents without being regarded as dominating. Laws constrain behavior, contracts limit choices, and social norms impose expectations. If any reduction in viable exit is classified as domination, then the concept becomes too broad to be useful.

More sharply: the definition appears to conflate constraint with domination, thereby collapsing all structured systems into forms of domination.

Response

The objection rests on a failure to distinguish between constraint as such and constraint that eliminates viability. The framework does not claim that all constraints are dominating. On the contrary, constraints are necessary for any system to function. Without constraints, no determinate relations can exist.

The relevant distinction is between:

constraints that preserve at least one viable exit

constraints that eliminate all viable exits

The former define structure; the latter destroy capacity. To make this explicit, recall that capacity was defined as the existence of at least one viable exit:

A constraint reduces capacity only if it reduces . It constitutes domination only if it reduces to the empty set, or in a direction that cannot be reversed through admissible repair. Thus, ordinary constraints—laws, rules, norms—do not constitute domination so long as they preserve at least one viable path of participation for each agent.

The definition of domination is therefore not too broad, but precisely calibrated. It identifies the point at which constraint ceases to structure action and instead eliminates the possibility of action altogether.

9.4 Objection: The Framework Eliminates Genuine Disagreement

A fourth objection concerns the role of disagreement. If legitimacy is defined in terms of structural admissibility, and if admissibility is determined by constraint satisfaction, then it may appear that disagreement is rendered irrelevant. Either a configuration satisfies the constraints, or it does not. There is no room for substantive conflict. This suggests that the framework either trivializes disagreement or assumes it away.

Response

This objection misunderstands the relationship between disagreement and consistency. Disagreement concerns the content of agents’ beliefs, preferences, and evaluations. Consistency concerns the structure of relations among those beliefs and actions.

The framework does not eliminate disagreement; it distinguishes between disagreement that is structurally admissible and disagreement that produces inconsistency. Agents may disagree about values, goals, or interpretations while still participating in a system that preserves viable exit, symmetry, and repair. Such disagreement is fully compatible with compositional consistency.

What is excluded is not disagreement as such, but disagreement that destroys the conditions under which participation can continue. That is, disagreement that eliminates viable exit or introduces irreconcilable contradictions. Thus:

Disagreement is not eliminated; it is bounded.

9.5 Objection: Closure Is Too Strong a Requirement for Real Systems

A final objection concerns the requirement of closure. The argument maintains that a system is valid if and only if it is closed under its constraints. However, real-world systems are often incomplete, evolving, and subject to error. They frequently operate without full closure. It may therefore be argued that the requirement of closure is too strong to apply to actual systems, and that the framework is therefore idealized to the point of impracticality.

Response

This objection again turns on a distinction between structural validity and empirical approximation. Closure is not introduced as an empirical claim about how systems always operate. It is introduced as a condition that must be satisfied for a system to produce determinate outcomes.

A system may operate without full closure, but in doing so, it sacrifices determinacy. It produces outcomes that are unstable, revisable, or contradictory. Such systems function only to the extent that they approximate closure. Thus, closure should not be understood as a description of all existing systems, but as a standard against which systems can be evaluated. The framework therefore does not deny that real systems are imperfect. It provides a means of identifying the ways in which they fail, and the conditions under which those failures become structurally significant.

9.6 Synthesis

The objections considered here target the strongest points of the argument: the transition from structure to legitimacy, the derivation of invariants, the definition of domination, the role of disagreement, and the requirement of closure. In each case, the objection succeeds only if it can show that the argument relies on unexamined assumptions or collapses important distinctions.

The responses show that this is not the case. Each step of the argument follows from the requirement that systems produce determinate outcomes under conditions of pluralism. The invariants are not introduced arbitrarily but derived as conditions necessary to avoid inconsistency. Domination is defined not as constraint, but as the elimination of viability. Disagreement is preserved within the bounds of consistency. Closure is required not as an ideal, but as the condition of validity. Taken together, these clarifications reinforce the central claim: that legitimacy is not a product of agreement or authority, but the structural condition under which a system can sustain coherent interaction among plural agents.

10. Conclusion

10.1 Final Statement

The argument developed in this paper began from a simple but inescapable condition: pluralist systems lack a shared final authority. From this starting point, it followed that no account of legitimacy grounded in agreement, procedure, or normative convergence could be structurally sufficient, since each of these presupposes precisely the condition that pluralism denies.

The analysis therefore proceeded by shifting the question. Rather than asking what agents must agree upon, it asked what must be true of any system in which agents can interact in a way that produces determinate, binding outcomes.

This shift required that the problem be restated in structural terms. Standing was defined as the condition under which an agent’s participation has consequence; capacity as the condition under which such participation can be sustained; and personal warrant as the internal consistency required for participation to be determinate. From these, the invariants of inclusion, symmetry, and directed repair were derived as necessary conditions for the preservation of compositional consistency.

The transition to constraint systems made explicit what was already implicit in these derivations: that any system capable of producing determinate outcomes must be understood as a system of constraints, and that validity consists in closure under those constraints.

The final step was to show that the preservation of viable exit—defined as the existence of at least one path through which an agent’s capacity for participation is maintained—is equivalent to the preservation of closure. From this, domination was identified not as asymmetry in outcomes, but as the reduction of viable exit, and non-domination as the preservation of such exit under composition. Taken together, these results establish the central claim:

This statement does not introduce a new principle. It expresses, in its most compressed form, the conclusion that has been derived at every stage of the argument. A system is legitimate if and only if it preserves the conditions under which its operations remain consistent, its participants remain capable of participation, and its outcomes remain determinate.

10.2 Contribution

The contribution of this paper is not to introduce a new normative theory, but to clarify the structural conditions under which any such theory must operate. First, it establishes that the problem of pluralist legitimacy is not fundamentally a problem of agreement, but of structure. The inability of existing theories to resolve this problem is not due to insufficient moral reasoning, but to the absence of an explicit account of the conditions required for compositional consistency.

Second, it provides such an account by identifying the minimal invariants—Inclusion, Symmetry, and Directed Repair—and demonstrating that they are not independent normative commitments, but necessary conditions for the existence of a constraint-closed system.

Third, it introduces a constraint algebra, , in which these invariants can be expressed and analyzed formally. This algebra does not operate by selecting among possible outcomes, but by determining which configurations are admissible at all. In doing so, it reframes the question of legitimacy from one of selection to one of admissibility.

Finally, it provides a precise structural definition of domination as the reduction of viable exit, thereby connecting the abstract conditions of constraint closure to concrete conditions of autonomy and capacity. These contributions together establish a unified framework in which philosophical claims about legitimacy can be directly mapped to formal properties of constraint systems.

10.3 Future Work

The framework developed here admits both formal and applied extensions. On the formal side, the constraint algebra can be further developed to incorporate additional invariants, to characterize different classes of constraint systems, and to establish more precise conditions under which closure is preserved or violated. In particular, the relationship between local constraint satisfaction and global closure invites further analysis, as does the role of temporal dynamics in systems subject to ongoing repair.

On the applied side, the framework can be extended to domains beyond those considered here, including economic systems, computational systems, and large-scale sociotechnical networks. In each case, the central question remains the same: whether the system preserves viable exit under composition, and thus whether it maintains the conditions required for structural legitimacy.

10.4 Final Core Claim

The argument of this paper can be reduced to a single claim, which should now be understood not as a thesis to be defended, but as the result of the preceding derivation:

This claim has a precise meaning. To say that legitimacy is grounded in structural consistency is to say that a system is legitimate if and only if it preserves the conditions under which its operations remain well-defined, its participants remain capable of participation, and its outcomes remain invariant under admissible compositions.

Any system that fails to satisfy these conditions cannot produce binding outcomes, regardless of the degree of agreement it achieves or the procedures it employs. Conversely, any system that satisfies these conditions produces binding outcomes independently of agreement.

Thus, legitimacy is neither constructed through consensus nor conferred by authority. It is the structural condition that remains when all configurations that fail to compose consistently—those that eliminate viable exit, disrupt symmetry, or prevent repair—are excluded.
