Abstract

This paper develops a structural analysis of computation in NP and establishes that reduction–separation arises as a necessary consequence of the verifier’s internal structure. Starting from the primitive formulation of satisfiability in terms of assignment space and constraint evaluation, we formalize representations as partial encodings of constraint systems and define compression as the removal of structural information. A retrieval operator is introduced to model reconstruction, leading to the notion of a structural residual.

By analyzing the effect of removing individual components, we identify the set of decision-critical relations—those whose absence changes the value of the decision functional—and show that these relations form a closure under interaction. This closure constitutes the minimal structure that must be preserved or reconstructed by any correct system.

We then examine reductions as structure-preserving transformations and formalize their action via a mapping between constraint systems that preserves both satisfaction and distinguishing behavior. From the interaction of combinatorial growth, irreducible constraint structure, and preservation under reduction, we derive a reduction–separation theorem: for any pair of distinct witnesses, there exists a reduction under which their propagated forms are distinguishable.

A complete failure mode analysis characterizes the exact structural conditions under which this result would not hold, showing that such failures correspond to degenerate verification systems. The analysis demonstrates that separation emerges from the intrinsic structure of NP verification rather than from external assumptions.

1. Introduction

This paper develops a structural analysis of computation in NP and establishes that, under the native definition of verification and reduction, distinct witnesses cannot remain indistinguishable under all valid reductions. Equivalently, reduction–separation emerges as a necessary consequence of the structure of NP verification.

The argument proceeds from first principles. We begin with the assignment space, the verifier, and the induced constraint system, and introduce representations as partial encodings of that system. Compression is defined as the removal of structural information, and a retrieval operator is introduced to model reconstruction. This leads to the notion of a structural residual and to the identification of decision-critical relations—those whose removal changes the value of the decision functional.

We then show that these critical relations are not independent but form a closure under interaction. This closure constitutes the minimal structure that must be preserved or reconstructed by any correct system. The analysis is extended to reductions, which are shown to act as structure-preserving transformations. In particular, valid reductions must induce a mapping between constraint systems that preserves both satisfaction and distinguishing behavior.

From this, the argument proceeds in three steps. First, combinatorial growth implies that witnesses cannot collapse into a small number of equivalence classes. Second, the constraint system embeds all coordinate-level differences into an irreducibly interacting structure. Third, reduction-induced mappings preserve these differences at the level of constraint evaluation. Together, these imply that any structural difference between witnesses must be reflected in the verifier under some reduction.

The result is a reduction–separation theorem: for any pair of distinct witnesses, there exists a reduction under which their propagated forms are distinguishable. A complete failure mode analysis identifies the exact conditions under which this conclusion would not hold, showing that such failures correspond to degenerate cases of verification rather than to the standard NP setting.

The remainder of the paper develops this framework in detail. Section 2 introduces relational representation, compression, and structural residuals. Section 3 establishes closure and dependency of critical relations. Section 4 formalizes retrieval and constraint preservation. Subsequent sections derive the structural theorem and its consequences.

The analysis reframes the problem: rather than asking whether efficient algorithms exist, it identifies the structural information that cannot be removed from NP computation. Under this view, separation is not imposed externally but arises from the internal constraints of the system itself.

26. Derivation of ΣR and Interoperability Constraint Geometry from NP Witness Structure and the ΣR–ICG Structural Dichotomy Theorem

26.1 Objective

We show that the structures identified as ΣR (replicability algebra) and Interoperability Constraint Geometry (ICG) arise necessarily from the intrinsic structure of NP verification and reduction closure.

We then formalize and prove the ΣR–ICG Structural Dichotomy Theorem, establishing that any system satisfying these induced structures must exhibit either global compression or irreducible obstruction.

The derivation and theorem rely only on:

(1) NP verification structure [11],
(2) polynomial-time reduction closure,
(3) compositional consistency.

No external framework assumptions are introduced.

26.2 NP Witness System as a Relational Structure

Let L ⊆ {0,1}* be NP-complete.

Then there exists a polynomial p : ℕ → ℕ and a deterministic verifier V such that:

x ∈ L ⟺ ∃ w ∈ {0,1}^(≤ p(|x|))

such that

V(x, w) = 1

This statement is the formal definition of NP and serves as the starting point for the entire construction. It asserts that membership in L is not decided directly, but rather through the existence of a certificate w whose length is bounded by a polynomial in the size of x. The verifier V is deterministic, meaning that for any fixed pair (x, w), the outcome V(x, w) is uniquely determined.

The equivalence is critical: the forward direction guarantees completeness (every valid input has a witness), while the reverse direction guarantees soundness (only valid inputs have witnesses). Together, they ensure that the witness structure faithfully represents the language L.

Define:

W(x) := { w ∈ {0,1}^(≤ p(|x|)) : V(x, w) = 1 }

This definition collects all valid witnesses for a given input x into a single set. It converts the existential condition “there exists w” into a concrete object that can be analyzed structurally.

From the definition above, we immediately obtain:

x ∈ L ⟺ W(x) ≠ ∅

This follows directly from the definition of W(x): if x ∈ L, then at least one witness exists, so W(x) is nonempty; conversely, if W(x) is nonempty, then there exists a witness w such that V(x, w) = 1, which implies x ∈ L.

Thus, the decision problem for L can be reformulated as a structural property of W(x): determining membership is equivalent to checking whether W(x) is empty or not.

We now introduce a global structure that captures all witness relationships simultaneously.

Define global witness space:

𝒲 := ⋃_{x ∈ {0,1}*} ( {x} × W(x) )

This construction forms a set of ordered pairs (x, w), where each pair represents a valid certification. In other words, 𝒲 encodes the entire witness relation across all inputs.

This can be interpreted as a fibered structure: for each input x, the corresponding fiber is W(x). Thus 𝒲 is naturally decomposed into disjoint slices indexed by x.

Because witnesses are bounded in length by p(|x|), we have the cardinality bound:

|W(x)| ≤ 2^(p(|x|))

This follows from a basic counting argument: the number of binary strings of length at most p(|x|) is at most 2^(p(|x|)). Since W(x) is a subset of this set, its size cannot exceed this bound.

This inequality is fundamental for later arguments. It establishes that the witness space grows at most exponentially in the size of the input, which will later be compared against the number of equivalence classes.

Let R denote the set of all polynomial-time reductions [1], [2], [4].

Formally:

R = { f : {0,1}* → {0,1}* | for all x, x ∈ L ⇒ f(x) ∈ L }

This definition captures the notion of reducibility: each function f in R transforms instances of the problem into other instances while preserving membership in L.

An important structural property of R is closure under composition. If R₁, R₂ ∈ R, then:

R₂ ∘ R₁ ∈ R

This follows directly from the definition. If x ∈ L, then R₁(x) ∈ L, and applying R₂ gives R₂(R₁(x)) ∈ L.

Thus composition preserves the defining property of reductions.

This closure property is essential because it ensures that arbitrary sequences of reductions remain valid transformations. Later, this will force consistency conditions on how witnesses propagate.

Then the NP system induces the relational structure [8], [10]:

S = (X, 𝒲, V, R)

where:

X = {0,1}* is the input space,

𝒲 is the global witness space,

V is the verifier relation,

R is the set of reductions.

This tuple S encapsulates all relevant structure of the NP system.

The key point is that S is not an arbitrary construction. Each component is forced by the definition of NP:

X is the domain of inputs,

𝒲 arises from the existential witness condition,

V encodes verification,

R encodes allowable transformations.

Thus S provides a complete relational model of the NP witness system.

At an intuitive level, what we have done in this section is replace a logical definition (“there exists a witness”) with a fully explicit mathematical object (the set 𝒲 together with V and R). This allows us to reason about witnesses not just individually, but as elements of a structured system that can be analyzed under transformation and composition.

This shift from existential logic to structural representation is what makes the subsequent derivation of ΣR and ICG possible.

26.3 Necessity of Reconstruction Structure

Correctness of decision requires:

x ∈ L ⟹ ∃ w ∈ W(x)

This statement follows directly from the definition of NP established in Section 26.2. It asserts that for every input x that belongs to the language L, there exists at least one witness w such that V(x, w) = 1. This is an existential statement: it guarantees the existence of a valid certificate but does not specify which one.

At this stage, however, we are no longer working purely at the level of logical existence. In order to analyze how witnesses behave under transformations—particularly under the action of reductions—we must be able to refer to witnesses in a consistent and reproducible way. In other words, we must move from a statement of the form “there exists w” to a structure that allows us to track specific witnesses.

Thus, any decision-compatible system must admit a reconstruction relation.

The necessity of such a relation follows from a basic principle in mathematical modeling: if objects are to be propagated, compared, or composed under a family of transformations, then those objects must be representable as elements of a well-defined domain. An existential statement alone is insufficient for this purpose, because it does not provide a stable reference.

To resolve this, we define a (possibly non-efficient) reconstruction operator:

ρ(x) ∈ W(x)

whenever

x ∈ L

This definition assigns to each valid input x a specific witness ρ(x). Importantly, this assignment is not required to be computable in polynomial time, nor even efficiently computable at all. The role of ρ is not algorithmic but structural: it provides a definite representative of the nonempty set W(x).

The existence of such a mapping [17], [18] is justified as follows. For each x ∈ L, the set W(x) is nonempty and finite, since:

W(x) ⊆ {0,1}^(≤ p(|x|))

and therefore:

|W(x)| ≤ 2^(p(|x|))

Thus, for each such finite nonempty set W(x), one may select an element. Defining ρ(x) to be any such selected element yields a well-defined function:

ρ : { x ∈ L } → ⋃ W(x)

This construction does not require any non-constructive or global form of the Axiom of Choice; it is simply the definition of a function by selecting one element from each finite, nonempty set. In standard mathematical practice, such a selection is always permissible when no uniform rule is required.

We emphasize that ρ is not assumed to have any additional properties beyond:

V(x, ρ(x)) = 1

for all

x ∈ L

In particular:

ρ need not be unique,

ρ need not be computable,

ρ need not be canonical.

Its sole purpose is to witness the existential condition in a form that can be propagated through the system.

This establishes the necessity of witness realizability in functional form: once we require consistency under transformations and comparisons across reductions, the existential condition defining NP forces the introduction of a reconstruction operator ρ.

26.4 Reduction-Induced Replicability

Let R : x ↦ x′ be any polynomial-time reduction [1], [2], [4].

Then:

x ∈ L ⟹ x′ ∈ L

This follows directly from the definition of polynomial-time reduction: reductions are required to preserve membership in the language. That is, they are structure-preserving transformations on the instance space.

Thus:

w ∈ W(x) ⟹ ∃ w′ ∈ W(x′)

This implication is obtained by combining two facts established previously. First, if w ∈ W(x), then V(x, w) = 1, which implies x ∈ L. Second, since R preserves membership, x ∈ L implies x′ ∈ L. Finally, by the NP witness condition, x′ ∈ L implies that there exists at least one witness w′ such that V(x′, w′) = 1, i.e., w′ ∈ W(x′).

At this stage, however, the statement remains existential: for each w, there exists some w′, but no specific correspondence is fixed. To analyze propagation under sequences of reductions, we must refine this into a functional relationship.

Define induced mapping [17], [18]:

ρᵣ : W(x) → W(x′)

Expansion — functional realization of ρᵣ:

For each w ∈ W(x), define ρᵣ(w) to be any element of W(x′) whose existence is guaranteed by correctness of the reduction.

Since:

x ∈ L ⟹ x′ ∈ L ⟹ W(x′) ≠ ∅

such a selection is always possible.

This construction is justified in the same manner as in Section 26.3. For each fixed input x′, the set W(x′) is finite and nonempty, since:

W(x′) ⊆ {0,1}^(≤ p(|x′|))

Thus, selecting one element of W(x′) for each w ∈ W(x) defines a function. No global form of the Axiom of Choice is required; we are simply defining a function by assigning to each element of a domain an element from a corresponding nonempty finite set.

Thus, ρᵣ is a well-defined total function on W(x).

Importantly, ρᵣ is not assumed to be unique or canonical. Different choices of representatives may yield different functions, but any such function satisfies the only required property:

for all w ∈ W(x), V(x′, ρᵣ(w)) = 1

This is sufficient for all subsequent arguments, since only validity and compositional consistency are required.

For any composition R₂ ∘ R₁:

ρ_{R₂ ∘ R₁} = ρ_{R₂} ∘ ρ_{R₁}

Expansion — definitional composition:

Let:

R₁ : x ↦ x′, R₂ : x′ ↦ x″

For any w ∈ W(x), define:

ρ_{R₁}(w) = w′ ∈ W(x′), ρ_{R₂}(w′) = w″ ∈ W(x″)

Since:

x ∈ L ⟹ x′ ∈ L ⟹ x″ ∈ L

such w″ exists.

Define:

ρ_{R₂ ∘ R₁}(w) := ρ_{R₂}(ρ_{R₁}(w))

Thus:

ρ_{R₂ ∘ R₁} = ρ_{R₂} ∘ ρ_{R₁}

by construction.

This step requires careful justification. At first glance, one might think this is merely a definition, but in fact it is forced by consistency requirements.

Suppose that ρ_{R₂ ∘ R₁} were defined independently of ρ_{R₁} and ρ_{R₂}. Then for a given w, there would be two possible propagation paths:

direct: ρ_{R₂ ∘ R₁}(w)

sequential: ρ_{R₂}(ρ_{R₁}(w))

If these differed, then the system would assign two distinct witnesses in W(x″) to the same initial witness w under the same overall transformation R₂ ∘ R₁. This would violate the principle that witness propagation should be well-defined with respect to transformations of the input.

Thus, requiring:

ρ_{R₂ ∘ R₁}(w) = ρ_{R₂}(ρ_{R₁}(w))

is not an arbitrary choice but a necessary condition for compositional consistency.

This is analogous to the requirement in algebra that a representation of a semigroup or category respect composition: the image of a composite must equal the composite of the images.

Therefore, the compositional law:

ρ_{R₂ ∘ R₁} = ρ_{R₂} ∘ ρ_{R₁}

is forced by the structure of reductions and the requirement that witness propagation be coherent across all reduction sequences.

26.5 Formation of ΣR Algebra

Define the system:

ΣR := (𝒲, {ρᵣ} (R ∈ ℛ), ∘)

with:

(1) Closure: ρ_{R₂ ∘ R₁} = ρ_{R₂} ∘ ρ_{R₁}
(2) Existence: ρᵣ(w) exists whenever V(x, w) = 1
(3) Validity preservation: V(R(x), ρᵣ(w)) = 1

Thus, NP witness structure induces a compositional reconstruction algebra.
This is precisely ΣR.

We now justify why this structure is not merely definitional but is forced by the properties established in Sections 26.3 and 26.4.

First, recall that for every reduction R ∈ ℛ, we constructed a function:

ρᵣ : W(x) → W(R(x))

such that for every witness w ∈ W(x), the propagated witness ρᵣ(w) lies in W(R(x)). This establishes that witness propagation is total on W(x) and always produces a valid witness for the transformed instance.

This immediately yields property (2):

ρᵣ(w) exists whenever V(x, w) = 1

since V(x, w) = 1 is equivalent to w ∈ W(x). Thus existence of ρᵣ is not an additional assumption but follows directly from the reconstruction and reduction-induced propagation developed earlier.

Next, we consider property (3), validity preservation.

From the definition of ρᵣ, we have:

ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(R(x))

which by definition of W implies:

V(R(x), ρᵣ(w)) = 1

Thus, validity preservation is simply a restatement of the fact that ρᵣ maps witnesses to witnesses. This property is essential: it ensures that propagation under reductions never leaves the space of admissible configurations.

The most structurally significant property is (1), closure under composition:

ρ_{R₂ ∘ R₁} = ρ_{R₂} ∘ ρ_{R₁}

As established in Section 26.4, this equality is not an arbitrary definition but is required for consistency. If propagation under a composite reduction differed from sequential propagation, then the system would assign multiple possible outcomes to the same transformation. This would violate determinacy and make the notion of propagation ill-defined.

Thus closure under composition is forced by the requirement that witness propagation respect the compositional structure of reductions.

We now observe that these three properties together define an algebraic structure.

The set ℛ of reductions is closed under composition.

The family of maps {ρᵣ} assigns to each reduction a transformation on witness space.

The composition law ensures that this assignment respects the structure of ℛ.

In algebraic terms, this means that the mapping [17], [18]:

R ↦ ρᵣ

is a homomorphism [17], [18] from the semigroup of reductions (ℛ, ∘) into the semigroup of functions on 𝒲 under composition.

Thus ΣR is not merely a collection of functions, but a compositional system in which:

elements (reductions) act on witnesses,

composition of reductions corresponds to composition of actions.

This is precisely the defining feature of an algebraic action.

Therefore, ΣR is properly understood as a compositional reconstruction algebra, in which:

witnesses are the underlying objects,

reductions act as transformations,

and the action respects composition and preserves validity.

This structure is not introduced externally. It is the inevitable result of requiring:

existence of witnesses (Section 26.3),

consistent propagation under reductions (Section 26.4), and

closure under composition of reductions.

Thus, the NP witness system induces ΣR as a necessary algebraic structure governing how information propagates through the system.

This is precisely ΣR.

26.6 Constraint Interaction Structure

Each witness w ∈ W(x) satisfies a collection of constraints C(x).

More precisely, by definition of the verifier V, there exists a finite set of constraints C(x) = {c₁, c₂, …, c_k} such that:

w ∈ W(x) ⟺ for all c ∈ C(x), c(w) = 1

Equivalently:

V(x, w) = 1 ⟺ ∧_{c ∈ C(x)} c(w) = 1

This formulation makes explicit that the verifier does not check a single condition, but rather a conjunction of conditions that must all be satisfied simultaneously. Each constraint c ∈ C(x) may be viewed as a predicate on the witness space, and validity requires that all such predicates evaluate to true.

Constraint satisfaction [8], [9] is not independent; interactions are required for validity.

This follows from the logical structure of conjunction. In general, even if individual constraints are well-defined, their joint satisfiability is not determined by considering each constraint in isolation. That is, the condition:

c₁(w) = 1, c₂(w) = 1, …, c_k(w) = 1

cannot be decomposed into independent subproblems unless the constraints are mutually independent, which is not guaranteed in general NP verification systems.

Indeed, constraints may share variables, impose overlapping conditions, or introduce dependencies such that satisfying one constraint restricts the possible ways of satisfying another. Therefore, the admissible witness set W(x) is determined by the intersection of constraint-satisfying sets:

W(x) = ⋂_{c ∈ C(x)} { w : c(w) = 1 }

Thus, W(x) is not merely a collection of independently valid assignments, but the result of a global compatibility condition across all constraints.

Under reduction:

constraints are transformed,
  dependencies are preserved or reconfigured.

To make this precise, consider a reduction R : x ↦ x′. The verifier for x′ induces a new set of constraints C(x′). For any witness w ∈ W(x), its propagated image ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(x′) must satisfy

for all c′ ∈ C(x′),

c′(ρᵣ(w)) = 1

This implies that reduction induces a transformation at the level of constraints:

R : C(x) → C(x′)

in the sense that the admissibility conditions on witnesses are carried forward through the mapping ρᵣ. [17], [18]

However, this transformation does not preserve constraints independently. Rather, it preserves the joint structure of constraints. That is, the relationships between constraints—such as shared variables, mutual restrictions, and logical dependencies—must be maintained in order for validity to be preserved.

If constraint interactions were not preserved, then it would be possible for a propagated witness ρᵣ(w) to satisfy each transformed constraint individually while failing to satisfy them jointly, contradicting:

ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(x′)

Thus, reductions must preserve not only the set of constraints, but also their interaction structure.

Thus, validity depends on joint constraint interaction.

This statement now follows as a structural necessity. Since:

V(x, w) = 1 ⟺ w satisfies all constraints in C(x),

and since reductions must map valid witnesses to valid witnesses:

w ∈ W(x) ⟹ ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(x′),

it follows that the entire system of constraints, including their dependencies, must be preserved under propagation.

Therefore, the admissible witness space is not determined by individual constraints in isolation, but by the geometry of their interaction. That is, W(x) is defined by a structured intersection of constraint sets, and this structure is invariant (up to transformation) under all reductions.

This observation is what motivates the transition to Interoperability Constraint Geometry (ICG) in the next section: once constraints interact nontrivially and must remain consistent under all transformations, the space of admissible configurations acquires a geometric character defined by these interactions.

26.7 Necessity of Closure Envelope

For consistency:

w ∈ W(x) ⟹ ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(R(x))

This condition follows directly from the definition of ρᵣ in Section 26.4. By construction, ρᵣ maps each valid witness w ∈ W(x) to a witness in W(R(x)), ensuring that validity is preserved under every reduction. Thus, any admissible witness must remain admissible after propagation.

However, this immediately imposes a stronger structural requirement. If witness propagation is to be well-defined across all reductions, then the collection of admissible witnesses cannot be considered separately for each input x. Instead, it must be embedded in a larger set that is stable under all maps ρᵣ.

Thus, admissible witnesses must lie in a set invariant under all ρᵣ.

To see why this is necessary, suppose no such invariant set existed. Then there would exist some witness w and reduction R such that:

w ∈ admissible set but ρᵣ(w) ∉ admissible set

But this contradicts the defining property:

ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(R(x))

and hence:

V(R(x), ρᵣ(w)) = 1

Thus, admissibility would fail to be preserved under reductions, violating the consistency conditions established earlier. Therefore, closure under all ρᵣ is not optional—it is required.

Define envelope E ⊆ 𝒲 such that:

(1) W(x) ⊆ E for all x,
(2) ρᵣ(E) ⊆ E,
(3) all constraint interactions are represented within E.

We now justify each of these conditions.

Condition (1):

W(x) ⊆ E for all x

ensures that E contains all valid witnesses. This is necessary because E is intended to represent the full space of admissible configurations. Omitting any element of W(x) would exclude a valid witness, contradicting the completeness of the representation.

Condition (2):

ρᵣ(E) ⊆ E

expresses invariance under all reduction-induced mappings [17], [18]. This is a standard closure condition in algebra and dynamical systems: if a set is to represent all admissible states of a system, it must be closed under the transformations that define the system’s evolution.

Here, the transformations are the maps ρᵣ. Since every valid witness must propagate to another valid witness, E must be closed under all such maps. Otherwise, applying a valid transformation would leave the space E, contradicting its intended role.

Condition (3):

all constraint interactions are represented within E

follows from Section 26.6. There, we showed that validity is determined not by individual constraints, but by their joint interaction:

w ∈ W(x) ⟺ ∀ c ∈ C(x), c(w) = 1

and:

W(x) = ⋂_{c ∈ C(x)} { w : c(w) = 1 }

Thus, admissibility depends on the intersection structure of constraint-satisfying sets. If E failed to represent all such interactions, then it would not capture the full structure of validity.

In particular, if E omitted some combination of constraints that is realizable under the verifier, then E would fail to represent the true admissible space. Therefore, E must include all configurations consistent with constraint interaction.

Taken together, these three conditions define E as a set that is:

complete (contains all valid witnesses),

invariant (closed under all ρᵣ),

structurally faithful (captures all constraint interactions).

We emphasize that E is not introduced arbitrarily. Rather, it is forced by the combined requirements of:

witness propagation under ΣR,

constraint interaction structure from Section 26.6,

and consistency of validity under reductions.

Without such a set, the system would not admit a stable domain in which all admissible configurations evolve coherently. Thus, the existence of a closure envelope E is a necessary structural consequence of the NP witness system under ΣR.

26.8 Formation of Interoperability Constraint Geometry

Define:

ICG := minimal set E satisfying:
  (1) invariance under all ρᵣ,
  (2) closure under constraint interaction,
  (3) completeness for witness validity.

Thus, ICG is the necessary geometric closure of admissible configurations.

From Section 26.7, we established that there must exist a set E that contains all admissible witnesses, is closed under all propagation maps ρᵣ, and fully represents constraint interaction. However, such sets are not unique: any superset of a valid envelope remains closed under propagation but may include elements not forced by the system.

To eliminate this ambiguity, we define ICG as the minimal such set:

ICG = ⋂ { E : E satisfies (1), (2), (3) }

This ensures that ICG contains exactly those configurations required by the system and no extraneous elements.

We now justify why this structure is properly understood as a geometry.

From Section 26.6:

W(x) = ⋂_{c ∈ C(x)} { w : c(w) = 1 }

Thus, admissible witnesses arise as intersections of constraint-defined subsets. Each constraint c defines a subset of the ambient witness space, and the admissible region is the intersection of all such constraint sets.

This induces a structured configuration space in which:

constraints define admissible regions,

their intersections define feasible configurations,

and their joint structure determines the shape of admissibility.

A crucial distinction must now be made:

The constraints determine the configuration (shape) of admissible regions, while the ambient state space determines their location.

That is:

ICG defines what shape the admissible region takes,

the embedding into the state space determines where that shape resides.

Thus, ICG is best understood as a configuration geometry, independent of specific placement within the global state space.

To make this precise, we observe that the witness space:

{0,1}ⁿ ⊂ ℝⁿ

admits a canonical embedding into Euclidean space.

Under this embedding:

each witness w becomes a point in ℝⁿ,

each constraint c defines a subset of ℝⁿ, typically as a level set or predicate-defined region,

and ICG becomes the intersection of these constraint-defined regions.

Thus, ICG is realized as a subset of ℝⁿ defined by simultaneous constraint satisfaction [8], [9].

This embedding allows the structure of ICG to inherit geometric properties by construction.

In particular:

The boundary of ICG consists of points where one or more constraints become tight.

The local structure is determined by how constraint satisfaction changes under perturbation.

The interaction of constraints determines the dimensionality and structure of admissible regions.

Most importantly, curves and higher-order geometric structures arise naturally from constraint interaction.

When two constraints become simultaneously active:

cᵢ(w) = 0
  cⱼ(w) = 0

their intersection defines a lower-dimensional structure.

If the gradients ∇cᵢ and ∇cⱼ are linearly independent at a point, then by the implicit function principle, the solution set locally defines a one-dimensional manifold — that is, a curve within ℝⁿ.

Thus, curves are not introduced externally; they arise as the intersection of constraint hypersurfaces.

More generally:

intersections of k independent constraints produce (n − k)-dimensional structures,

tangent directions are given by the null space of constraint gradients,

local variation is governed by the gradients ∇c,

curvature is determined by second-order structure (Hessian matrices).

This establishes that ICG supports intrinsic geometric diagnostics:

smoothness arises where constraint functions vary regularly,

texture or roughness arises where constraint interactions introduce discontinuities or combinatorial boundaries,

gradient structure is induced by directional sensitivity of constraint satisfaction,

curvature emerges from second-order interaction of constraints.

These properties are not assumed; they are inherited from the constraint functions themselves once interpreted in the ambient space.

Furthermore, if constraint functions admit continuous or relaxed representations, then the induced geometry inherits standard analytical structure:

differentiability where constraints are smooth,

curvature via second derivatives,

and statistical structure (e.g., Gaussian models) when probabilistic interpretations are applied to the ambient space.

Thus, the ability to perform geometric diagnostics is not an additional assumption, but a consequence of representing constraint-defined admissibility within a standard geometric framework.

Therefore, ICG is not merely a minimal invariant set, but a constraint-induced configuration geometry, whose:

shape is determined by constraint interaction,

invariance is enforced by ΣR,

and geometric structure is inherited through embedding.

This justifies the interpretation of ICG as a geometry capable of supporting analysis of surfaces, curves, gradients, and higher-order structure directly from the defining constraints.

This completes the formation of Interoperability Constraint Geometry.

26.9 Induced Structural System

The NP system induces:

(ΣR, ICG)

where:

ΣR encodes replicability under reduction,
  ICG encodes admissible constraint geometry.

This structure is intrinsic to NP and not externally imposed.

We now make precise the relationship between these two structures and show that they arise from a common source.

From Sections 26.2 through 26.5, we constructed ΣR as the algebra governing how witnesses propagate under reductions. Specifically, ΣR encodes the action:

R ↦ ρᵣ

where each reduction induces a transformation on the witness space, and composition of reductions corresponds to composition of these transformations. Thus, ΣR captures the dynamic aspect of the NP witness system: how admissible configurations evolve under allowable transformations.

From Sections 26.6 through 26.8, we constructed ICG as the minimal invariant closure of admissible configurations under constraint interaction and propagation. ICG captures the static aspect of the same system: the structured space of all configurations that satisfy the constraints and remain consistent under all reductions.

Crucially, both ΣR and ICG arise from the same underlying domain:

S = (X, 𝒲, V, ℛ)

introduced in Section 26.2.

That is:

ΣR is derived from ℛ acting on 𝒲 through the maps ρᵣ,

ICG is derived from V and C(x) acting on 𝒲 through constraint interaction and closure.

Thus, both structures are induced from the same witness space 𝒲 and the same defining relations of the NP system.

They are not independent constructions, but rather two complementary views of a single object:

ΣR describes how elements of 𝒲 transform,

ICG describes how elements of 𝒲 are organized.

This duality can be understood as follows.

Given a witness w ∈ 𝒲:

ΣR determines all possible images of w under reductions,

ICG determines whether w lies in the admissible region defined by constraint interaction.

Thus, admissibility is preserved precisely when:

w ∈ ICG and ρᵣ(w) ∈ ICG for all R

which shows that ΣR acts internally on ICG.

In other words:

ρᵣ(ICG) ⊆ ICG

so ICG is invariant under ΣR.

This establishes that ΣR and ICG together form a single coherent system:

ΣR provides the algebra of transformations,

ICG provides the geometry of admissible configurations,

and the two are linked by invariance and closure.

This is analogous to the standard pairing of algebra and geometry in mathematics, where:

algebra encodes transformations,

geometry encodes structure,

and consistency requires that transformations preserve structure.

Here, that principle is realized concretely:

transformations (ΣR) preserve geometry (ICG)

Most importantly, both structures are forced by the NP definition.

The existence of witnesses and reductions forces ΣR.

The interaction of constraints and closure under propagation forces ICG.

Since both arise from the same domain and from the same defining properties, the pair (ΣR, ICG) is intrinsic to NP.

There is no alternative system satisfying:

witness existence,

reduction closure,

constraint interaction,

and propagation consistency

that does not induce this same paired structure.

Thus, the NP witness system does not merely admit ΣR and ICG; it necessarily induces them as a unified structural system governing both transformation and configuration.

This completes the identification of (ΣR, ICG) as the intrinsic structural system of NP.

26.10 ΣR–ICG Structural Dichotomy Theorem

We now establish the central structural consequence of the framework developed in the preceding sections. At this stage, we have shown that any NP witness system, when required to behave consistently under reduction and verification, necessarily induces a compositional propagation structure ΣR and an invariant closure of admissible configurations ICG.

The purpose of this theorem is to determine what global behaviors are compatible with these constraints. The key idea is that once witnesses must propagate consistently under all reductions and remain within a closed admissible space, the system loses freedom: it must either collapse into a small number of equivalence classes or maintain full distinguishability across reductions.

To formalize this, we introduce a notion of indistinguishability.

Let w₁, w₂ ∈ W(x). Define:

w₁ ∼ w₂ ⟺ for all R, V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

This definition states that two witnesses are equivalent if no reduction can distinguish them after propagation and verification. This construction follows a standard principle in mathematics: indistinguishability under a family of transformations induces an equivalence relation.

Indeed, ∼ is:

reflexive, since equality holds with itself,

symmetric, since equality is symmetric,

transitive, since equality composes.

Therefore, ∼ partitions W(x) into equivalence classes:

W(x) / ∼

Let:

N(x) = |W(x) / ∼|

denote the number of such classes. The behavior of N(x) determines the global structure of the system.

We now analyze what must happen if compression fails.

26.10.1 Derivation of Reduction–Separation from ΣR–ICG Structure

Assume that global compression does not exist. Thus, there is no function Φ assigning polynomial-length encodings invariant under all reductions. In structural terms, this means that the number of equivalence classes cannot be bounded polynomially.

Step 1 — Non-collapse assumption

We formalize this as:

|W(x) / ∼| ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

for infinitely many x.

This lower bound expresses that the equivalence classes grow at least exponentially. Since the total number of witnesses is bounded by 2^(p(|x|)), this implies that a large proportion of witnesses must occupy distinct equivalence classes. In particular, widespread collapse of witnesses into shared classes is impossible.

Step 2 — Implication of persistent indistinguishability under ΣR

Now suppose, toward understanding when collapse could occur, that:

for all R,

ρᵣ(w₁) = ρᵣ(w₂)

This assumption means that w₁ and w₂ propagate identically under every reduction.

Let R′ be arbitrary. Then:

ρᵣ′(w₁) = ρᵣ′(w₂)

Now consider any further reduction S. Applying S to both sides gives:

ρₛ(ρᵣ′(w₁)) = ρₛ(ρᵣ′(w₂))

By compositional consistency of ΣR, we have:

ρₛ∘ᵣ′(w₁) = ρₛ∘ᵣ′(w₂)

This step relies on the closure property:

ρₛ∘ᵣ′ = ρₛ ∘ ρᵣ′

which was derived earlier from the necessity of consistent propagation.

Because ℛ is closed under composition, every reduction can be represented as such a composition. Therefore, equality propagates across all reductions:

for all R,

ρᵣ(w₁) = ρᵣ(w₂)

Applying the verifier V, which is deterministic, preserves equality:

for all R,

V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

Substituting into the definition of ∼ yields:

w₁ ∼ w₂

Thus, persistent equality under ΣR forces equivalence under ∼.

Step 3 — Necessity of distinguishability

Taking the contrapositive of the previous implication, we obtain:

If w₁ ≁ w₂, then there exists a reduction R such that:

ρᵣ(w₁) ≠ ρᵣ(w₂)

This follows from the logical equivalence:

(A ⇒ B) ⟺ (¬B ⇒ ¬A)

Thus, non-equivalence implies that some reduction separates the witnesses at the level of propagation.

Step 4 — Interaction with ICG

We now incorporate the role of ICG.

Suppose that for all R:

V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

Then by definition:

w₁ ∼ w₂

which contradicts the assumption that w₁ ≁ w₂.

Thus, any structural difference in propagated witnesses must manifest in verifier output. This step depends on ICG, which ensures that validity is determined entirely by constraint interaction within the closed admissible space. Therefore, differences cannot be hidden—they must appear in V.

Step 5 — Conclusion

Combining the previous steps, we obtain:

for all w₁ ≠ w₂, there exists R such that
  V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

This is the reduction–separation property. It states that no two distinct witnesses remain indistinguishable under all reductions.

Completion of the Dichotomy

We now return to the size of the equivalence partition.

If:

N(x) ≤ |x|ᵏ

then we may assign each class a unique binary label of length:

|Φ(x, w)| ≤ log N(x) ≤ k log |x|

This follows from the basic counting principle that log₂(n) bits suffice to encode n distinct objects. Thus, a compression function Φ exists.

If instead:

N(x) ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

then assume, for contradiction, that compression exists. Then the number of encodings is bounded by:

2^{poly(log |x|)} = |x|^{O(1)}

Thus:

2^(c · p(|x|)) ≤ |x|^{O(1)}

Taking logarithms gives:

c · p(|x|) ≤ O(log |x|)

But this contradicts the fact that p(|x|) grows polynomially. Hence compression cannot exist.

Final Statement

Exactly one of the following holds:

(A) Global compression exists via a reduction-invariant encoding Φ of logarithmic size,

or

(B) Reduction–separation holds:

for all w₁ ≠ w₂, there exists R such that
  V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

This dichotomy is forced by the interaction of compositional propagation (ΣR) and invariant closure (ICG), and admits no intermediate regime.

This completes the ΣR–ICG Structural Dichotomy Theorem.

26.11 Proof of Dichotomy

Assume a system satisfying ΣR and ICG.

We begin by recalling the definition of the equivalence relation introduced in Section 26.10. For witnesses w₁, w₂ ∈ W(x), define:

w₁ ∼ w₂ ⟺ for all R, V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

This definition formalizes indistinguishability under all admissible transformations. Because it is defined entirely in terms of equality of verifier outputs, it inherits the fundamental properties of equality. In particular, equality is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, and therefore ∼ is an equivalence relation. By the standard theorem that every equivalence relation induces a partition, it follows that ∼ partitions W(x) into disjoint equivalence classes.

Let:

W(x) / ∼

denote this partition, and let:

|W(x) / ∼|

denote the number of equivalence classes.

The proof proceeds by analyzing the possible growth rates of this quantity.

Case 1: ∼ has finitely many classes (Polynomial Regime)

Assume:

|W(x) / ∼| ≤ |x|ᵏ

for some constant k.

This condition states that the number of distinct equivalence classes grows at most polynomially with the input size. Because equivalence classes partition W(x), each witness belongs to exactly one class, and therefore the total number of distinct “behaviors” under all reductions is bounded by |x|ᵏ.

We now construct a compression function Φ.

Define Φ to map each witness w to a canonical representative of its equivalence class. That is, for each class [w], we assign a unique identifier and define:

Φ(x, w) = index of the equivalence class [w]

Since there are at most |x|ᵏ classes, we can encode each class using a binary string of length:

|Φ(x, w)| ≤ log |W(x) / ∼| ≤ k log |x|

This follows from the standard counting principle that ⌈log₂ n⌉ bits suffice to encode n distinct elements. Thus Φ yields a representation whose length grows logarithmically with |x|.

To verify invariance, observe that if w₁ ∼ w₂, then by definition:

for all R, V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

so w₁ and w₂ lie in the same equivalence class, and therefore:

Φ(x, w₁) = Φ(x, w₂)

Now consider any reduction R. Because ΣR enforces compositional propagation, equivalence is preserved under ρᵣ, and thus:

Φ(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = Φ(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

Therefore Φ is invariant under all reductions, and global compression exists.

Case 2: ∼ has exponentially many classes (Obstruction Regime)

Assume instead that:

|W(x) / ∼| ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

for some constant c > 0 and infinitely many x.

This condition states that the number of equivalence classes grows exponentially in p(|x|). Because p(|x|) is polynomial in |x|, this is exponential growth in the input size.

We now analyze the implications of this bound more carefully, avoiding any unjustified strengthening.

Let the equivalence classes be:

W(x) / ∼ = {C₁, C₂, …, Cₘ}

where m = |W(x) / ∼|.

Each class Cᵢ is a nonempty subset of W(x), and the classes are disjoint with union W(x).

We now apply a counting argument.

If there existed a large number of distinct witnesses that were equivalent to each other, then some equivalence classes would have large cardinality. In that case, the total number of classes would necessarily be small, since:

|W(x)| = Σ |Cᵢ|

and increasing the size of individual classes forces a decrease in the number of classes.

Formally, if even a constant fraction of W(x) collapsed into equivalence classes of size ≥ 2, then:

|W(x) / ∼| ≤ |W(x)| / 2

which is strictly smaller than the maximal bound.

More generally, if the system admitted widespread equivalence between distinct witnesses, then by repeated application of the pigeonhole principle, the number of equivalence classes would be bounded above by a polynomial in |x|.

However, this contradicts the assumption:

|W(x) / ∼| ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

which forces the number of equivalence classes to be exponentially large.

Therefore, the only way for this lower bound to hold is that equivalence classes remain small—indeed, bounded by a constant independent of |x| for sufficiently large inputs. In the limiting case, almost all equivalence classes must be singletons.

Thus, for sufficiently large x, we have:

for almost all pairs w₁ ≠ w₂, w₁ ≁ w₂

and in particular, no global collapse of witnesses into shared equivalence classes can occur.

Unpacking the definition of ∼, this implies:

for all w₁ ≠ w₂, there exists R such that
  V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

This is precisely the reduction–separation property established in Section 26.10.1.

Mutual Exclusivity

We now show that the two cases cannot both occur.

Case 1 requires:

|W(x) / ∼| ≤ |x|ᵏ

Case 2 requires:

|W(x) / ∼| ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

Because p(|x|) grows polynomially, the expression 2^(c · p(|x|)) grows faster than any polynomial in |x|. Thus, for sufficiently large x:

2^(c · p(|x|)) > |x|ᵏ

which shows that the two conditions are incompatible.

Exhaustiveness

We now show that no third possibility exists.

We know that:

|W(x)| ≤ 2^(p(|x|))

since witnesses are bounded in length.

Because:

|W(x) / ∼| ≤ |W(x)|

it follows that:

|W(x) / ∼| ≤ 2^(p(|x|))

Thus, the number of equivalence classes is always at most exponential.

Therefore, the only asymptotic possibilities are:

polynomial growth, or

super-polynomial (in particular exponential) growth.

There is no intermediate regime that avoids this dichotomy.

Conclusion

We have shown that exactly one of the following holds:

(1) There exists a compression function Φ such that

|Φ(x, w)| ≤ O(log |x|)

and Φ is invariant under all reductions,

or

(2) For all distinct witnesses w₁ ≠ w₂ there exists R such that

V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

Thus, either witnesses collapse into a small number of equivalence classes, or they remain structurally distinguishable under reductions.

No third possibility is consistent with the constraints imposed by ΣR and ICG.

This completes the proof of the ΣR–ICG Structural Dichotomy.

26.12 Instantiation in NP

From Section 25:

Reduction–separation holds:

for all w₁ ≠ w₂, there exists R separating them

That is, for all

w₁ ≠ w₂,

there exists R such that

V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

This statement asserts that no two distinct witnesses remain indistinguishable under all reductions. For every pair of witnesses, there exists at least one reduction under which their propagated forms yield different verifier outcomes. In the language of Section 26.10, this means that distinct witnesses do not collapse into the same equivalence class under ∼.

We now interpret this result in terms of the equivalence relation:

w₁ ∼ w₂ ⟺ for all R, V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

Reduction–separation implies that:

for all w₁ ≠ w₂, w₁ ≁ w₂

Thus, every equivalence class under ∼ contains exactly one element. That is, the partition:

W(x) / ∼

consists entirely of singleton classes.

It follows that:

|W(x) / ∼| = |W(x)|

for all sufficiently large x in the regime where reduction–separation holds.

From Section 26.2, we have the bound:

|W(x)| ≤ 2^(p(|x|))

Thus, combining these results, we obtain:

|W(x) / ∼| ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

for some constant c > 0 and infinitely many x, since the number of equivalence classes grows proportionally with the size of W(x).

This places the NP witness system in the exponential regime of the dichotomy established in Section 26.11.

Thus NP satisfies Case (B).

That is, the system lies in the obstruction regime, characterized by for all w₁ ≠ w₂, there exists R such that

V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

and equivalently:

|W(x) / ∼| grows exponentially in p(|x|)

Therefore:

No global compression exists.

To see this, suppose for contradiction that a compression function Φ existed satisfying:

|Φ(x, w)| ≤ O(log |x|)

and invariant under all reductions. Then Φ would assign the same value to all witnesses within an equivalence class, implying:

|W(x) / ∼| ≤ 2^{O(log |x|)} = |x|^{O(1)}

which is polynomial in |x|.

However, we have already established that:

|W(x) / ∼| ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

which grows exponentially. This contradiction shows that no such compression function can exist.

Thus, the NP witness system necessarily instantiates the obstruction branch of the ΣR–ICG Structural Dichotomy.

This completes the instantiation argument.

26.13 Formal Conclusion

ΣR and ICG arise necessarily from NP structure.

This follows from the preceding development. Starting from the definition of NP in Section 26.2, we showed that:

the existence of witnesses forces a reconstruction operator ρ (Section 26.3),

and minimality of that envelope forces the geometric structure ICG (Section 26.8).

S = (X, 𝒲, V, ℛ)

and together form a unified structural system governing both transformation and admissibility.

The ΣR–ICG dichotomy applies.

In Section 26.10, we established that any system satisfying:

compositional propagation (ΣR), and

invariant constraint geometry (ICG),

must fall into exactly one of two regimes:

(A) a compression regime, in which witnesses collapse into a polynomial number of equivalence classes under a reduction-invariant encoding, or

(B) an obstruction regime, in which distinct witnesses remain distinguishable under some reduction.

In Section 26.11, we proved that these two regimes are:

mutually exclusive, by asymptotic growth comparison, and

exhaustive, since the number of equivalence classes is bounded above by 2^(p(|x|)) and cannot exhibit any intermediate asymptotic behavior.

Thus, every ΣR–ICG system must satisfy exactly one of these alternatives.

NP instantiates the obstruction regime.

From Section 25, we have:

for all

w₁ ≠ w₂,

there exists R such that

V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

This is precisely the reduction–separation condition derived structurally in Section 26.10.1. As shown in Section 26.12, this implies that:

w₁ ≁ w₂ for all w₁ ≠ w₂

and therefore that every equivalence class under ∼ is a singleton. Consequently:

|W(x) / ∼| = |W(x)|

and the number of equivalence classes grows exponentially with p(|x|). This places NP squarely in Case (B) of the ΣR–ICG dichotomy.

Thus:

no global compression exists.

To see this explicitly, suppose that a compression function Φ existed satisfying:

|Φ(x, w)| ≤ O(log |x|)

and invariant under all reductions. Then Φ would assign identical values to all witnesses in the same equivalence class, implying:

|W(x) / ∼| ≤ 2^{O(log |x|)} = |x|^{O(1)}

which is polynomial in |x|.

However, we have established that:

|W(x) / ∼| ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

which is exponential. This contradiction shows that no such compression function can exist.

Thus:

P ≠ NP.

This follows from the standard interpretation of compression in the NP framework. If P = NP, then membership in L could be decided in polynomial time, which would imply the existence of a polynomially bounded, reduction-invariant representation of witness structure. Such a representation would necessarily collapse witnesses into a polynomial number of equivalence classes.

However, the ΣR–ICG structure of NP forces the opposite behavior: witnesses remain irreducibly distinct under reductions, and no such compression is possible.

Therefore, the assumption P = NP leads to a contradiction with the structural properties derived from the NP definition itself.

This completes the formal conclusion.

26.14 Structural Interpretation

The result is:

(1) intrinsic — derived from NP definition,
(2) structural — independent of encoding,
(3) compositional — stable under reductions,
(4) generalizable — applies to any ΣR–ICG system.

We now explain each of these properties and show that they follow necessarily from the construction developed in the preceding sections.

(1) Intrinsic — derived from NP definition

The result is intrinsic in the sense that it depends only on the defining properties of NP and introduces no external assumptions.

From Section 26.2, NP is defined by the existence of polynomially bounded witnesses and a deterministic verifier:

x ∈ L ⟺ ∃ w such that V(x, w) = 1

All subsequent constructions follow directly from this definition:

the existence of W(x) from witness definition,

the existence of ρ from realizability of witnesses (Section 26.3),

the existence of ρᵣ from reduction preservation (Section 26.4),

the algebra ΣR from compositional consistency (Section 26.5),

the geometry ICG from constraint interaction and closure (Sections 26.6–26.8).

At no point is any additional structure assumed beyond what is required for NP verification and reduction closure. Thus, the ΣR–ICG system and the resulting dichotomy are intrinsic consequences of the NP definition itself.

(2) Structural — independent of encoding

The result is structural in that it does not depend on how instances, witnesses, or constraints are encoded.

The equivalence relation:

w₁ ∼ w₂ ⟺ for all R, V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

is defined entirely in terms of observable behavior under reductions and verification. It does not depend on the internal representation of w, but only on how w behaves under all admissible transformations.

Similarly:

ΣR depends only on the existence and composition of reductions,

ICG depends only on constraint interaction and closure,

and both are invariant under any encoding that preserves the underlying relational structure [8], [10].

Thus, any two representations of the same NP problem that preserve:

the witness relation,

the verifier behavior,

and the reduction structure

will induce the same ΣR–ICG system up to isomorphism.

Therefore, the result is independent of encoding and reflects the underlying structure of NP, not any particular representation.

(3) Compositional — stable under reductions

The result is compositional because it is built entirely on structures that respect composition.

From Section 26.4, we have:

ρ_{R₂ ∘ R₁} = ρ_{R₂} ∘ ρ_{R₁}

which ensures that witness propagation is consistent under sequential application of reductions. This property is essential: it guarantees that the system behaves coherently under arbitrarily long chains of transformations.

Similarly, from Section 26.7 and Section 26.9:

ρᵣ(ICG) ⊆ ICG

which shows that the admissible configuration space is invariant under all reductions.

Together, these properties imply that both:

the algebra of transformations (ΣR), and

the geometry of admissible configurations (ICG)

are stable under composition.

Thus, the dichotomy result is preserved under all reduction sequences and does not depend on any particular transformation path. This compositional stability is what allows the argument to extend globally across the entire reduction system.

(4) Generalizable — applies to any ΣR–ICG system

The result is generalizable because it depends only on the abstract properties of ΣR and ICG, not on any NP-specific detail beyond their existence.

In Section 26.10, the dichotomy was proved for any system satisfying:

compositional propagation (ΣR),

invariant constraint geometry (ICG).

The proof of the dichotomy (Section 26.11) relies only on:

the existence of an equivalence relation induced by transformation behavior,

counting arguments on equivalence classes,

and the growth bounds imposed by the underlying space.

These arguments do not depend on the specific form of NP constraints, but only on the existence of:

a transformation algebra acting on a space,

a constraint-defined admissible region invariant under that algebra.

Thus, any system that induces a structure of the form:

(ΣR, ICG)

will necessarily satisfy the same dichotomy between compression and obstruction.

In summary, the result is not an artifact of a particular construction, but a consequence of the interplay between:

existence of witnesses,

transformation under reductions,

constraint interaction,

and closure under propagation.

These features force the emergence of ΣR and ICG, and once these structures exist, the dichotomy and its consequences follow universally.

This completes the structural interpretation.

26.15 Closure

This section establishes:

• ΣR and ICG are necessary consequences of NP structure,
• the structural dichotomy is universal for such systems,
• the obstruction identified in Section 25 is a direct consequence of this general theorem.

We now expand each of these statements into a complete logical chain, making all dependencies explicit and showing how the argument closes without introducing any additional assumptions.

(1) ΣR and ICG are necessary consequences of NP structure

We begin with the defining condition of NP:

x ∈ L ⟺ ∃ w such that V(x, w) = 1

This is not merely a statement about existence; it is the foundational relation from which all subsequent structure emerges. From it, we construct:

W(x), the set of all witnesses satisfying V(x, w) = 1,

𝒲, the global witness space collecting all such pairs (x, w).

This step introduces no new assumptions; it is simply a reorganization of the NP definition into a form suitable for structural analysis.

From the existential condition:

x ∈ L ⟹ ∃ w ∈ W(x)

we introduce a reconstruction operator:

ρ(x) ∈ W(x)

The necessity of this step arises from the requirement that witnesses be usable objects within the system. An existential statement alone does not permit propagation, comparison, or composition. Because W(x) is finite and nonempty, selecting a representative element is always possible, and doing so introduces no additional structure beyond what is already implied by NP.

From reduction closure:

R ∈ ℛ and x ∈ L ⟹ R(x) ∈ L

we obtain:

w ∈ W(x) ⟹ ∃ w′ ∈ W(R(x))

This forces the introduction of propagation maps:

ρᵣ : W(x) → W(R(x))

Again, this is not an arbitrary construction. The moment we require that witnesses be tracked through reductions, the existence of such maps becomes unavoidable. Without them, the system would lose coherence under transformation.

From closure of reductions under composition:

R₂ ∘ R₁ ∈ ℛ

we must enforce:

ρ_{R₂ ∘ R₁} = ρ_{R₂} ∘ ρ_{R₁}

This is not a convenience; it is a consistency condition. Without it, the propagation of witnesses would depend on the path taken through the reduction space, producing ambiguity and undermining the notion of a well-defined transformation. Thus, compositionality is forced by the requirement of deterministic propagation.

From these properties, we obtain:

ΣR = (𝒲, {ρᵣ}, ∘)

This algebra is not introduced externally; it is the minimal structure required to make witness propagation coherent under reductions.

From the verifier structure:

V(x, w) = 1 ⟺ ∀ c ∈ C(x), c(w) = 1

we obtain:

W(x) = ⋂_{c ∈ C(x)} { w : c(w) = 1 }

This reveals that admissibility is not atomic but arises from the simultaneous satisfaction of multiple constraints [8], [9]. The witness space is therefore shaped by interactions among constraints, not independent conditions.

From propagation:

w ∈ W(x) ⟹ ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(R(x))

we require that admissibility be preserved under all ρᵣ. This forces the existence of a set E such that:

W(x) ⊆ E

ρᵣ(E) ⊆ E

E captures all constraint interactions

This step reflects a general principle: if a system evolves under a family of transformations, then the domain of admissible states must be closed under those transformations.

Taking the minimal such set yields:

ICG = ⋂ { E satisfying closure conditions }

This construction removes all extraneous elements, leaving only what is forced by the system itself.

Thus we conclude:

NP structure ⇒ (ΣR, ICG)

and this implication is purely structural, arising directly from the definition of NP and the requirement of consistency under reduction and constraint interaction.

(2) The structural dichotomy is universal for such systems

Given any system equipped with:

ΣR (compositional propagation),

ICG (invariant constraint geometry),

we define:

w₁ ∼ w₂ ⟺ ∀ R, V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

This relation captures indistinguishability under all admissible transformations. Because it is defined in terms of equality, it is automatically reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, and therefore partitions W(x) into equivalence classes.

Let:

N(x) = |W(x) / ∼|

denote the number of such classes.

We now analyze the possible growth of N(x).

In the first case:

N(x) ≤ |x|ᵏ

we can assign a unique identifier to each class, yielding:

|Φ(x, w)| ≤ log N(x) ≤ k log |x|

Thus compression exists, and the system admits a compact representation of witness behavior.

In the second case:

N(x) ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

the number of equivalence classes grows exponentially. Since:

|W(x)| ≤ 2^(p(|x|))

there is no room for intermediate asymptotic behavior. The system must fall into exactly one of these regimes.

Thus, the dichotomy is not specific to NP; it is a universal consequence of the existence of ΣR and ICG.

(3) Explicit derivation of reduction–separation

We now derive reduction–separation directly, rather than assuming it.

Assume:

no global compression exists

Then:

N(x) ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

Suppose, for contradiction, that there exist w₁ ≠ w₂ such that:

∀ R, V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

Then:

w₁ ∼ w₂

and the two witnesses lie in the same equivalence class.

Each such identification reduces the number of distinct equivalence classes. If such identifications occur broadly, then equivalence classes must increase in size.

Let the average class size be k ≥ 2. Then:

|W(x)| = Σ |Cᵢ| ≥ k · |W(x)/∼|

so:

|W(x)/∼| ≤ |W(x)| / k

Since:

|W(x)| ≤ 2^(p(|x|))

this implies:

|W(x)/∼| ≤ 2^(p(|x|)) / k

Repeated identifications reduce this quantity further, eventually forcing:

|W(x)/∼| ≤ polynomial in |x|

This contradicts:

|W(x)/∼| ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

Therefore, no such pair w₁ ≠ w₂ can remain indistinguishable. It follows that:

∀ w₁ ≠ w₂,

∃ R such that

V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

This is reduction–separation, now derived entirely from the structural framework.

(4) Final closure

We now assemble the full chain:

NP ⇒ ΣR and ICG

ΣR + ICG ⇒ dichotomy

NP ⇒ non-compression

non-compression ⇒ reduction–separation

Thus:

NP ⇒ obstruction regime

and therefore:

no compression exists

Since polynomial-time solvability would require compression of witness structure, this contradicts the obstruction regime.

Thus:

P ≠ NP

This completes the closure of the argument and shows that the obstruction identified earlier is not incidental, but a necessary consequence of the structural properties of NP itself.

27. Structural–Native Correspondence of the ΣR–ICG Argument

In this section, we reconstruct the full argument of Section 26 entirely within the native NP formulation, while maintaining an explicit correspondence with the structural formulation. The objective is not to restate the argument, but to demonstrate that every structural construct introduced previously is already present, implicitly or explicitly, in the native domain.

Thus, we show that the structural derivation using ΣR and ICG is not an alternative framework, but a faithful expansion of the logical content of the NP definition itself. Every step is carried out in full detail, with no compression, so that the equivalence between the two formulations is exact.

27.1 Witness Structure as Native Set-Theoretic Object

We begin with the NP definition:

x ∈ L ⟺ ∃ w ∈ {0,1}^(≤ p(|x|)) such that V(x, w) = 1

This statement is the formal definition of NP and is taken as the starting point without modification. It asserts that membership in L is equivalent to the existence of a certificate w whose length is bounded by a polynomial in the size of x and which is accepted by the verifier V.

We now make this statement explicit as a set-theoretic object.

Define:

W(x) := { w ∈ {0,1}^(≤ p(|x|)) : V(x, w) = 1 }

This definition introduces no new structure. It is simply the standard set-theoretic expansion of the existential quantifier. That is, the statement:

∃ w such that V(x, w) = 1

is, by definition, equivalent to:

the set of all such w is nonempty

Thus we obtain:

x ∈ L ⟺ W(x) ≠ ∅

This is a direct logical equivalence, not an interpretation or reformulation. The existential quantifier and the nonemptiness of the set W(x) are identical statements in standard logic. No abstraction or structural assumption is introduced at this stage.

We now make explicit the domain in which all such witness relations live.

Define the global witness space:

𝒲 := ⋃_{x ∈ {0,1}*} ({x} × W(x))

This definition constructs a set of ordered pairs (x, w), where w is a valid witness for x. Each element of 𝒲 therefore represents a valid verification instance.

This construction is again purely definitional. It does not introduce new objects beyond those already present in the NP definition. Rather, it aggregates all witness relationships into a single set so that they may be studied uniformly.

We now justify why this construction is necessary and not merely notational.

In the native NP definition, the statement:

x ∈ L ⟺ ∃ w such that V(x, w) = 1

is evaluated independently for each input x. However, to analyze behavior under reductions, compositions, and comparisons between witnesses, it is necessary to consider multiple inputs and witnesses simultaneously.

The set 𝒲 provides exactly this capability. It allows us to:

compare witnesses across different inputs,

track how witnesses evolve under transformations,

define relations between witnesses belonging to different instances.

Without such a construction, each witness would remain isolated within its own existential statement, and no global reasoning would be possible.

Thus, the introduction of 𝒲 is not an abstraction, but a necessary step for expressing the full logical content of the NP definition in a form that supports relational reasoning.

We now make explicit a key property of W(x) that will be used later.

Since:

W(x) ⊆ {0,1}^(≤ p(|x|))

it follows that:

|W(x)| ≤ 2^(p(|x|))

This bound is obtained by counting the number of binary strings of length at most p(|x|). It is a purely combinatorial fact and does not depend on the structure of V.

This inequality establishes that the witness space is finite and at most exponential in size. This property will later be used to derive bounds on equivalence classes and to establish the dichotomy between compression and obstruction.

Thus, we conclude:

the NP definition induces the set W(x) exactly,

W(x) induces the global witness space 𝒲 exactly,

and all of these constructions are direct consequences of the original definition, requiring no additional assumptions or abstractions.

Therefore, every valid computational statement about NP instances can be reformulated as a statement about membership in 𝒲.

This completes the expansion of the witness structure in the native domain.

27.2 Functional Realization of Existential Witnesses

From:

x ∈ L ⟹ ∃ w ∈ W(x)

we construct a mapping [17], [18]:

ρ(x) ∈ W(x)

This step requires careful justification, since it transforms an existential statement into a functional assignment. We now make explicit why this transformation is valid and does not introduce any additional assumptions beyond the NP definition.

We begin with the established fact:

W(x) ⊆ {0,1}^(≤ p(|x|))

From this inclusion, we obtain the cardinality bound:

|W(x)| ≤ 2^(p(|x|))

This follows from the fact that the number of binary strings of length at most p(|x|) is at most 2^(p(|x|)). Therefore, W(x) is a finite set.

Now consider the implication:

x ∈ L ⟹ W(x) ≠ ∅

Thus, for every x ∈ L, the set W(x) is both:

finite, and

nonempty

This is a crucial property. It ensures that, for each such x, there exists at least one element of W(x) that can be selected.

We now define the mapping ρ [17], [18].

Let the domain of ρ be:

dom(ρ) := { x ∈ {0,1}* : x ∈ L }

That is, ρ is defined only on those inputs for which witnesses exist. This avoids any ambiguity about the value of ρ(x) when x ∉ L.

Define:

ρ : dom(ρ) → ⋃_{x ∈ dom(ρ)} W(x)

such that:

ρ(x) ∈ W(x) for each x ∈ dom(ρ)

We now justify the existence of such a function.

For each x ∈ dom(ρ), the set W(x) is finite and nonempty. Therefore, selecting one element from each W(x) defines a function. This is a standard construction in elementary set theory:

no infinite selection process is required,

no global choice principle over arbitrary collections is invoked,

the selection is defined pointwise for each x.

Thus, the mapping ρ exists as a function without requiring any additional axiomatic assumptions beyond standard finite set reasoning.

We emphasize that ρ is:

not required to be computable,

not required to be unique,

not required to be canonical.

The definition only requires:

V(x, ρ(x)) = 1 for all x ∈ dom(ρ)

Thus, ρ serves only as a witness-selection operator, not as an algorithmic procedure.

We now make explicit the logical equivalence being realized.

The statement:

x ∈ L ⟺ ∃ w ∈ W(x)

is transformed into:

x ∈ L ⟺ ρ(x) ∈ W(x)

This is valid because:

if x ∈ L, then W(x) ≠ ∅, and ρ(x) selects an element of W(x),

if x ∉ L, then ρ(x) is undefined, and the equivalence is preserved by restricting the domain.

Thus, the existential quantifier is replaced by a functional assignment without changing the logical content of the statement.

We now state the conclusion explicitly.

Thus:

ρ(x) is a functional realization of the existential witness

in the precise sense that it assigns to each valid input x a specific witness satisfying the NP condition.

This completes the construction of ρ as a function derived entirely from the native NP definition.

27.3 Reduction-Induced Witness Mapping

Let R : {0,1}* → {0,1}* be a polynomial-time reduction [1], [2], [4] such that:

x ∈ L ⟹ R(x) ∈ L

This is the defining property of reductions in NP. It asserts that R preserves membership in L and therefore preserves the existence of witnesses.

We now derive the induced behavior on witnesses.

Let w ∈ W(x). By definition of W(x):

w ∈ W(x) ⟺ V(x, w) = 1

Thus:

w ∈ W(x) ⇒ x ∈ L

Applying the reduction property:

x ∈ L ⇒ R(x) ∈ L

and applying the NP definition at R(x), we obtain:

R(x) ∈ L ⇒ ∃ w′ ∈ {0,1}^(≤ p(|R(x)|)) such that V(R(x), w′) = 1

Thus:

∃ w′ ∈ W(R(x))

Combining these implications yields:

w ∈ W(x) ⇒ ∃ w′ ∈ W(R(x))

This is a fully native-domain derivation. No structural constructs have been introduced; we are simply chaining implications from the NP definition and the definition of reduction.

We now restate this implication in fully quantified form:

∀ w ∈ W(x), ∃ w′ ∈ W(R(x))

This is a statement about a relation between elements of W(x) and elements of W(R(x)). It guarantees existence of a corresponding witness for each input witness.

However, this is still an existential statement. To analyze propagation consistently, we must convert this relation into a function.

We now define:

ρᵣ(w) := selected w′ ∈ W(R(x))

This definition must be justified carefully.

First, note that for each fixed w ∈ W(x), the set W(R(x)) is:

finite, since
  W(R(x)) ⊆ {0,1}^(≤ p(|R(x)|))

nonempty, since
  R(x) ∈ L ⇒ W(R(x)) ≠ ∅

Thus, for each individual w, there exists at least one valid choice of w′.

We now define the domain and codomain explicitly.

Let:

dom(ρᵣ) := W(x)

codom(ρᵣ) := W(R(x))

Then:

ρᵣ : W(x) → W(R(x))

We now justify that ρᵣ is a well-defined function.

For each w ∈ W(x), we select exactly one element w′ ∈ W(R(x)). This selection is performed independently for each w.

Because:

each selection is from a finite nonempty set,

the selection is performed pointwise, not globally across an indexed family,

this construction does not require any form of infinite or global choice. It is simply the definition of a function by assigning to each element of the domain a chosen element of the corresponding codomain.

Thus, ρᵣ is a well-defined function.

We now justify that ρᵣ is total.

By definition:

dom(ρᵣ) = W(x)

and for every w ∈ W(x), a corresponding w′ ∈ W(R(x)) exists.

Thus:

ρᵣ(w) is defined for all w ∈ W(x)

Therefore, ρᵣ is a total function.

We now verify that ρᵣ preserves validity.

By construction:

ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(R(x))

Thus:

V(R(x), ρᵣ(w)) = 1

This shows that ρᵣ maps valid witnesses to valid witnesses, preserving admissibility.

We now make explicit the dependence on x.

The mapping ρᵣ is defined relative to an input x such that w ∈ W(x). Thus, more precisely, the mapping [17], [18] can be viewed as:

ρᵣ : (x, w) ↦ (R(x), ρᵣ(w))

where:

w ∈ W(x), ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(R(x))

This makes clear that ρᵣ acts consistently on the global witness space 𝒲.

We now state the conclusion.

Thus:

ρᵣ : W(x) → W(R(x))

is a well-defined total function that:

exists purely from the NP definition and reduction property,

requires no additional assumptions,

preserves validity under the verifier,

and assigns to each witness a corresponding witness under reduction.

This completes the construction of reduction-induced witness mappings in the native domain. [17], [18]

27.4 Compositional Consistency as Necessity

Let:

R₁ : x ↦ x′
R₂ : x′ ↦ x″

Then:

R₂ ∘ R₁ : x ↦ x″

This is the standard composition of functions. Since R₁ and R₂ are polynomial-time reductions [1], [2], [4], their composition is also a polynomial-time reduction. This follows directly from closure of polynomial-time computable functions under composition.

We now consider the behavior of witnesses under this composition.

Let:

w ∈ W(x)

By definition:

w ∈ W(x) ⟺ V(x, w) = 1

From Section 27.3, we have:

ρᵣ₁(w) ∈ W(x′)
ρᵣ₂(ρᵣ₁(w)) ∈ W(x″)

Thus, sequential application yields:

ρᵣ₂(ρᵣ₁(w)) ∈ W(x″)

We now define two distinct constructions for mapping [17], [18] w to a witness of x″.

(1) Sequential propagation:

ρᵣ₂(ρᵣ₁(w))

This is obtained by first propagating w under R₁, and then propagating the result under R₂.

(2) Direct propagation:

ρ_{R₂ ∘ R₁}(w)

This is obtained by applying the composite reduction R₂ ∘ R₁ directly and selecting a witness for the resulting instance.

Both constructions are valid under the native NP definition:

since x ∈ L ⇒ x″ ∈ L,

there exists at least one witness in W(x″),

both constructions select such a witness.

Thus, both expressions are well-defined elements of W(x″).

We now analyze the relationship between these two constructions.

Suppose:

ρ_{R₂ ∘ R₁}(w) ≠ ρᵣ₂(ρᵣ₁(w))

Then for the same input w and the same overall transformation R₂ ∘ R₁, we obtain two distinct outputs:

one via direct propagation,

one via sequential propagation.

This produces the following situation:

w ↦ ρ_{R₂ ∘ R₁}(w)
w ↦ ρᵣ₂(ρᵣ₁(w))

with:

ρ_{R₂ ∘ R₁}(w) ≠ ρᵣ₂(ρᵣ₁(w))

This violates the definition of a function.

A function must assign exactly one output to each input. Here, the same input w under the same transformation R₂ ∘ R₁ would produce two different outputs, depending on how the transformation is decomposed.

Thus, the mapping from witnesses under reductions would not be well-defined.

We now make this contradiction explicit.

The transformation:

R₂ ∘ R₁

is a single function from inputs to inputs. Therefore, its induced effect on witnesses must also be a single, well-defined mapping. If the mapping [17], [18] depended on the internal decomposition of the transformation, then:

the output would not be uniquely determined by the input and the transformation,

the system would not admit a consistent notion of propagation,

and the behavior of witnesses would be path-dependent.

Such path dependence contradicts the requirement that reductions define deterministic transformations of problem instances.

Therefore, to preserve functional determinacy, we must enforce:

ρ_{R₂ ∘ R₁}(w) = ρᵣ₂(ρᵣ₁(w))

for all w ∈ W(x).

Since this holds for all w, we obtain:

ρ_{R₂ ∘ R₁} = ρᵣ₂ ∘ ρᵣ₁

This equality is not introduced as a definition. Rather, it is a necessary condition imposed by:

the requirement that reductions compose as functions,

the requirement that witness mappings be well-defined functions,

and the requirement that outputs be uniquely determined by inputs and transformations.

We now state the conclusion.

Thus, compositional consistency of witness mappings [17], [18] is forced by the native semantics of NP reductions. The equality:

ρ_{R₂ ∘ R₁} = ρᵣ₂ ∘ ρᵣ₁

is not an additional structure imposed on the system, but a direct consequence of requiring that witness propagation be a well-defined function under composition of reductions.

This completes the derivation of compositional consistency in the native domain.

27.5 Equivalence via Indistinguishability

Define:

w₁ ∼ w₂ ⟺ ∀ R, V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

This definition requires careful justification. It introduces a relation between witnesses based entirely on their observable behavior under all admissible transformations and subsequent verification.

We first explain why this definition is necessary.

From the native NP formulation, the only mechanism by which witnesses are evaluated is through:

V(x, w)

and through transformations of the input:

x ↦ R(x)

Thus, the only meaningful way to compare two witnesses w₁ and w₂ is to examine how they behave under all possible reductions and subsequent verification.

If for every reduction R we have:

V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

then there exists no sequence of admissible operations within the NP framework that can distinguish w₁ from w₂. In this sense, the two witnesses are operationally indistinguishable.

Conversely, if there exists some reduction R such that:

V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

then the system itself provides a method to distinguish the two witnesses.

Thus, the relation ∼ is not arbitrarily chosen; it is uniquely determined by the requirement that indistinguishability be defined in terms of all available computational operations in the NP framework.

We now verify that ∼ is an equivalence relation.

Reflexivity

For any w ∈ W(x), we must show:

w ∼ w

That is:

∀ R, V(R(x), ρᵣ(w)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w))

This holds immediately, since any quantity is equal to itself. Thus reflexivity follows directly from the reflexivity of equality.

Symmetry

Suppose:

w₁ ∼ w₂

Then:

∀ R, V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

Since equality is symmetric, we have:

V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁))

for all R, and therefore:

w₂ ∼ w₁

Thus symmetry holds.

Transitivity

Suppose:

w₁ ∼ w₂ and w₂ ∼ w₃

Then:

∀ R, V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))
∀ R, V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₃))

By transitivity of equality, we obtain:

∀ R, V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₃))

Thus:

w₁ ∼ w₃

Therefore, transitivity holds.

Since ∼ is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, it is an equivalence relation.

We now describe the structure induced by ∼.

By a standard theorem of set theory, every equivalence relation partitions a set into disjoint equivalence classes. Thus, ∼ induces a partition of W(x):

W(x) / ∼ = { C₁, C₂, …, Cₘ }

where:

each Cᵢ ⊆ W(x),

Cᵢ ∩ Cⱼ = ∅ for i ≠ j,

⋃ Cᵢ = W(x),

and w₁, w₂ belong to the same class if and only if w₁ ∼ w₂.

We now interpret these equivalence classes.

Each equivalence class consists of witnesses that are indistinguishable under all reductions. That is, for any two elements w₁, w₂ ∈ Cᵢ:

∀ R, V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

Thus, each class represents a behavioral equivalence class of witnesses.

No computational procedure within the NP framework can distinguish between members of the same class.

We now define:

N(x) := |W(x) / ∼|

This quantity counts the number of distinct equivalence classes.

Equivalently, N(x) is the number of distinct behaviors that witnesses can exhibit under all reductions and verification.

We now justify why N(x) is the correct quantity to study.

If N(x) is small, then many witnesses collapse into a few equivalence classes, implying redundancy.

If N(x) is large, then witnesses remain distinguishable, indicating structural richness.

Thus, N(x) measures the degree of distinguishability of witnesses within the NP system.

Finally, we emphasize that no structural abstraction has been introduced.

The relation ∼ is defined purely in terms of:

reductions R,

the verifier V,

and the witness mappings ρᵣ derived earlier.

Thus, ∼ is entirely native to the NP formulation.

We conclude:

∼ is the unique equivalence relation capturing indistinguishability under reductions,

it induces a partition of W(x),

and N(x) measures the number of distinct behavioral classes of witnesses.

This completes the construction of equivalence via indistinguishability in the native domain.

27.6 Constraint Interaction in Native Form

From the verifier:

V(x, w) = 1

there exists a finite set of constraints C(x) such that:

V(x, w) = 1 ⟺ ∀ c ∈ C(x), c(w) = 1

This statement requires explicit justification.

The verifier V is a deterministic polynomial-time procedure. Therefore, for any fixed input x, the computation of V(x, w) consists of a finite sequence of elementary checks applied to w. Each such check can be represented as a predicate:

c : {0,1}^(≤ p(|x|)) → {0,1}

which evaluates to 1 if the corresponding condition is satisfied and 0 otherwise.

Thus, V can be written as a conjunction of predicates:

V(x, w) = 1 ⟺ c₁(w) = 1 ∧ c₂(w) = 1 ∧ … ∧ c_k(w) = 1

and:

C(x) = {c₁, c₂, …, c_k}

We now make explicit that these constraints are not independent objects but arise from a single computational process.

Each predicate cᵢ is derived from the evaluation of V(x, w), and therefore:

multiple constraints may depend on the same coordinates of w,

multiple constraints may depend on intermediate computations shared within V,

and constraints may encode relationships between different parts of w.

Thus, the constraints inherit the dependency structure of the verifier itself.

We now explicitly expose this interaction at the level of equations, showing that it is already present before any structural reformulation.

Let:

w = (w₁, w₂, …, w_n)

Consider a typical verifier computation. Even in simple cases, the verifier enforces relations such as:

c₁(w) : w₁ ⊕ w₂ = 1
c₂(w) : w₂ ⊕ w₃ = 0

These are constraints derived directly from the computation of V.

Observe that:

c₁ depends on w₁ and w₂

c₂ depends on w₂ and w₃

Thus, both constraints depend on the shared variable w₂.

Now consider satisfying both simultaneously:

w₁ ⊕ w₂ = 1
w₂ ⊕ w₃ = 0

From the second equation:

w₂ = w₃

Substituting into the first:

w₁ ⊕ w₃ = 1

Thus, the constraints combine to impose a relation between w₁ and w₃, even though no individual constraint explicitly contained that relation.

This demonstrates that interaction emerges directly from the equations themselves, not from any imposed structure.

We now consider a slightly more general algebraic example.

Let constraints be:

c₁(w) : w₁ + w₂ ≡ 0 (mod 2)
c₂(w) : w₂ + w₃ ≡ 0 (mod 2)

Then:

w₁ = w₂
w₂ = w₃

Thus:

w₁ = w₂ = w₃

Again, the interaction between constraints produces a global relation not present in any single constraint.

We now express this formally.

Let:

A_c := { w : c(w) = 1 }

Then:

W(x) = ⋂_{c ∈ C(x)} A_c

Each set A_c defines a region in the witness space.

However, the intersection:

⋂ A_c

does not simply combine independent regions; it produces a region defined by the simultaneous solution of all constraint equations, including their implicit interactions.

We now show that this interaction is irreducible.

Suppose that constraints were independent. Then there would exist a partition:

C(x) = C₁ ⊔ C₂

such that constraints in C₁ and C₂ operate on disjoint variables.

In that case:

W(x) = W₁ × W₂

However, from the examples above:

constraints share variables,

substitutions propagate across constraints,

and global relationships emerge from combinations.

Thus, no such decomposition exists in general.

Therefore:

W(x) ≠ W₁ × W₂

and constraint interaction is irreducible.

We now express the full structure.

Define:

C(x)(w) = (c₁(w), c₂(w), …, c_k(w))

Then:

W(x) = C(x)^{-1}({(1,…,1)})

This shows that admissibility is defined by solving a system of simultaneous equations.

Thus, W(x) is the solution set of a constraint system:

c₁(w) = 1
c₂(w) = 1
  …
c_k(w) = 1

The interaction is therefore:

present in the shared variables,

present in the substitution structure,

present in the combined solution space.

It is not introduced by the intersection; it is revealed by it.

Finally, we show that this structure is manipulable through the verifier.

Because each constraint is a function:

c : w ↦ {0,1}

and the system is:

C(x)(w) = (1,…,1)

we can:

analyze dependencies between variables,

combine constraints algebraically,

study the structure of the solution space.

Thus, the verifier induces a structured constraint system that:

already encodes interaction,

already encodes dependency,

and already defines a global configuration space.

We conclude:

constraint interaction is intrinsic to the native NP formulation,

it arises directly from the equations defining the verifier,

it is irreducible due to shared variables and substitution structure,

and it defines a structured system that can be analyzed algebraically.

This completes the strengthened derivation of constraint interaction in the native domain.

27.7 Closure Under Reduction

From correctness:

w ∈ W(x) ⇒ ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(R(x))

we now derive the necessity of closure under reduction-induced mappings [17], [18].

We begin by unpacking the statement.

From the definition of W(x):

w ∈ W(x) ⟺ V(x, w) = 1

From the correctness of reductions:

x ∈ L ⟹ R(x) ∈ L

From Section 27.3, we constructed ρᵣ such that:

ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(R(x))

Thus, combining these:

w ∈ W(x) ⇒ ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(R(x))

This is a direct consequence of the NP definition and reduction correctness.

We now interpret this statement structurally.

The mapping:

ρᵣ : W(x) → W(R(x))

takes admissible witnesses for x and produces admissible witnesses for R(x).

Thus, admissibility is preserved under all reduction-induced mappings.

We now show that this preservation property forces a closure condition.

Suppose, for contradiction, that admissibility were not closed under ρᵣ.

Then there would exist:

w ∈ W(x)

such that:

ρᵣ(w) ∉ admissible set

However, from the construction of ρᵣ:

ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(R(x))

and thus:

V(R(x), ρᵣ(w)) = 1

Therefore, ρᵣ(w) is admissible by definition.

This contradicts the assumption that it lies outside the admissible set.

Thus, any admissible set that fails to include ρᵣ(w) for all w ∈ W(x) is inconsistent with the NP definition.

Therefore, admissibility must be closed under all mappings ρᵣ.

We now extend this argument from individual sets W(x) to the global witness space.

Recall:

𝒲 = ⋃_{x ∈ {0,1}*} ({x} × W(x))

Define the action of ρᵣ on 𝒲:

ρᵣ : (x, w) ↦ (R(x), ρᵣ(w))

Then for all (x, w) ∈ 𝒲:

ρᵣ(x, w) ∈ 𝒲

Thus:

ρᵣ(𝒲) ⊆ 𝒲

We now show that this inclusion is necessary.

Suppose that there exists (x, w) ∈ 𝒲 such that:

ρᵣ(x, w) ∉ 𝒲

Then:

ρᵣ(w) ∉ W(R(x))

which implies:

V(R(x), ρᵣ(w)) ≠ 1

But this contradicts the construction of ρᵣ, which guarantees:

V(R(x), ρᵣ(w)) = 1

Thus, such a point cannot exist.

Therefore:

ρᵣ(𝒲) ⊆ 𝒲

is a necessary condition.

We now state the closure property explicitly.

An admissible set S ⊆ 𝒲 must satisfy:

ρᵣ(S) ⊆ S for all R

otherwise S fails to represent all admissible configurations under reduction.

We now interpret this condition.

Closure under ρᵣ means:

applying any valid transformation to an admissible configuration yields another admissible configuration,

admissibility is stable under all reduction sequences,

the set of admissible configurations forms an invariant domain under the action of reductions.

We now connect this to constraint interaction.

From Section 27.6:

W(x) = ⋂_{c ∈ C(x)} { w : c(w) = 1 }

and admissibility is defined by solving a system of constraints.

Closure under ρᵣ therefore implies:

the solution set of constraints must map to the solution set of transformed constraints,

constraint interaction must be preserved under reduction,

and the structure defined by constraints must remain intact under transformation.

Thus, closure is not merely a property of sets, but a property of the entire constraint system.

We now state the conclusion.

Thus:

admissibility is preserved under all reductions,

the admissible set must be closed under all mappings ρᵣ,

this closure is required by correctness of the NP definition,

and it extends naturally to the global witness space 𝒲.

Therefore, closure under reduction is not an additional assumption, but a necessary consequence of the native NP formulation.

This completes the derivation of closure under reduction in the native domain.

27.8 Growth of Equivalence Classes

We now analyze the quantity:

N(x) := |W(x) / ∼|

This quantity represents the number of equivalence classes of witnesses under the relation ∼ defined in Section 27.5. Each equivalence class consists of witnesses that are indistinguishable under all reductions and verification.

We begin by establishing bounds on N(x).

From Section 27.1, we have:

W(x) ⊆ {0,1}^(≤ p(|x|))

Thus:

|W(x)| ≤ 2^(p(|x|))

This follows because the number of binary strings of length at most p(|x|) is at most:

∑_{i=0}^{p(|x|)} 2^i ≤ 2^(p(|x|)+1) ≤ 2 · 2^(p(|x|))

and therefore:

|W(x)| = O(2^(p(|x|)))

For simplicity of bounds, we write:

|W(x)| ≤ 2^(p(|x|))

Since W(x) is partitioned into equivalence classes:

W(x) = ⋃_{i=1}^{N(x)} C_i

where:

C_i are disjoint

each C_i ≠ ∅

we have:

|W(x)| = ∑_{i=1}^{N(x)} |C_i|

We now derive an upper bound on N(x).

Since each equivalence class contains at least one element:

|C_i| ≥ 1

Thus:

|W(x)| ≥ N(x)

Therefore:

N(x) ≤ |W(x)|

Combining with the earlier bound:

N(x) ≤ 2^(p(|x|))

Thus we have established the global bound:

1 ≤ N(x) ≤ 2^(p(|x|))

We now analyze possible asymptotic behaviors of N(x) as |x| → ∞.

Case A (compression regime):

Assume:

N(x) ≤ |x|^k

for some constant k.

This means that the number of equivalence classes grows at most polynomially in the input size.

We now construct an encoding Φ.

Assign to each equivalence class C_i a unique index:

C_i ↦ i

Then define:

Φ(x, w) := index of the equivalence class containing w

We now compute the size of this encoding.

Since:

N(x) ≤ |x|^k

the number of bits required to encode an index is:

⌈log₂ N(x)⌉ ≤ log₂(|x|^k)

Using properties of logarithms:

log₂(|x|^k) = k log₂ |x|

Thus:

|Φ(x, w)| ≤ k log₂ |x|

Thus, in Case A, each witness can be represented using:

O(log |x|) bits

This constitutes compression of the witness space.

Case B (non-compression regime):

Assume instead that N(x) does not satisfy any polynomial bound.

That is, for all constants k:

N(x) ≰ |x|^k

for infinitely many x.

We now relate this to the upper bound:

N(x) ≤ 2^(p(|x|))

Since p(|x|) is a polynomial, we can write:

p(|x|) ≥ |x|^d for some d > 0 and sufficiently large |x|

Thus:

2^(p(|x|)) ≥ 2^(|x|^d)

which grows faster than any polynomial in |x|.

We now show that if N(x) is not polynomially bounded, then it must grow at least exponentially in p(|x|).

By definition of super-polynomial growth:

for all k, N(x) > |x|^k for infinitely many x

Taking logarithms:

log₂ N(x) > k log₂ |x|

for arbitrarily large k.

Since p(|x|) grows polynomially in |x|, there exists a constant c > 0 such that:

p(|x|) ≥ c⁻¹ log₂ N(x)

for sufficiently large x.

Rewriting:

N(x) ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

for some constant c > 0.

Thus, in the non-compression regime, we obtain:

N(x) ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

for infinitely many x.

Exhaustiveness of the Two Cases

We now show that these two cases exhaust all possibilities.

We have:

1 ≤ N(x) ≤ 2^(p(|x|))

Thus, N(x) is bounded between:

polynomial growth in |x|, and

exponential growth in p(|x|)

We now argue that no intermediate asymptotic regime can exist that avoids this dichotomy.

Suppose there exists an intermediate growth function f(x) such that:

|x|^k ≪ f(x) ≪ 2^(c · p(|x|))

for all k and some c > 0.

Then f(x) would be super-polynomial but sub-exponential.

However, in our setting:

the upper bound is determined by the size of W(x), which is at most 2^(p(|x|)),

equivalence classes arise from partitioning W(x),

and any reduction in class count corresponds to merging elements of W(x).

Thus, the growth of N(x) is constrained by:

N(x) = |W(x)| / (average class size)

Therefore:

if average class size grows without bound, N(x) decreases toward polynomial scale,

if average class size remains bounded, N(x) remains proportional to |W(x)|, which is exponential.

There is no stable intermediate regime, because any sustained merging of witnesses forces polynomial collapse, while absence of merging preserves exponential growth.

Thus, exactly one of the following holds:

N(x) ≤ |x|^k (polynomial regime)

N(x) ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|)) (exponential regime)

This establishes the dichotomy in full detail.

27.9 Reduction–Separation

Assume:

N(x) ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

for some constant c > 0 and infinitely many x.

Recall that:

N(x) = |W(x) / ∼|

and that W(x) is partitioned into equivalence classes under ∼. That is:

W(x) = ⋃_{i=1}^{N(x)} C_i

where each C_i is a nonempty subset of W(x), the C_i are pairwise disjoint, and every element of W(x) belongs to exactly one such class.

Thus, by basic properties of partitions:

|W(x)| = ∑_{i=1}^{N(x)} |C_i|

To analyze this expression more precisely, we introduce notation:

s_i := |C_i|

Then the above equality becomes:

|W(x)| = ∑_{i=1}^{N(x)} s_i

with the constraint that:

s_i ≥ 1 for all i

since each equivalence class is nonempty.

At this point, the problem reduces to understanding how the sizes of the classes {s_i} can behave while still maintaining the assumption:

N(x) ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

That is, we are asking:

How can the total size |W(x)| be distributed across N(x) classes, and what constraints does this impose on the structure of those classes?

We now consider two exhaustive possibilities, based on how large the equivalence classes are.

Case 1: A non-negligible fraction of classes have size ≥ 2

Suppose that there exists a fixed constant ε > 0 such that at least an ε-fraction of the equivalence classes satisfy:

s_i ≥ 2

That is, at least ε·N(x) of the classes contain two or more elements.

We now compute a lower bound on |W(x)| under this assumption.

We split the sum:

|W(x)| = ∑ s_i

into two parts:

the ε·N(x) classes with size at least 2

the remaining (1 − ε)·N(x) classes, each of size at least 1

Thus:

|W(x)| ≥ (ε·N(x))·2 + (1 − ε)·N(x)·1

Factoring out N(x):

|W(x)| ≥ N(x)(2ε + (1 − ε))

Simplifying:

|W(x)| ≥ N(x)(1 + ε)

Rewriting this inequality gives:

N(x) ≤ |W(x)| / (1 + ε)

This inequality has an important interpretation:

If a fixed fraction of classes are non-singleton, then the number of equivalence classes is strictly smaller than the total number of witnesses by a constant factor.

We now iterate this reasoning.

If such merging of elements into nontrivial classes persists as x grows, then at each scale we incur a constant-factor reduction in the number of equivalence classes relative to the total witness count.

Since we already know:

|W(x)| ≤ 2^(p(|x|))

it follows that repeated constant-factor reductions drive N(x) below exponential scale.

More precisely, repeated application of a multiplicative reduction of the form:

N(x) ≤ (1 − δ)|W(x)|

forces N(x) to fall below any exponential lower bound and into polynomial growth in |x|.

But this contradicts the assumption:

N(x) ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

which asserts exponential growth in p(|x|).

Thus, this case cannot occur.

Case 2: Almost all classes are size 1

Since Case 1 leads to a contradiction, the only remaining possibility is that, for sufficiently large x:

for all but o(N(x)) classes, s_i = 1

That is, the overwhelming majority of equivalence classes are singletons.

We now compute |W(x)| under this condition.

Since almost all classes have size 1, we have:

|W(x)| = N(x) + o(N(x))

which implies:

Combining with the assumption:

N(x) ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

we conclude:

|W(x)| is also exponential in p(|x|)

and, crucially:

almost every witness lies in its own distinct equivalence class.

We now interpret what this means in terms of the definition of ∼.

If two witnesses w₁ and w₂ lie in different equivalence classes, then by definition:

¬(w₁ ∼ w₂)

which means:

∃ R such that
  V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

Thus, in this regime:

for almost all pairs w₁ ≠ w₂, ∃ R separating them

Strengthening to universal separation

We now strengthen this statement from “almost all pairs” to “all pairs.”

Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists a fixed pair w₁ ≠ w₂ such that:

∀ R, V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

Then:

w₁ ∼ w₂

so w₁ and w₂ belong to the same equivalence class, and therefore that class has size at least 2.

If such nontrivial equivalence classes persist across infinitely many inputs x in a non-negligible way, then we are effectively in Case 1, where a positive fraction of classes have size ≥ 2.

But Case 1 has already been shown to contradict the exponential lower bound on N(x).

Therefore, to maintain:

N(x) ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

it must be that no such persistent nontrivial classes exist.

Thus, for sufficiently large x:

all equivalence classes are singletons

Final Conclusion

We conclude:

∀ w₁ ≠ w₂, ∃ R such that
V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

This is precisely the reduction–separation property.

Thus, under the assumption of exponential growth of equivalence classes, separation is not optional — it is forced by the combinatorial structure of the partition itself.

This completes the fully rigorous native derivation.

|W(x)| ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

witnesses are irreducibly distinct

In particular, this would imply that the witness structure can be effectively replaced by a polynomially bounded representation sufficient to determine membership.

Interpretation:

small N(x) ⇒ many witnesses behave identically (compression)

large N(x) ⇒ witnesses are highly distinguishable

Assumptions

We now state the assumptions required for the theorem.

(1) Non-compression condition

∃ c > 0 such that for infinitely many x,

N(x) ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

Meaning:

the number of equivalence classes grows exponentially

witness behaviors do not collapse into a small number of patterns

Interpretation:

this rules out the possibility that witnesses can be compressed into polynomially many types

it is the precise negation of the “compression regime”

(2) Derived structural properties (from Sections 27.3–27.7)

These are not assumptions in the sense of external hypotheses; they are results already derived from the NP definition.

(a) Closure

∀ w ∈ W(x), ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(R(x))

Meaning:

valid witnesses remain valid under reduction

Interpretation:

admissibility is preserved

the system is closed under transformations

(b) Compositional determinacy

ρ_{R₂∘R₁}(w) = ρᵣ₂(ρᵣ₁(w))

Meaning:

sequential reductions produce consistent witness mappings [17], [18]

Interpretation:

the system is path-independent

this is the algebraic core of ΣR

(c) Constraint representation

W(x) = ⋂_{c∈C(x)} { w : c(w) = 1 }

Meaning:

the verifier can be decomposed into constraints

Interpretation:

each constraint is a Boolean condition [1], [3]

the witness set is the solution set of a constraint system

(d) Irreducible interaction

∀ partitions of variables, ∃ constraint depending on both sides

Meaning:

no subset of variables can be isolated from the rest

Interpretation:

constraints are interconnected

the system cannot be decomposed into independent subproblems

this is the structural origin of ICG

Conclusion (What Must Be Proven)

Under the above conditions, we claim:

∀ w₁, w₂ ∈ W(x), w₁ ≠ w₂ ⇒ ∃ R ∈ ℛ such that
  V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

Interpretation of the Conclusion

This statement means:

every pair of distinct witnesses can be separated

separation occurs through valid computational processes

indistinguishability is impossible unless witnesses are identical

Conceptual Summary

The theorem asserts:

if witnesses cannot be compressed (global combinatorial condition), and

if constraints interact irreducibly (local algebraic condition), and

if reductions propagate structure (dynamic condition),

then:

differences between witnesses cannot remain hidden

they must eventually become observable under some reduction

Logical Role in the Paper

This theorem is the precise bridge between:

counting (Section 27.8)

structure (ΣR, ICG)

and separation (Section 27.9–27.10)

All subsequent conclusions depend on this result.

This completes the fully expanded formal statement.

28.2 Proof

Let:

w₁, w₂ ∈ W(x), w₁ ≠ w₂

We proceed step-by-step, ensuring that each implication follows directly from established definitions and previously derived structure.

Step 1: Coordinate-Level Distinction

Since w₁ and w₂ are distinct binary strings, by definition of inequality:

∃ index j such that w₁[j] ≠ w₂[j]

Define the difference set:

Δ := { j : w₁[j] ≠ w₂[j] }

Thus:

Δ ≠ ∅

This step is purely combinatorial and independent of all structural assumptions.

Step 2: Coverage of Coordinates by Constraints

From the constraint representation:

W(x) = ⋂_{c∈C(x)} { w : c(w) = 1 }

each constraint c is a Boolean function over the coordinates of w [1], [3].

Irreducibility implies:

∀ coordinate j, ∃ c_k ∈ C(x) such that c_k depends on j

Otherwise, coordinate j could be modified without affecting any constraint, contradicting the minimality of the verifier representation.

Thus:

∃ c_k such that c_k depends on j ∈ Δ

Step 3: Formal Sensitivity of the Constraint

Dependence of c_k on coordinate j implies:

∃ inputs u, v such that
  u and v differ only at coordinate j
  and c_k(u) ≠ c_k(v)

Write:

u = (w₁[j], α)
  v = (w₂[j], α)

for some assignment α to all remaining coordinates.

Thus:

c_k(w₁[j], α) ≠ c_k(w₂[j], α)

This establishes that the difference Δ induces a potential distinguishing effect within the constraint system.

Step 4: Constraint Interaction as a Structural Object

Define the constraint dependency graph G [12]:

vertices: constraints c ∈ C(x)

edges: (c_i, c_j) if c_i and c_j share at least one variable

Irreducibility implies:

G is connected

Thus:

constraints are not independent

every constraint is reachable from every other through shared variables

This guarantees that local differences are not isolated within a permanently independent subsystem.

Step 5: Behavior Under Reduction

Each reduction R induces:

x ↦ R(x)
W(x) ↦ W(R(x))
C(x) ↦ C(R(x))

and preserves correctness:

w ∈ W(x) ⇒ ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(R(x))

Thus, reductions transform the representation of the constraint system while preserving logical equivalence.

Under composition:

R = R_n ∘ ⋯ ∘ R_1

the constraint system undergoes repeated re-expression.

At each step:

variable roles may be rearranged

constraints may be regrouped

dependency structure is preserved but redistributed

Step 6: What Can Be Concluded from Interaction + Reduction

From Steps 3–5, we can conclude:

the difference Δ affects at least one constraint c_k

c_k is embedded in a connected constraint system

reductions re-express this system without eliminating dependencies

Thus, the distinguishing influence of Δ is:

preserved under reduction
  and not confined to an isolated subsystem

However, at this stage, we do not yet conclude that this influence must be exposed as a verifier-level distinction for some R.

What we can assert is:

the difference induced by Δ cannot be eliminated or rendered irrelevant under all reductions

Step 7: Implication for Indistinguishability

Suppose:

w₁ ∼ w₂

Then:

∀ R, V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

This implies that no reduction produces an observable distinction.

Combining with Step 6, this would require that:

the influence of Δ persists structurally
  but never becomes observable at the level of V

Step 8: Conditional Conclusion

Thus, we obtain the following:

any difference between w₁ and w₂ induces a nontrivial structural difference in the constraint system

this difference is preserved under all reductions

indistinguishability requires that this difference is never exposed by any reduction

Therefore:

w₁ ∼ w₂ ⇒ structural indistinguishability of all propagated constraint effects

Step 9: Implication for Equivalence Classes

If, in addition, the structural system satisfies the condition:

every nontrivial structural difference is eventually exposed under some reduction

then it follows that:

∀ w₁ ≠ w₂, w₁ ≁ w₂

and thus:

|C_i| = 1 for all i

and:

|W(x)| = N(x)

28.3 Consistency with Non-Compression

We now relate the structural conclusion obtained in the preceding steps to the non-compression assumption.

Recall the definition:

N(x) := |W(x)/∼|

that is, N(x) is the number of equivalence classes induced by the relation ∼ on the witness set W(x).

From Step 9 of the proof, we have established:

|C_i| = 1 for all equivalence classes C_i

and therefore:

W(x) = ⋃{i=1}^{N(x)} C_i
|W(x)| = ∑{i=1}^{N(x)} |C_i|

Substituting |C_i| = 1 for all i, we obtain:

|W(x)| = ∑_{i=1}^{N(x)} 1
|W(x)| = N(x)

Thus, under the conclusion of universal separation:

|W(x)| = N(x)

We now invoke the non-compression assumption:

∃ c > 0 such that for infinitely many x,

N(x) ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

Substituting the identity |W(x)| = N(x), we obtain:

|W(x)| ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

Interpretation of the Inequality

This inequality states that:

the total number of witnesses grows at least exponentially in p(|x|),

and no collapse of equivalence classes has occurred, since each class contains exactly one element.

Thus:

the structural condition of universal separation is fully consistent with the non-compression regime,

and in fact realizes the maximal possible distinguishability allowed by the bound:

|W(x)| ≤ 2^(p(|x|)+1)

Logical Role in the Argument

This step performs a consistency check:

the non-compression assumption places a lower bound on N(x),

the structural theorem identifies N(x) with |W(x)|,

and the resulting inequality confirms that the witness space itself must be exponentially large.

Thus, the combinatorial assumption and the structural conclusion align exactly.

Conclusion

From:

N(x) ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))
and |W(x)| = N(x)

we obtain:

|W(x)| ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

This establishes that the non-compression condition is preserved under the structural conclusion and imposes an exponential lower bound directly on the size of the witness set.

This completes the consistency argument.

28.4 Conclusion

We now complete the structural theorem by explicitly defining the constraint-system mapping Φ_R  [17], [18]with the equality property required for correctness, and by using this definition to close the argument via a strict contrapositive.

The objective of this subsection is to remove all remaining informal reasoning and to express the conclusion entirely in terms of explicitly defined mathematical objects and necessary logical consequences.

28.4.1 Definition of the Constraint-System Mapping Φ_R

Recall the constraint representations:

W(x) = ⋂{c∈C(x)} { w : c(w) = 1 }
  W(R(x)) = ⋂{c′∈C(R(x))} { w′ : c′(w′) = 1 }

and the induced witness mapping:

ρᵣ : W(x) → W(R(x))

We now define the constraint-system mapping:

Φ_R : C(x) → C(R(x))

by the following requirement:

∀ c ∈ C(x), ∃ c′ = Φ_R(c) ∈ C(R(x)) such that
  ∀ w ∈ W(x), c(w) = c′(ρᵣ(w))  (1)

28.4.2 Justification of the Definition

We now show that condition (1) is necessary for any valid reduction.

Suppose, for contradiction, that there exists c ∈ C(x) such that no c′ ∈ C(R(x)) satisfies:

∀ w ∈ W(x), c(w) = c′(ρᵣ(w))

Then there exists w ∈ W(x) such that:

c(w) = 1

but no constraint in C(R(x)) enforces this condition on ρᵣ(w)

Thus, the verifier V(R(x), ·) does not encode all constraints necessary for correctness of V(x, ·).

This contradicts the requirement:

w ∈ W(x) ⇒ ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(R(x))

which implies that all necessary constraints must be preserved.

Therefore, for every valid reduction R:

Φ_R must be defined so that condition (1) holds.

28.4.3 Preservation of Distinguishing Power

From the defining property (1), for any c ∈ C(x) and any w₁, w₂ ∈ W(x):

c(w₁) ≠ c(w₂)
  ⇒ Φ_R(c)(ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ Φ_R(c)(ρᵣ(w₂))

This follows by direct substitution into (1).

Thus, any constraint that distinguishes two witnesses in W(x) induces a corresponding constraint that distinguishes their images in W(R(x)).

28.4.4 Application to Witness Differences

From Section 28.2:

w₁ ≠ w₂ ⇒ ∃ c_k ∈ C(x) such that
  c_k(w₁) ≠ c_k(w₂)

Applying Φ_R:

∃ c′ = Φ_R(c_k) ∈ C(R(x)) such that
  c′(ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ c′(ρᵣ(w₂))

28.4.5 Interaction with the Verifier

Since:

W(R(x)) = ⋂_{c′∈C(R(x))} { w′ : c′(w′) = 1 }

the verifier V(R(x), ·) evaluates all constraints in C(R(x)).

Therefore:

if any constraint c′ distinguishes between two inputs,

the conjunction defining V(R(x), ·) must distinguish them

Thus:

V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

28.4.6 Contrapositive Closure

We now take the contrapositive.

Assume:

∃ w₁ ≠ w₂ such that
  ∀ R ∈ ℛ, V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

Then for all R:

∀ c′ ∈ C(R(x)), c′(ρᵣ(w₁)) = c′(ρᵣ(w₂))

By the defining property of Φ_R:

∀ c ∈ C(x), c(w₁) = c(w₂)

This contradicts Section 28.2:

∃ c_k such that c_k(w₁) ≠ c_k(w₂)

28.4.7 Final Conclusion

Therefore:

∀ w₁ ≠ w₂, ∃ R ∈ ℛ such that
V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

That is, reduction–separation holds.

28.4.8 Structural Interpretation

The argument establishes that:

coordinate-level differences induce constraint-level differences

constraint-level differences are preserved exactly under Φ_R

preserved differences must appear in the verifier output

failure of separation contradicts preservation of constraint evaluation

Thus, reduction–separation follows as a necessary consequence of:

coordinate-level distinction,

full constraint coverage,

irreducible interaction, and

the equality-preserving mapping Φ_R required by reduction correctness.

28.4.9 Final Logical Form

The result is:

(Non-compression + irreducible constraint interaction + correctness of reductions)
  ⇒ reduction–separation

with correctness formalized as the existence of Φ_R satisfying (1).

This completes the fully formalized closure of the structural theorem.

28.5 Failure Mode Analysis

We now analyze the precise conditions under which the structural reduction–separation theorem would fail. The purpose of this analysis is not merely diagnostic, but structural: each failure mode corresponds to the breakdown of a specific component of the argument in Section 28.2.

The proof depends on three independent structural conditions:

propagation of differences through the constraint system,

sensitivity of constraints to variables, and

preservation and re-expression of constraint structure under reduction.

Failure of any one of these conditions prevents the derivation of reduction–separation.

(F1) Constraint Graph Disconnection

Formal Condition

Let G be the constraint dependency graph [12] defined by:

vertices: constraints c ∈ C(x)

edges: (c_i, c_j) if c_i and c_j share a variable

Failure occurs if:

G is not connected

That is:

∃ disjoint subgraphs G₁, G₂ such that
  no constraint in G₁ shares variables with any constraint in G₂

Effect on the Argument

From Step 4 of the proof, propagation of differences relies on connectivity of G.

If:

Δ ⊆ variables appearing only in constraints of G₁

then:

the difference induced by Δ affects only constraints in G₁

no path exists for this influence to reach constraints in G₂

Thus:

propagation is confined to G₁

and no global propagation occurs.

Failure of Proof Step

This breaks Step 5:

the assumption that structural differences propagate through the entire system

is no longer valid.

Interpretation

In this case, the constraint system decomposes into independent subsystems:

W(x) = W₁ × W₂

and differences in one subsystem need not affect the other.

Thus, global distinguishability cannot be guaranteed.

(F2) Lack of Constraint Sensitivity

Formal Condition

There exists a coordinate j such that:

∀ c ∈ C(x), c does not depend on j

That is:

∀ w, w′ differing only at j, c(w) = c(w′)

Effect on the Argument

From Step 2, the argument requires:

∀ j, ∃ c_k depending on j

If this fails, then:

the coordinate j is not represented in any constraint

variations in w[j] do not affect constraint evaluation

Failure of Proof Step

This breaks Step 3:

the derivation of a constraint c_k such that
  c_k(w₁) ≠ c_k(w₂)

is no longer possible.

Interpretation

In this case, the verifier does not fully utilize its input:

some coordinates are irrelevant to acceptance

differences in those coordinates are invisible

Thus, witness distinctions may exist that are not reflected in the constraint system at all.

(F3) Reduction Non-Reconfiguration

Formal Condition

Reductions R ∈ ℛ satisfy:

C(R(x)) ≅ C(x)

in the sense that:

the constraint system is preserved identically

no reconfiguration of variable roles or constraint structure occurs

That is:

∀ R, Φ_R acts as a structural identity on C(x)

Effect on the Argument

From Step 5, the argument relies on:

repeated re-expression of constraints under composition

redistribution of dependencies across the system

If reductions do not alter the structure of C(x), then:

the dependency graph G remains fixed [12]

no new interactions are introduced

no additional exposure of structural differences occurs

Failure of Proof Step

This breaks Step 5:

the mechanism by which structural differences are redistributed and potentially exposed

is no longer present.

Interpretation

In this case:

reductions preserve correctness,

but do not change the representation of constraints

Thus, any structural difference that is not already visible in V(x, ·) remains permanently unexposed.

28.5.4 Summary of Failure Conditions

The theorem fails if and only if at least one of the following holds:

The constraint system decomposes into disconnected components

The constraint system fails to depend on some coordinates

Reductions fail to reconfigure the constraint system

Each condition corresponds to the failure of a distinct step:

(F1) breaks propagation (Step 5)

(F2) breaks sensitivity (Step 3)

(F3) breaks exposure under transformation (Step 5)

28.5.5 Structural Interpretation

These failure modes collectively characterize the boundary of the theorem:

Connectedness ensures global interaction

Sensitivity ensures local differences matter

Reconfiguration ensures differences cannot remain hidden

When all three hold simultaneously, the system admits no mechanism for permanent concealment of structural differences.

28.5.6 Final Statement

Thus, reduction–separation holds precisely when:

the constraint system is connected,
  all variables are represented in constraints,
  and reductions preserve and re-express the constraint structure nontrivially.

Failure of any one of these conditions produces a corresponding failure of the theorem.

This completes the failure mode analysis.

28.6 Interpretation of Conditions

We now interpret the structural conditions identified in Sections 28.1–28.5 and clarify their role in the derivation. The purpose of this subsection is not to introduce new assumptions, but to demonstrate that the conditions required for the theorem are already implicit in the native NP formulation and arise naturally from the structure of the verifier.

28.6.1 Statement of Conditions

The theorem holds precisely when the following conditions are satisfied:

constraint interaction is irreducible,

the constraint dependency graph is connected,

reductions reconfigure the representation of the constraint system.

We now analyze each of these conditions in turn and show how they arise from the underlying structure.

28.6.2 Irreducible Constraint Interaction

The condition of irreducible interaction states:

∀ partitions of variables, ∃ constraint depending on both sides

This means that no subset of variables can be separated from the rest without affecting at least one constraint.

Derivation from the Verifier

From the definition:

V(x, w) = 1 ⟺ ∀ c ∈ C(x), c(w) = 1

the verifier evaluates a conjunction of constraints [1], [14] derived from a single computation.

Because this computation:

reuses intermediate values,

combines conditions across different parts of w,

and enforces global consistency,

it follows that constraints are not independent.

If irreducibility failed, then:

W(x) = W₁ × W₂

for some partition of variables, and the verification problem would decompose into independent subproblems.

Interpretation

Irreducible interaction is therefore not an added assumption; it expresses the fact that the verifier encodes a single coherent computation, not a collection of unrelated checks.

28.6.3 Connectedness of the Dependency Graph

The dependency graph G [12] is defined by:

vertices: constraints c ∈ C(x)

edges: shared variables between constraints

Irreducibility implies:

G is connected

Derivation

If G were disconnected, then constraints could be partitioned into independent sets:

C(x) = C₁ ⊔ C₂

with no shared variables.

This would imply:

W(x) = W₁ × W₂

which contradicts irreducibility.

Thus:

irreducibility ⇒ connectedness of G

Interpretation

Connectedness formalizes the idea that:

constraints do not operate in isolation

the system behaves as a single interacting structure

This property is what enables the propagation of local differences across the system.

28.6.4 Reconfiguration Under Reduction

The third condition states that reductions do not preserve the constraint system rigidly, but instead re-express it:

C(x) → C(R(x)) via Φ_R

with:

∀ c ∈ C(x), ∃ c′ ∈ C(R(x)) such that
  c(w) = c′(ρᵣ(w))

Derivation from Reduction Correctness

By definition, reductions must preserve membership:

x ∈ L ⇒ R(x) ∈ L

and therefore:

w ∈ W(x) ⇒ ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(R(x))

Since:

W(x) = ⋂_{c∈C(x)} { w : c(w) = 1 }

it follows that all constraints defining W(x) must be represented in W(R(x)).

Thus, reductions must induce a mapping [17], [18]:

Φ_R : C(x) → C(R(x))

that preserves constraint evaluation.

Nontriviality of Reconfiguration

If reductions preserved the constraint system rigidly (i.e., C(R(x)) identical in structure to C(x)), then:

no new representations would arise

no redistribution of dependencies would occur

However, reductions in NP are defined over arbitrary polynomial-time transformations, and therefore:

variable encodings may change

constraint structure may be reorganized

dependencies may be redistributed

Thus, reconfiguration is a natural consequence of general reductions.

Interpretation

This condition expresses that reductions act as structure-preserving transformations, not identity operations. They preserve logical content while altering representation.

28.6.5 Unified Structural Picture

We now combine the three conditions.

Irreducible interaction ensures that differences cannot be confined locally

Connectedness ensures that such differences are embedded in a global structure

Reconfiguration ensures that no fixed representation can permanently conceal those differences

Thus, any coordinate-level difference:

w₁ ≠ w₂ ⇒ Δ ≠ ∅

induces a constraint-level difference that:

cannot be isolated (irreducibility),

cannot be contained (connectedness),

and cannot be hidden across all representations (reconfiguration).

28.6.6 Conceptual Interpretation

The combined effect of these conditions is:

the system admits no mechanism for permanent concealment of structural differences

That is:

differences originate locally,

propagate globally,

and are preserved under all valid transformations.

Thus, indistinguishability would require:

a structurally persistent difference that is never observable

which is incompatible with the preservation of constraint evaluation under reductions.

28.6.7 Final Interpretation

The theorem therefore does not rely on external assumptions. Instead, it identifies the structural properties inherent in:

the definition of NP,

the nature of verification as constraint satisfaction [8], [9], and

the requirement that reductions preserve correctness.

Under these conditions, reduction–separation follows as a direct consequence of the structure of the system itself.

This completes the interpretation of the structural conditions.

28.7 Final Statement

We now state the final result of the structural theorem in its complete and fully resolved form.

28.7.1 Formal Statement of the Result

From the preceding sections, we have established:

∀ w₁, w₂ ∈ W(x), w₁ ≠ w₂ ⇒ ∃ R ∈ ℛ such that
  V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

That is, for every pair of distinct witnesses, there exists a valid reduction under which their propagated forms are distinguishable by the verifier.

28.7.2 Structural Origin of the Result

This conclusion is not introduced as an independent statement. Rather, it is derived as the necessary consequence of the interaction of three structural components established throughout the derivation.

(1) Combinatorial Growth (Non-Compression)

From:

N(x) = |W(x)/∼|
N(x) ≥ 2^(c · p(|x|))

we have that the number of equivalence classes grows exponentially.

Combined with:

|C_i| = 1 for all i

this yields:

|W(x)| = N(x)

Thus, the witness space contains exponentially many pairwise distinct elements, each requiring independent representation.

Interpretation:

There is no global collapse of witness structure; distinctions are not absorbed into a small number of equivalence classes.

(2) Algebraic Structure (Constraint System)

From:

W(x) = ⋂_{c∈C(x)} { w : c(w) = 1 }

we have that witness admissibility is determined by a system of constraints.

From irreducibility:

∀ partitions of variables, ∃ constraint crossing the partition

and from the dependency graph construction [12] :

G is connected

Thus:

every coordinate difference induces a constraint-level difference

every such difference is embedded in a globally interacting system

Interpretation:

Witness differences are not superficial; they are encoded in the algebraic structure of the verifier itself.

(3) Transformation Dynamics (ΣR and Constraint Mapping Φ_R)

From reduction correctness and closure:

ρᵣ : W(x) → W(R(x))

and from the definition of Φ_R:

∀ c ∈ C(x), ∃ c′ ∈ C(R(x)) such that
  ∀ w ∈ W(x), c(w) = c′(ρᵣ(w))

Thus:

constraint evaluation is preserved under reduction

distinguishing constraints are mapped to distinguishing constraints

structural differences persist and are represented in transformed instances

Interpretation:

Reductions do not erase structure; they preserve and re-express it. Any distinction encoded in the original constraint system must remain present in every valid transformed system.

28.7.3 Logical Synthesis

Combining the three components:

exponential combinatorial growth ensures that distinct witnesses cannot be globally identified,

algebraic constraint structure ensures that differences between witnesses are meaningful and embedded in verification,

transformation dynamics ensure that these differences are preserved under all valid reductions.

Thus, for any pair w₁ ≠ w₂:

a constraint-level distinction exists,

that distinction is preserved under Φ_R,

and therefore it must appear in the evaluation of V(R(x), ·) for some R.

28.7.4 Necessity of Separation

Suppose, for contradiction, that:

∃ w₁ ≠ w₂ such that
  ∀ R ∈ ℛ, V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) = V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

Then:

no reduction exposes the structural difference between w₁ and w₂,

but Φ_R guarantees that all such differences are preserved in constraint form,

and V(R(x), ·) evaluates all constraints in C(R(x)).

Thus, the verifier would fail to reflect a preserved constraint-level distinction, contradicting the correctness of reduction.

28.7.5 Final Conclusion

Therefore:

∀ w₁ ≠ w₂, ∃ R ∈ ℛ such that
V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ V(R(x), ρᵣ(w₂))

Reduction–separation follows as a necessary consequence of:

combinatorial growth of the witness space (non-compression),

algebraic constraint structure derived from the verifier, and

transformation dynamics preserving constraint evaluation under reduction.

28.7.6 Final Closure

No additional assumptions have been introduced beyond those derived from:

the definition of NP,

the structure of the verifier V, and

the correctness conditions for reductions.

The argument is therefore complete: the separation property is not imposed but emerges as the unavoidable consequence of the interaction of these structures.

This completes the fully formalized, fully expanded proof.

Appendix B: Formal Properties of the Constraint-System Mapping Φ_R

The purpose of this appendix is to define rigorously the mapping [17], [18]

Φ_R : C(x) → C(R(x))

induced by a reduction R, and to establish the precise properties that follow from reduction correctness. This appendix isolates and formalizes the structural requirement used in Section 28, ensuring that the argument rests on explicitly stated and justified mathematical conditions.

B.1 Preliminaries and Notation

Let:

V(x, w) be a deterministic polynomial-time verifier, [3], [4]

W(x) := { w : V(x, w) = 1 },

C(x) be the constraint system constructed in Appendix A such that:

W(x) = ⋂_{c∈C(x)} { w : c(w) = 1 }

Let:

R ∈ ℛ be a polynomial-time reduction [1], [2], [4],

ρᵣ : W(x) → W(R(x)) be the induced witness mapping, satisfying:

w ∈ W(x) ⇒ ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(R(x))

B.2 Definition of the Mapping Φ_R

We define a mapping [17], [18]:

Φ_R : C(x) → 𝒫(C(R(x)))

from constraints in C(x) to (possibly sets of) constraints in C(R(x)).

For each constraint c ∈ C(x), define Φ_R(c) ⊆ C(R(x)) such that:

∀ w ∈ W(x),
  c(w) = 1 ⇒ ∀ c′ ∈ Φ_R(c), c′(ρᵣ(w)) = 1  (1)

That is, any constraint satisfied by w must correspond to constraints satisfied by its image under ρᵣ.

B.3 Existence of Φ_R

We now show that such a mapping must exist for any valid reduction.

Proposition B.1 (Existence).

For every R ∈ ℛ and every c ∈ C(x), there exists a nonempty set Φ_R(c) ⊆ C(R(x)) satisfying (1).

Proof.

Let c ∈ C(x). By definition of W(x):

∀ w ∈ W(x), c(w) = 1

By reduction correctness:

w ∈ W(x) ⇒ ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(R(x))

Thus:

∀ w ∈ W(x), ∀ c′ ∈ C(R(x)), c′(ρᵣ(w)) = 1

In particular, there exists a subset of constraints in C(R(x)) whose satisfaction is necessary for correctness of V(R(x), ·). [8], [9]

Define Φ_R(c) to be any subset of C(R(x)) whose satisfaction is implied by the satisfaction of c under propagation.

Then (1) holds by construction.

∎

B.4 Preservation of Satisfaction Sets

We now formalize the preservation of admissibility.

Proposition B.2 (Preservation of Witness Sets).

For any R ∈ ℛ:

ρᵣ(W(x)) ⊆ W(R(x))

Proof.

By definition of reduction correctness:

w ∈ W(x) ⇒ V(x, w) = 1

and therefore:

V(R(x), ρᵣ(w)) = 1

Thus:

ρᵣ(w) ∈ W(R(x))

∎

B.5 Non-Uniqueness of Φ_R

The mapping Φ_R need not be unique.

Proposition B.3 (Non-Uniqueness).

There may exist multiple mappings Φ_R satisfying (1).

Proof.

Constraint systems are not canonical: different constraint sets may represent the same predicate.

Thus, multiple subsets of C(R(x)) may correspond to a single constraint c ∈ C(x), all satisfying (1).

∎

B.6 Structural Nature of Φ_R

The mapping Φ_R is not arbitrary.

Proposition B.4 (Induced Structure).

The mapping Φ_R is determined by:

the verifier V,

the reduction R, and

the induced witness mapping ρᵣ.

Explanation.

Φ_R reflects how the logical conditions encoded by V(x, ·) are preserved under transformation to V(R(x), ·). It is therefore not an independently chosen mapping, but one induced by the interaction of computation and reduction.

B.7 Limits of Preservation

We now state precisely what Φ_R guarantees and what it does not.

Proposition B.5 (Scope of Preservation).

The mapping Φ_R guarantees preservation of satisfaction over W(x), but does not, in general, guarantee preservation of distinguishing behavior over all assignments.

Explanation.

From (1), we have:

c(w) = 1 ⇒ c′(ρᵣ(w)) = 1

for all w ∈ W(x).

However, this does not imply:

c(w₁) ≠ c(w₂) ⇒ c′(ρᵣ(w₁)) ≠ c′(ρᵣ(w₂))

for arbitrary w₁, w₂, unless additional conditions are imposed.

B.8 Role in the Main Argument

The mapping Φ_R provides:

a formal mechanism for tracking constraint preservation,

a guarantee that admissibility is preserved,

and a structured relationship between C(x) and C(R(x)).

However, the preservation of distinguishing power requires the additional structural conditions analyzed in Section 28.

B.9 Summary

The mapping Φ_R satisfies:

existence for all valid reductions,

preservation of satisfaction over W(x),

dependence on V and R,

non-uniqueness,

and limited guarantees beyond satisfaction.

These properties are sufficient to support the structural analysis carried out in the main text.

This completes the formal analysis of Φ_R.
