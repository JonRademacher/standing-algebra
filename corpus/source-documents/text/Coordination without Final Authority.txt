Coordination without Final Authority: Structural Axioms of Pluralist Legitimacy

Abstract

How can political authority be legitimate in societies whose members disagree about religion, morality, and the ultimate grounds of political obligation? This paper develops a structural answer to that question. It argues that legitimacy in pluralist societies depends upon two conditions: Pluralistic Inclusion, under which every person persistently governed by a social order possesses effective standing or a realizable pathway to standing, and Symmetry of Standing, under which no participant possesses final and uncontrolled authority over the standing of another. These principles are presented as necessary conditions for legitimate coordination among persons who reject a common final authority.

The paper further argues that inclusion and symmetry serve not only moral functions but also structural ones. They preserve information flow, feedback, contestation, and repair, thereby supporting institutional learning and adaptation over time. Conversely, exclusion and domination generate legitimacy deficits by weakening the mechanisms through which authority remains accountable to those subject to it. The resulting framework reframes legitimacy as a property of the relational architecture through which authority is exercised, challenged, revised, and reproduced under conditions of persistent pluralism.

Keywords: legitimacy; pluralism; standing; political authority; non-domination; democratic theory

Introduction

Modern pluralist societies are characterized by persistent disagreement concerning religion, morality, politics, and the nature of the good life. Citizens may share legal institutions and common political problems while rejecting one another's deepest moral and philosophical commitments. Under such conditions, the justification of political authority becomes increasingly difficult. If no comprehensive doctrine commands universal acceptance, on what basis can collectively binding decisions claim legitimacy?

The challenge is not merely to reach decisions, but to explain why those decisions should possess authority over those who reject the premises from which they were derived. This paper approaches the problem from a different direction. Rather than beginning with the substantive content of political doctrines, it asks what structural conditions must exist before any claim to authority can become legitimate under conditions of pluralism. The central proposal is that legitimacy depends upon two foundational requirements.

The first is Pluralistic Inclusion. Every person persistently governed by a social order must possess recognized standing within that order, or an effective pathway through which such standing can be acquired and exercised. No person may be reduced to a permanent political nullity. The second is Symmetry of Standing. Participants recognized within a social order must relate to one another as bearers of equal basic standing. Differences in expertise, office, responsibility, or delegated authority may be legitimate, but no participant may possess final and uncontrolled authority over the standing of another.

Together, these principles constitute what will be called the Axiom of Pluralistic Inclusion and the Axiom of Symmetry. The argument advanced here is that these axioms identify necessary structural conditions for pluralist legitimacy. A social order that excludes affected persons from participation, or that permits some participants to exercise unanswerable authority over others, cannot reproduce its authority through reciprocal recognition alone. Instead, it becomes increasingly dependent upon coercion, dependency, exclusion, or other mechanisms external to shared authorization.

The paper further argues that inclusion and symmetry serve not only moral purposes but also important structural functions. They preserve the informational and corrective capacities through which institutions receive feedback, process contestation, identify errors, and adapt to changing conditions. Legitimacy should therefore be understood not merely as a static property of institutions, but as a dynamic feature of systems capable of reproducing authority through continuing participation, accountability, and repair.

The resulting theory does not aspire to provide a complete account of justice, nor does it identify a single ideal institutional form. Its aim is more modest. It seeks to identify the minimum relational conditions under which persons who disagree about ultimate truth can nevertheless participate in a shared order of collectively binding action. In a pluralist society, legitimacy begins not with agreement about what is ultimately true, but with the preservation of standing among those who must act together despite that disagreement.

The Problem of Pluralism and Legitimacy

By a pluralist society, we mean a society in which individuals and groups hold irreducibly diverse religious, moral, philosophical, cultural, and political commitments, such that no single comprehensive doctrine can reasonably be expected to command the acceptance of every person subject to the society’s institutions. Pluralism in this sense is not merely the temporary presence of disagreement that might disappear if participants acquired better information or reasoned more carefully. Nor is it reducible to a superficial variety of preferences within an otherwise unified moral framework. It describes a condition in which persistent disagreement arises among persons who may nevertheless be sincere, informed, and capable of reasoning. Their disagreements reach questions concerning the nature of the good, the demands of justice, the sources of moral obligation, the proper limits of political power, and the forms of life worth pursuing. (Rawls 1993; Waldron 1999)

Modern constitutional democracies are paradigmatic, though imperfect, instances of this condition. They encompass multiple religions, secular and nonsecular moral outlooks, competing conceptions of freedom and equality, and divergent understandings of personal and collective flourishing. No participant can simply assume that the deepest premises of their own worldview are shared by everyone else. This diversity is one of the central conditions under which that order must operate. Under these circumstances, the traditional aspiration to locate political authority in a universally acknowledged source becomes difficult to sustain. The difficulty is not necessarily that truth does not exist. It is that an assertion of truth does not, by itself, establish the standing of one person or institution to direct the conduct of another.

This condition creates a problem that is simultaneously normative and structural. Laws must be enacted, institutions maintained, resources distributed, conflicts adjudicated, and threats addressed. Collective decisions cannot always be postponed until unanimity is achieved. Yet such decisions are ordinarily backed by coercive institutions. They determine what persons may do, what they must contribute, what risks they must bear, and what penalties they may suffer for noncompliance. The central question is therefore not merely how disagreement can be managed efficiently. It is how a decision can acquire a legitimate claim to bind persons who reject the moral, religious, or philosophical premises favored by those who made it.

The problem can be stated more precisely:

How can a community make and enforce collectively binding decisions when its members disagree about the ultimate grounds of political obligation, without treating the power of one participant or faction as a final authority over the others?

This question contains two distinguishable problems. The first is the problem of decision: how can a community reach a determinate outcome when its participants disagree? The second is the problem of authorization: why should the outcome have the standing to bind participants who opposed it? A procedure may solve the first problem without solving the second. (Estlund 2008; Christiano 2008) Dictatorial command, inherited hierarchy, or simple majoritarianism can produce determinate decisions. Determinacy alone, however, does not establish legitimacy. A rule may settle what will happen while leaving unresolved why those subjected to it ought to recognize the decision as authoritative rather than merely coercive.

Stability secured by fear, dependency, exclusion, or overwhelming force may be empirically durable while remaining normatively defective. Legitimacy concerns the structure of the relationship through which persons are governed, not simply the absence of visible conflict. The challenge of pluralism is therefore not to eliminate disagreement (Rawls 1993, Waldron 1999) but to identify conditions under which disagreement can persist without converting political coordination into relations of final subordination. A legitimate pluralist order must be capable of generating common action while leaving participants recognizable as distinct sources of claims, reasons, objections, and demands for revision.

3.1 Consent and the Social-Contract Response

The social-contract tradition offered one of the most influential responses to this problem. Although its principal authors differed profoundly, its central insight was that political authority cannot be justified merely by the superior force, inherited status, or natural dominion of rulers. Rousseau’s claim that force does not create right expresses the basic distinction between possessing the ability to compel and possessing the authority to command (Rousseau 1997). If no person has an inherent title to rule another, political authority must arise through some form of agreement, authorization, or convention among those who will be subject to it. The social contract accordingly shifts the source of authority from the ruler’s identity to the relationship among participants. Political power is legitimate not because the ruler is naturally superior, but because those governed can be represented as authors or authorizers of the political order. (Rousseau 1997; Raz 1986)

Yet consent does not fully resolve the pluralist legitimacy problem. (Raz 1986; Estlund 2008) If the model already describes persons as free and equal, excludes certain forms of bargaining power, or specifies what information is unavailable to them, it may encode substantive commitments that have not yet been independently justified. This does not make consent irrelevant. It shows that consent cannot be the most fundamental concept in a theory of pluralist legitimacy. The structure within which consent is sought must therefore satisfy conditions that make authorization possible.

3.2 Political Liberalism and Public Reason

Rawls’s political liberalism addresses the persistence of reasonable disagreement more directly. Rather than seeking agreement on a comprehensive religious or philosophical doctrine, Rawls asks how citizens divided by reasonable pluralism can endorse a shared political conception of justice. Political principles must be capable of presentation through public reasons that do not require citizens to abandon their deeper commitments. Citizens may support the same constitutional essentials for different reasons, generating an overlapping consensus rather than a unified worldview (Rawls 1993).

Legitimate political power cannot depend on the triumph of one comprehensive doctrine. It must be exercised within terms of cooperation that citizens regarded as free and equal can reasonably accept despite their broader disagreements. Political justification is consequently constrained by reciprocity. (Rawls 1993; Rawls 1999) Those who exercise coercive power must be prepared to offer reasons whose authority does not depend exclusively on premises that other citizens are structurally required to reject.

Rawls’ framework, however, begins from a conception of citizens as free and equal participants in a fair system of cooperation. (Rawls 1993) That conception has considerable normative appeal, but it also raises a foundational question. Why must persons be related as free and equal participants before political justification can begin? Political liberalism supplies a normative account of this status, but the possibility remains that freedom and equality perform an even more basic structural role. They may describe not only how citizens ought morally to be regarded, but also the relational conditions under which political justification can count as justification among plural agents at all.

A person whose objections can be ignored without recourse is not participating in public justification on equal terms. (Forst 2012) A person who lacks an effective route to contest the rules governing political argument is not situated as a co-author of those rules. Likewise, an institution that reserves the final power to determine which reasons count as public, while remaining unanswerable to those excluded by that determination, risks recreating final authority inside the practice of public reason. The question is therefore not only whether public reasons are offered, but whether the institutional structure gives affected persons standing to challenge, revise, and contest the terms under which reasons are evaluated.

The present theory accepts Rawls’s recognition that legitimate coercion must be publicly justifiable under conditions of reasonable pluralism. It asks what must be structurally true of a political relationship before such justification can operate. The proposed axioms of inclusion and symmetry are intended to identify those antecedent conditions.

3.3 Discourse, Participation, and Democratic Authorization

Habermas relocates legitimacy from hypothetical agreement to the procedures through which citizens participate in the formation of law. (Habermas 1996) On a discourse-theoretic account, legitimate norms are connected to processes of public deliberation in which those affected can participate as free and equal interlocutors. (Habermas 1996; Cohen 1997) Political autonomy is not exhausted by private freedom from interference. It also requires public participation in the communicative processes through which collectively binding rules are made (Habermas 1996). Rawls and Habermas consequently offer distinct but related accounts of how coercive law might be justified among persons who cannot rely on a common comprehensive doctrine. Rawls emphasizes public reason and an overlapping political consensus, while Habermas emphasizes inclusive deliberation and democratic will-formation.

The discourse-theoretic approach brings the relational character of legitimacy into clearer focus. Legitimacy cannot be reduced to the independently correct content of a decision. (Cohen 1997; Mansbridge et al. 2012) It is connected to whether those affected could participate in testing the reasons for the norm, presenting objections, introducing relevant information, and seeking revision. A procedurally closed but substantively benevolent authority remains defective because the governed appear only as recipients of decisions rather than as participants in their formation.

Nevertheless, ideal discourse conditions also rely on assumptions about equal participation. The structural question therefore reappears. What distinguishes genuine participation from the symbolic presence of participants whose interventions cannot affect the coordination process? What prevents a deliberative institution from hearing objections while remaining structurally insulated from them? A theory of discourse may identify the norms governing legitimate deliberation, but an account of standing is needed to explain when a participant’s contribution constitutes a claim that the institution is obligated to receive, evaluate, and answer.

3.4 Non-Domination and the Problem of Uncontrolled Power

Republican theories of freedom, especially Pettit’s account of freedom as non-domination, identify another dimension of the problem. A person may be unfree even when power is not presently being exercised against them. If another possesses an uncontrolled capacity to interfere arbitrarily in the person’s choices, the relationship is one of domination even when the dominant party behaves benevolently or refrains from interference (Pettit 1997; Pettit 2012). What matters is not only the frequency of interference but also the structure through which one agent remains dependent on another’s unchecked will. This insight is essential to pluralist legitimacy. Benevolent rule does not cease to be domination merely because the ruler chooses favorable outcomes. (Pettit 1997; Shapiro 2012) Nor does an excluded population gain equal status merely because those who govern it occasionally consider its interests.

Non-domination therefore moves beyond the narrow question of whether actual decisions are harmful. It asks whether power is controlled by those subject to it. (Pettit 2012) Domination disrupts pluralist legitimacy because it assigns final authority to one participant’s frame, judgment, or will while denying another participant a comparably effective capacity to contest the relationship. The defect lies not merely in unequal influence. The defect lies in an asymmetry of fundamental standing through which one party can determine the conditions governing another without remaining accountable within a shared structure of challenge and repair. The account must nevertheless distinguish domination from functionally differentiated authority. Expertise, office, and delegated jurisdiction may justify unequal decisional roles without establishing unequal basic standing. This distinction between functional and final authority will be developed in Section 6.

3.5 From Existing Theories to Structural Conditions

Consent, public reason, democratic discourse, and non-domination each express part of this underlying structure. Yet these theories often begin by describing persons as free and equal, specifying fair procedures, or presupposing a community within which participants already possess the status required for authorization. The prior structural problem receives less direct attention: what conditions must obtain for a plurality of agents to constitute a legitimate field of coordination in the first place?

This paper argues that two such conditions can be identified. The first is pluralistic inclusion. The second is symmetry of standing. These conditions are described as structural because they concern relations among participants rather than the substantive content of any particular doctrine. The relevant question is not initially whether the institution’s objectives are morally correct, but whether it coordinates persons through relations in which every affected participant is included as a bearer of claims and no participant possesses final, unanswerable control over the others.

The argument is conditional but not trivial. If an order claims legitimacy among plural agents, it must treat them as participants whose distinct reasons and objections remain relevant to collective authorization. An order that permanently excludes some affected persons cannot claim to coordinate with them. It acts upon them. An order that grants one participant final authority over the standing of others does not preserve plurality within coordination. It replaces plural coordination with subordination.

The absence of final authority does not mean that no decision may ever be final for practical purposes. The relevant distinction is between decision closure and authority closure. Decision closure ends a particular deliberative process so that coordinated action can occur. Authority closure insulates the decision-maker or governing framework from further contestation by denying the standing of those subject to it. Legitimacy under pluralism should therefore be understood as an architecture of continuing contestability. For a decision to remain part of a legitimate order, the persons bound by it must retain recognized pathways through which they can challenge its application, contest its justification, seek allies, present new information, and pursue revision.

This quality distinguishes legitimate coordination from final authority. Final authority claims not only the power to decide but also the power to determine unilaterally whether challenges to that decision are admissible. It controls both the outcome and the standing of those who might contest it. Pluralist legitimacy rejects this closure because it would allow one participant to extinguish the relational conditions through which authority could be justified.

The central thesis can now be stated precisely: in a society characterized by persistent disagreement concerning ultimate moral, religious, and political authority, collectively binding decisions can claim pluralist legitimacy only when every person subject to the coordination order possesses non-null standing within it and when no participant possesses final, uncontrolled authority over the standing of another. These conditions do not eliminate disagreement. They establish the structural field within which disagreement can persist without becoming domination.

4. Standing as the Basis of Legitimacy

Before any directive can claim legitimate authority, a more basic question must be answered:

Who possesses the standing to make, contest, authorize, or resist the claims through which collective coordination occurs?

In ordinary usage, to have standing is to occupy a recognized position from which one may properly speak, object, demand an answer, or initiate a process of judgment. Legal standing provides the most familiar example. Political and moral standing refers to an agent’s recognized capacity to introduce claims into a shared normative order and to require that those claims receive institutionally appropriate consideration. To possess standing is not necessarily to control the resulting decision. It is to count as a participant whose agency, interests, objections, and exposure to consequences cannot permissibly be treated as irrelevant. Standing identifies the conditions under which the order must encounter that participant not merely as an object to be managed, protected, directed, or restrained, but as a source of claims bearing upon the legitimacy of collective action.

This distinction is foundational. A person may be visible to an institution without possessing standing within it. An authority may survey a population, solicit public comments, or permit representatives to speak while reserving an uncontrolled discretion to disregard every resulting claim. Consultation becomes standing-bearing only when the contribution of the affected party enters the coordination process as something the institution is obligated to receive and address under publicly ascertainable conditions. Recognition without consequence may be ceremonial. Standing requires that a claim possess normative and institutional significance.(Forst 2012; Young 2000)

Standing should therefore be distinguished from decisional supremacy. To possess standing is not to possess the unilateral power to determine the outcome. Institutions may impose relevance, evidence, jurisdictional, and procedural conditions on claims, but those conditions must themselves remain contestable. An authority cannot establish legitimacy merely by defining every challenge to its own authority as inadmissible.

This distinction allows pluralist legitimacy to accommodate disagreement. Equal standing does not require that both claims be granted or that the conflict disappear. It requires that neither claimant’s position be rejected by denying their status as a participant or by declaring the other participant’s worldview intrinsically authoritative. The coordination order must resolve the incompatibility through procedures and reasons that preserve the standing of both parties even when only one substantive outcome can be adopted. If standing depended entirely on actual interpersonal recognition, the refusal of a dominant faction to recognize a minority could itself extinguish the minority’s standing.

A theory that equates standing with power would convert domination into its own justification. A theory that treats standing as entirely independent of institutional power, however, risks reducing it to an abstract moral status without practical consequences. Effective standing must therefore connect normative recognition to institutional capacity. An agent possesses more than nominal standing only when the coordination order provides a realizable pathway through which the agent’s claim can enter, challenge, and potentially alter institutional action.

This gives rise to a distinction between formal standing and effective standing. Formal standing exists when the rules declare an agent eligible to participate or object. Effective standing exists when the surrounding institutional conditions make the exercise of that status practically available. 4.3 Standing, Consent, and Membership

Standing is logically prior to consent because consent cannot authorize a political relationship until the parties entitled to consent have been identified. (Theuns 2021) Any account of authorization must answer at least four preliminary questions: whose consent is necessary, what information must be available, what alternatives must remain accessible, and under what conditions refusal counts as valid. These are standing questions before they are consent questions.

If an institution determines unilaterally that only property owners, citizens of a preferred class, members of a particular religion, or persons endorsing an official doctrine may authorize its decisions, it has already distributed standing before requesting consent. The resulting consent may be unanimous among the selected participants while excluding many of those governed by the outcome. Representation, delegation, advocacy, guardianship, and institutional mediation can carry claims into the coordination process. 4.5 Standing as a Threshold Condition of Legitimacy

Standing functions as a threshold condition because substantive evaluation cannot compensate for the complete exclusion of affected participants from the structure of authorization. This qualification is essential. If standing were treated as sufficient, any decision produced through inclusive procedures would have to be accepted as legitimate regardless of its substance. Such a conclusion would leave no basis for condemning decisions that preserve procedural participation while violating basic rights or imposing substantively impermissible burdens. The absence of a standing failure means only that the decision has passed one necessary threshold. Additional requirements concerning rights, justification, proportionality, competence, legality, and substantive justice may still apply. This persistence is crucial because majority rule becomes domination if the majority may use an electoral victory to extinguish the future standing of its opponents.

A legitimate decision therefore binds without annihilating the claim-bearing status of those who opposed it. It closes a particular decision while preserving the broader architecture within which the decision can later be contested, interpreted, amended, or replaced. Standing is what allows losers to remain participants rather than becoming subjects of the winners. Standing supplies the common foundation of the two axioms developed below. Pluralistic Inclusion prohibits the permanent nullification of persons subject to a coordination order. Symmetry prohibits the conversion of differentiated authority into final control over another participant’s standing. Inclusion therefore concerns entry into the field of legitimate coordination; symmetry concerns the relations maintained within it.

Axiom 1: Pluralistic Inclusion (The Successor Axiom)

The first axiom concerns the boundary of the political community. Before a social order can determine how authority should be distributed, how collective decisions should be made, or which substantive rights should be protected, it must determine who counts as a participant in the practices through which those questions are answered. Every political order therefore contains, whether explicitly or implicitly, a rule of inclusion. It identifies the persons whose claims must be heard, whose interests must be considered, whose injuries demand a response, and whose authorization is relevant to the exercise of collective power.

This boundary-setting function is not administratively neutral. To exclude an individual from the community of standing is not merely to give that person less influence over a particular decision. It is to deny that the person occupies the kind of position from which an institution can be obligated to receive, evaluate, or answer their claims. The excluded person may remain physically present within the territory, economically useful to its institutions, legally controlled by its rules, and vulnerable to its sanctions while nevertheless being absent from the authorized structure of political justification.

The Axiom of Pluralistic Inclusion prohibits this condition. It holds that no person who is persistently subject to the consequences, directives, or coercive capacities of a social order may be permanently constituted as a nullity within that order. Every such person must possess recognized standing or an effective institutional pathway through which standing can be acquired, exercised, and defended. 6.3 Functional Authority versus Final Authority

6.5 Repair and Equilibrium

Repair is the organized process through which a social order identifies an asymmetry of standing, interrupts the mechanisms reproducing it, and restores the affected participants to a relationship of reciprocal claim-bearing status. The objective is to remove the structural superiority through which one participant can determine another’s governing conditions without reciprocal accountability. The target of repair is not necessarily equality of wealth, authority, expertise, or social influence in every respect. It is restoration of the conditions necessary for equal standing. Repair must therefore be directed at the specific mechanisms producing the asymmetry.

Repair must target the mechanism producing the asymmetry, whether exclusion, inaccessible review, concentrated authority, or inherited barriers to effective standing. A pluralist order must preserve internal pathways through which formal or effective asymmetries can be identified and corrected. Repair may require differentiated measures, but it must restore equal standing rather than relocate final authority. A formally available but prohibitively costly exit does not convert domination into consent. The availability of internal repair is therefore crucial. Persons should not be forced to choose between submission and the destruction of their social position. A legitimate coordination order must permit them to challenge domination while preserving their standing as members. The complete Axiom of Symmetry can now be restated.

Axiom of Symmetry

The refined symmetry condition therefore permits differentiated authority where that authority remains bounded, contestable, reviewable, and incapable of determining unilaterally whether another participant counts. The central demand of the axiom can therefore be expressed concisely:

Structural Dynamics of Legitimacy

The preceding axioms identify the minimum conditions under which persons can participate in a pluralist order as recognized and equal bearers of claims. Yet the significance of those axioms extends beyond questions of moral status or political fairness. Inclusion and symmetry are not merely normative requirements imposed upon an otherwise neutral institutional landscape. They are also structural conditions that shape how information flows, how conflicts are processed, how errors are corrected, and how institutions adapt over time.

The present theory therefore proposes a structural dynamics of legitimacy. The objective is not to reduce political life to physics, nor to claim that social systems obey deterministic natural laws in the manner of physical objects. The language of "social physics" should therefore be understood as heuristic rather than literal. It directs attention toward invariant relational properties that influence a system's capacity for coordination. These constraints do not determine unique outcomes, but they shape the range of outcomes that remain sustainably achievable.

The central claim of this section is that legitimacy possesses a dynamic dimension. It is a property of the processes through which institutions receive information, correct mistakes, maintain accountability, and reproduce authority across time. Inclusion and symmetry are important because they create the conditions under which a pluralist order can continue learning from those it governs.

7.1 Information, Feedback, and Institutional Learning

No authority possesses perfect information concerning the needs, experiences, preferences, injuries, and aspirations of all participants. (Young 2000; Warren 2017) Institutions therefore depend upon mechanisms through which relevant information can be gathered, interpreted, and incorporated into collective decision-making. Information, however, does not appear spontaneously. It emerges through participants who occupy different roles, experience different consequences, and observe different aspects of social reality. A population dispersed across diverse experiences necessarily contains information unavailable to any single participant or authority. (Young 2000; Mansbridge et al. 2012)

Domination distinguishes received information from corrective feedback. An authority may gather information from governed persons while retaining complete discretion over whether their claims can initiate reconsideration or review. Such arrangements narrow the institution’s epistemic field, weaken error correction, encourage strategic compliance, and displace contestation outside ordinary institutional channels.

The Axiom of Pluralistic Inclusion affects this informational architecture directly. When persons are excluded from standing, their experiences may still generate consequences, but those consequences lose an authorized pathway into institutional awareness. The institution continues to act upon the affected population while simultaneously reducing its ability to learn from that population. The institution may continue receiving partial information concerning the excluded participant through administrative observation or third-party reporting. Yet such information differs fundamentally from information carried by standing. The difference is significant because a claim contains not only data but interpretation. It presents the participant as an independent source of meaning concerning the consequences of institutional action.

Feedback exists when information concerning institutional consequences can return to decision-making structures in a form capable of influencing subsequent decisions. (Mansbridge et al. 2012; Warren 2017) The importance of symmetry becomes evident at this stage. As feedback deficiency increases, institutions become progressively less capable of learning from their own consequences. Errors may remain uncorrected not because no one notices them, but because those who notice them lack standing sufficient to force institutional attention. The practical result is organizational blindness.

7.2 Contestation and Legitimacy Deficits

One fundamental requirement for evaluating pluralist societies is contestability. (Pettit 2012; Lafont 2020) The relevant question is therefore not whether contestation exists. The relevant question is whether contestation possesses authorized pathways. A society with high contestation capacity permits participants to challenge decisions while preserving their standing within the political order. A society with low contestation capacity increasingly forces opposition outside institutional channels.

When institutions reduce legitimate avenues for challenge, they simultaneously reduce their capacity for internal correction. Contestability therefore serves a stabilizing role. Conflict routed through recognized procedures remains part of a shared political order. (Habermas 1996; Cohen 1997) Conflict denied such pathways is more likely to appear as withdrawal, mistrust, non-compliance, institutional disengagement, or open confrontation. The interaction of exclusion, asymmetry, information loss, and feedback failure generates what may be termed a legitimacy deficit. A legitimacy deficit is a structural mismatch between the authority exercised and the authority that can be justified through the relationships constituting the order. (O’Neill 2002; Acemoglu and Robinson 2012) The deficit concerns an underlying structural condition rather than any single observable outcome.

7.3 Adaptation Without Final Authority

The ultimate importance of inclusion and symmetry lies in their relationship to institutional adaptation. Institutional adaptation depends directly upon the structural conditions established in the previous sections. This insight allows us to distinguish between mere persistence and legitimately reproduced stability. Persistence means only that an institution continues to exist. Legitimately reproduced stability occurs where institutional continuity is sustained through processes in which affected participants retain standing to inform, contest, and revise authority. Inclusion preserves entry into the political field. Symmetry preserves equal standing within that field. Together they generate the informational, corrective, and adaptive capacities through which a pluralist society can govern itself without appealing to a final authority standing above the participants themselves.

Objections and Replies

The theory should not be defended by denying institutional complexity, historical variation, or the need for authoritative decision-making. Nor should it be defended by treating every asymmetry as domination, every disagreement as exclusion, or every failure of consensus as illegitimacy. Its narrower claim is that legitimate authority cannot permanently extinguish the standing of affected persons or reserve to one participant the uncontrolled power to determine whether another participant counts. The objections considered below help clarify that claim and establish its proper scope.

8.1 Objection One: Stable Hierarchies Exist

Monarchies, empires, religious establishments, military organizations, corporations, bureaucracies, and patriarchal households have often exhibited durable asymmetries of authority. Some have survived across generations or centuries. If the theory predicts that asymmetry necessarily produces instability or collapse, the historical record appears to falsify it. The structural theory does not identify legitimacy with mere persistence. It distinguishes at least three properties:

Durability refers to the continued existence of an institutional arrangement across time. Structural stability refers to the order’s capacity to reproduce coordination, integrate relevant feedback, correct errors, and respond to changing conditions without progressively increasing its dependence on exclusion or uncontrolled coercion. Legitimacy refers to the standing-preserving authority of the order’s decisions over those subject to them. A system may possess one of these properties without possessing the others. This deficit may produce different historical outcomes. The realization mechanism varies across domains, but the invariant relation remains: uncontrolled asymmetry weakens the order’s claim to coordinate through reciprocal standing. The theory does not require every hierarchy to generate the same observable consequence. What must remain invariant is the distinction between bounded functional differentiation and uncontrolled authority over another participant’s basic standing. The concrete manifestation of a legitimacy deficit may differ, but the underlying structural failure remains identifiable. Historical endurance is evidence of reproductive capacity, not proof of standing-preserving authorization.

Yet emergency authority remains symmetry-compatible only when its scope, duration, purpose, and conditions of review are bounded. An emergency cannot legitimately become a self-renewing basis through which the institution exercising extraordinary power determines unilaterally whether the emergency continues and whether its actions may be challenged. The general condition is:

Here, denotes legitimate differentiated authority. The objection therefore identifies a necessary clarification. Symmetry requires that differentiation remain answerable to the standing of those affected. Expertise can justify bounded authority within a domain. It cannot justify unrestricted authority over the persons to whom that expertise is applied.

8.3 Objection Three: Equality Is Not Universally Valued

The third objection challenges the purported pluralist neutrality of the theory. By making symmetry a condition of legitimacy, the theory appears to privilege an egalitarian liberal doctrine over rival comprehensive views. It therefore seems to introduce the kind of final normative authority that it claims to avoid. The reply begins by distinguishing the claim that e from the claim that symmetry of standing is structurally necessary for pluralist legitimacy. The theory does not assert the first proposition. It asserts the second proposition as a condition of a specific kind of authority: authority that claims to bind plural agents without subordinating them to a final worldview.

A participant may believe that members of group ought to govern others. The mere existence of that belief is compatible with sociological pluralism. What the participant cannot do is invoke pluralist legitimacy while reserving to their own doctrine the final authority to determine whether dissenters possess standing. The hierarchical doctrine may claim authority through revelation, tradition, natural order, conquest, or superior virtue. It cannot simultaneously claim to coordinate a plurality of agents as mutually recognized participants while denying that some of them count as participants. The argument is conditional:

If an order claims pluralist legitimacy, symmetry of basic standing follows from the form of that claim. The order may reject the condition, but it must then abandon the assertion that its authority arises from coordination among participants who remain distinct sources of claims. It instead asserts a non-pluralist ground of authority.

This establishes an incompatibility between two architectures of authority.. Suppose authority  claims the right to govern participant  while denying  any standing to challenge the doctrine on which that right rests. The conclusion is secured only by excluding the governed party from the process through which the conclusion is authorized. The theory calls this a standing closure. The authority may still compel compliance, but it cannot derive legitimacy from reciprocal coordination because reciprocity has been denied at the foundational level.

The symmetry principle is therefore not neutral among all conceivable political orders. (Forst 2012) Symmetry requires only that no participant’s comprehensive doctrine acquire final control over whether other affected participants are eligible to contest its political application. The objection thus exposes an important limit of the theory’s neutrality. It allows a wide range of substantive doctrines to participate in collective coordination, but it does not permit any doctrine to extinguish the standing of those who reject it.

8.4 Objection Four: The Theory Is Merely Procedural

The fourth objection contends that inclusion, participation, contestability, and symmetrical standing concern only the procedure through which political decisions are made. A procedure can include everyone and still produce gravely unjust outcomes. If the theory evaluates legitimacy primarily through standing, it may be unable to condemn substantively unjust decisions that emerge from formally inclusive procedures.

This objection draws attention to a familiar criticism of purely procedural accounts of justice. Rawls’s theory, for example, does not merely endorse whatever outcome emerges from an actual vote. It places basic liberties and principles of justice before ordinary calculations of social advantage, partly to prevent aggregate benefits from overriding the separateness and claims of individual persons. (Rawls 1971) Scholarship on Rawls has also emphasized that equal political liberty and its fair value require more than an empty formal entitlement to participate. (Krishnamurthy 2013) This structural theory accepts the core of this objection. Inclusion and symmetry are not sufficient conditions of substantive justice, and the theory should not claim otherwise. An inclusive and symmetrical process can make mistakes, act on false information, adopt disproportionate measures, or violate independent moral constraints. The two axioms establish necessary conditions for pluralist legitimacy, not a complete account of justice.

The acceptance of this limitation does not reduce the axioms to insignificant procedural preferences. First, the conditions concern more than procedures in a narrow sense. Second, the axioms constrain outcomes as well as decision-making procedures. Third, substantive injustice frequently operates through standing failures. Nevertheless, some substantive wrongs may not be fully reducible to inclusion or symmetry. The present theory develops the first two terms without claiming that they exhaust them. The objection therefore establishes an important boundary. The structural axioms provide a threshold theory of legitimacy, not a complete moral theory. It permits the inquiry to continue.

Conclusion

Pluralism presents a distinctive challenge for political legitimacy. In societies characterized by persistent disagreement concerning religion, morality, philosophy, and the common good, no single doctrine can be assumed to possess uncontested authority over all participants. Yet collective action remains necessary. The central question explored in this paper has therefore been straightforward but fundamental: how can a society generate legitimately binding coordination among persons who do not agree on the ultimate foundations of authority?

The argument developed here has proposed that any answer to this question must begin not with the substance of particular political doctrines but with the structure of the relationships through which authority is exercised. Legitimacy, on this view, is not merely a property of outcomes. It is a property of the relational architecture through which outcomes are produced, challenged, revised, and maintained. To clarify this architecture, the paper introduced two foundational axioms.

Inclusion identifies who must appear within the field of coordination. Symmetry governs how those participants may relate to one another once they have entered that field. Inclusion without symmetry permits formally recognized but subordinate populations. Symmetry without inclusion merely equalizes the status of those already admitted while leaving others outside the political community altogether. Legitimate pluralist coordination emerges only when every affected participant is recognized and no participant occupies a position of unanswerable superiority.

Rather than treating legitimacy as a static property possessed or lacking at a single moment, the paper argued that legitimacy is connected to the ongoing capacity of institutions to process information, receive feedback, accommodate contestation, and repair asymmetries of standing. Exclusion restricts informational access. Domination weakens corrective feedback. Both conditions generate legitimacy deficits by creating a divergence between the authority exercised by institutions and the authority that can be justified through reciprocal participation. Such deficits need not immediately produce institutional collapse, but they diminish a society's capacity for self-correction and increase its dependence upon coercion, dependency, or other nonreciprocal mechanisms of compliance. The axioms do not prohibit differentiated authority, and they do not predict the immediate collapse of every illegitimate institution. Their narrower claim is that exclusion and final domination prevent authority from being reproduced through the reciprocal standing of those subject to it.

The theory is intended neither as a moral metaphysics nor as a deterministic science of history. Its purpose is more modest and more foundational. It seeks to identify the minimum relational conditions under which persons who lack a common final authority can nevertheless participate in a shared political order without being reduced either to subjects of domination or to objects of administration. Future work can extend where the axioms developed here invite application questions and raise further theoretical questions. Nevertheless, the core conclusion of this paper can be stated simply.

A pluralist society cannot rely upon unanimity of belief, shared metaphysical commitments, inherited hierarchies, or uncontested truths to secure legitimacy. It must instead construct institutions capable of generating common action among persons who remain different from one another. The success of that project depends upon preserving two fundamental conditions. Every person subject to the order must remain present within it as a bearer of claims and every participant must remain protected against relations of final domination.

A society may remain divided about religion, morality, economics, identity, and the meaning of the good life. It may never achieve consensus regarding ultimate truths. Yet if it preserves inclusion and symmetry, it preserves something more fundamental: a framework within which disagreement can continue without destroying the equal standing of the participants themselves. In a world without a universally accepted final authority, legitimacy begins when no person is rendered nobody and no person is permitted to become everybody.
