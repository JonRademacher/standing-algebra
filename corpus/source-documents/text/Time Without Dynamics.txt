Abstract

This paper presents the physics instantiation of a constraintbased admissibility substrate that has been independently developed across multiple formal domains. Related instantiations of the same underlying concept appear in On Relevance (philosophy), Standing Algebra (mathematics), and ΣR (a computational formalization implemented in Lean4). The present work applies the framework to physics, demonstrating how the same admissibility substrate accounts for temporal asymmetry and irreversibility without introducing new dynamical laws, privileged initial conditions, or hypothesislevel axioms.

Rather than characterizing physical behavior in terms of trajectories or equations of motion, the framework operates at the level of admissible transformations. Admissibility is formulated measuretheoretically, as a property of measurable transformations preserving domainindependent monotonicity conditions. Interoperability is defined as the strict intersection of admissibility classes across domains. Within this formulation, irreversibility arises as a measuretheoretic obstruction to the existence of admissible inverses, while local reversibility and apparent time symmetry remain compatible with a globally directed arrow of time.

Although the formal development is nongeometric, admissible state variations admit a derivative statespace interpretation, explaining why some local transformations extend globally while others fail. The physics instantiation presented here aligns with the philosophical, mathematical, and computational treatments, collectively demonstrating that the admissibility substrate is portable across domains while remaining internally rigorous within each. No claim is made that these instantiations exhaust the framework’s applicability.

1. Introduction: The Incompleteness of EntropyBased Accounts

1.1 The Arrow of Time Problem

A persistent problem in the foundations of physics is the apparent incompatibility between the timereversal invariance of fundamental dynamical laws and the unidirectional character of macroscopic temporal phenomena. At the microscopic level, the equations governing classical mechanics, quantum mechanics, and relativistic field theory are, with limited and wellunderstood exceptions, invariant under time reversal. If a trajectory is a solution to the equations of motion, then a suitably defined reversed trajectory is also a solution. Formally, for a wide class of physical systems, there exists a timereversal operator such that

where denotes the dynamical evolution operator on the system’s state space.

Despite this microscopic symmetry, macroscopic phenomena exhibit a robust and universal temporal asymmetry. Physical systems prepared in lowentropy macrostates reliably evolve toward higherentropy macrostates; the reverse evolution is not observed in nature except in carefully engineered, locally isolated contexts. Broken objects do not spontaneously reassemble, thermal gradients relax rather than sharpen, and reliable records exist of the past but not of the future. These observations are codified in the Second Law of Thermodynamics, which states that the entropy of an isolated system does not decrease.

However, the statement that entropy increases does not, by itself, constitute an explanation of time’s arrow. Entropy is a function defined on macrostates, not a generator of dynamics. The Second Law constrains the statistical behavior of ensembles, but it does not explain why physical evolution consistently realizes one temporal orientation rather than the other. The microscopic equations of motion permit entropydecreasing trajectories just as readily as entropyincreasing ones. Any account that identifies entropy increase as the cause of temporal asymmetry therefore risks circularity unless it explains why entropy itself exhibits directed behavior.

This motivates a sharper formulation of the problem: why are entropydecreasing global trajectories not physically realized, despite being dynamically admissible? A satisfactory account of time’s arrow must answer this question without presupposing the very asymmetry it seeks to explain.

1.2 Limitations of Existing Approaches

Boltzmann Statistical Typicality

The dominant contemporary explanation of macroscopic irreversibility appeals to statistical mechanics. On the Boltzmannian account, entropy is defined as

where is the region of phase space corresponding to a macrostate and is the Liouville measure. Highentropy macrostates occupy vastly larger regions of phase space than lowentropy macrostates. Consequently, for almost all microstates compatible with a lowentropy macrostate, forward time evolution leads to macrostates of higher entropy. Entropy increase is therefore said to be typical.

While this reasoning explains why entropy increase is overwhelmingly probable, it does not explain why the universe is ever found in a lowentropy macrostate, nor why typicality arguments apply preferentially in one temporal direction rather than symmetrically in both. The same statistical considerations imply that entropy should increase both toward the future and toward the past from any given lowentropy macrostate. Statistical typicality alone therefore fails to ground a genuine temporal asymmetry.

The Past Hypothesis

To address this deficiency, many authors introduce the Past Hypothesis, which postulates that the universe began in an exceptionally lowentropy macrostate. Given this boundary condition, entropy increase toward the future follows as a statistical consequence. Although this hypothesis is mathematically coherent and empirically consistent, it functions as an axiomatic stipulation rather than a derived result. It relocates the problem of time asymmetry from dynamics to initial conditions without explaining why such conditions obtain.

Moreover, the Past Hypothesis does not render entropydecreasing trajectories physically illegitimate. It merely assigns them vanishing probability relative to a chosen initial macrostate. As a result, temporal asymmetry remains contingent rather than structurally necessary.

Cosmological Boundary Conditions

Related approaches appeal to special features of the early universe, such as constraints on spacetime geometry or gravitational degrees of freedom. While such proposals narrow the class of admissible initial states and may successfully account for low initial entropy, they continue to treat temporal asymmetry as a consequence of privileged boundary conditions imposed on an otherwise symmetric framework. The arrow of time is not derived from the structure of physical state space itself but from assumptions imposed at its boundaries.

Summary of the Limitation

Across these approaches, a common limitation emerges: time asymmetry is assumed rather than derived. Whether through probabilistic typicality, postulated initial conditions, or cosmological constraints, the direction of entropy increase is treated as a given feature of the universe rather than as a necessary consequence of the structure of physically admissible evolution. None of these frameworks establish that entropydecreasing global trajectories are physically illegitimate; they establish only that such trajectories are rare relative to a selected measure or boundary condition.

1.3 Thesis Statement

This paper advances a different claim, grounded in the Interoperability Constraint Geometry (ICG) framework I have developed elsewhere. I argue that the arrow of time and the monotonic behavior of entropy arise not from dynamical laws, probabilistic typicality, or special initial conditions, but from a global structural constraint on physically admissible state transitions.

The central thesis is the following:

The universe itself defines a geometric legitimacy envelope for state transitions.
Only those transitions that remain globally interoperable—i.e., embeddable without contradiction into the total physical state of the universe—are physically realizable.

Within this framework, entropy monotonicity is not a statistical tendency layered atop timesymmetric laws. Instead, it emerges as a structural property of the set of admissible evolutions. Entropydecreasing trajectories are not merely improbable; at global scales, they fail to compose with the rest of the universe in a manner consistent with causal structure, decoherence, and thermodynamic closure.

Accordingly, the arrow of time is understood as an orientation induced by the geometry of physically legitimate transitions rather than as a primitive temporal direction or an artifact of special boundary conditions. The remainder of this paper formalizes this claim and demonstrates that it follows from, and unifies, established results in statistical mechanics, quantum theory, and cosmology.

It depends on whether a state or trajectory can be consistently embedded into the total physical system without violating global constraints. This distinction will be central to the argument developed in later sections.

2.2 Entropy as a Measure on CoarseGrained State Space

Entropy enters physics not as a fundamental dynamical variable but as a measuretheoretic quantity defined relative to a coarsegraining of state space.

Crucially, entropy is not a generator of motion and does not appear in the fundamental equations of evolution. The time derivative of entropy is not fixed by the microscopic laws; it depends on how trajectories traverse the coarsegrained structure of state space.

Geometry Rather Than Dynamics

From this perspective, entropy increase reflects the fact that highentropy macrostates occupy vastly larger regions of state space than lowentropy ones. A lowentropy macrostate corresponds to a highly constrained, geometrically thin subset of , while highentropy macrostates correspond to geometrically robust regions.

This geometric asymmetry alone, however, does not explain temporal directionality. For any trajectory that evolves from a lowentropy region to a highentropy one, the timereversed trajectory is equally valid under the microscopic dynamics. Entropy, therefore, does not by itself distinguish future from past. Any explanation of entropy monotonicity must appeal to constraints on which trajectories are physically admissible, not merely to the geometry of state space in isolation.

2.3 Known Constraints on Physical Realizability

The distinction between mathematical possibility and physical admissibility is enforced in practice by a collection of wellestablished constraints. These constraints are not speculative additions; they are integral to accepted physical theory.

Causality (General Relativity)

In relativistic physics, physically admissible states and trajectories must respect the causal structure of spacetime. While the field equations of general relativity admit solutions containing closed timelike curves or other exotic features, such solutions raise consistency problems when embedded in a universe containing quantum fields, thermodynamic processes, and observers. Causal structure therefore functions as a global constraint on physical realizability, not merely as a local dynamical rule.

Unitarity and Decoherence (Quantum Mechanics)

In quantum mechanics, unitarity governs microscopic evolution, but physically realized macrostates depend on decoherence and entanglement with an environment. States that require global phase coherence across macroscopic degrees of freedom are dynamically definable but physically unstable and unobservable. Decoherence imposes an effectively irreversible structure on admissible evolutions by enforcing consistency between subsystems and their environments.

Thermodynamic Consistency

Thermodynamic laws impose further global constraints. While local entropy reduction is permitted and routinely observed, such reductions are physically admissible only when accompanied by compensating entropy increase elsewhere. A global entropy decrease would require finetuned correlations across the entire universe, rendering such trajectories incompatible with the observed independence and decoherence of macroscopic subsystems.

Summary

Taken together, these considerations establish a foundational point that is often left implicit: physical evolution is constrained not only by equations of motion but by global conditions of consistency and embeddability. The space of physically admissible states is therefore a structured subset of the mathematical state space, shaped by causal, quantum, and thermodynamic constraints.

This background provides the basis for the next section, in which I formalize these constraints using the Interoperability Constraint Geometry (ICG) framework and show how entropy monotonicity and temporal orientation emerge as structural properties of admissible evolution rather than as contingent statistical tendencies.

3. Interoperability Constraint Geometry (ICG)

3.1 Motivation: Composability as a Physical Requirement

Physical evolution is typically characterized as the lawful progression of states under welldefined dynamical equations. However, across all major physical frameworks, lawful evolution is necessary but not sufficient for physical realizability. A state transition may satisfy the local equations of motion of a subsystem while nevertheless failing to be physically admissible when embedded into the universe as a whole.

This distinction is already implicit in multiple areas of physics:

In classical mechanics, Liouville’s theorem guarantees measure preservation under Hamiltonian flow, yet conservation laws and boundary conditions restrict which trajectories are dynamically accessible.

In quantum mechanics, unitary evolution preserves von Neumann entropy for isolated systems, yet most mathematically definable states are physically unrealizable due to decoherence and environmental entanglement.

In relativistic physics, locally valid solutions to Einstein’s field equations may fail to embed into a globally causal spacetime.

What unifies these cases is a structural requirement that is often left implicit: physical evolution must be composable. That is, the evolution of any subsystem must remain globally consistent with the evolution of all other subsystems and with the constraints imposed by causal structure, quantum coherence, and thermodynamic consistency.

The Interoperability Constraint Geometry (ICG) formalizes this requirement. It does not introduce a new physical interaction or modify existing dynamics. Rather, it makes explicit a constraint that is already enforced—often tacitly—across accepted physical theories.

10. Conclusion

10.1 What Has Actually Been Shown

At the end of this work, the central result can be stated plainly:

Across all physically successful theories, there is a shared distinction between what the equations allow and what the world actually realizes.

This distinction is not an interpretation layered on top of physics. It is not a philosophical preference, and it is not a new law. It is something physicists already rely on—often tacitly—whenever they decide that certain formal solutions “do not happen,” “cannot be prepared,” or “are not physically meaningful.”

What this work has done is to make that distinction explicit, precise, and shared across domains.

We have shown, in detail, that classical mechanics, quantum mechanics, general relativity, thermodynamics, and information theory all face the same structural situation:

Their equations generate more possibilities than the world permits.

The excluded possibilities are not excluded arbitrarily.

They are excluded because they fail a common requirement: global consistency.

10.2 What “Structural” Means Here

It is important to be clear about the nature of the claim.

This work does not propose a new mechanism, force, or substance. It does not claim that time, entropy, or causality are “made of” some deeper entity. Instead, it makes a structural claim of the following kind:

Dynamics tell us how states would evolve if they could.

Structure tells us which of those evolutions can actually exist as part of the same world.

Put more simply: equations generate candidates; structure selects realities.

The Pluralist NonDomination Substrate (PNDS) introduced here is nothing more—and nothing less—than the minimal kind of global state space required for this selection to make sense. It allows multiple descriptions to coexist without forcing any one of them to dominate the others. The Interoperability Constraint Geometry (ICG) is the mathematical way of saying which transitions between those states can be part of a single, coherent history.

No theory is reduced to another.
No equations are replaced.
Nothing new is made to “do the work.”

10.3 Why This Keeps Showing Up Everywhere

One of the striking features of the analysis is how repetitive it becomes once you know what to look for.

In every domain examined, the same structural features appear:

Loss of information under reduction
Coarsegraining, partial trace, erasure, and projection are all noninjective.

Irreversibility without violating reversibility
Fundamental dynamics are reversible; realized histories are not.

Failure of closure under inversion
The set of realizable processes forms a semigroup, not a group.

Exclusion of closed loops
Entropy cycles, backwardtime histories, and closed timelike curves are mathematically definable but physically unrealized.

These are not analogies. They are the same mathematical pattern appearing in different clothing. The framework developed here does not invent that pattern—it gives it a name and a geometry.

10.4 A Glimpse Ahead: Algebraic Quantum Field Theory

The framework becomes particularly suggestive when viewed through algebraic quantum field theory (AQFT), where global structure already plays a central role.

In AQFT, physical content is encoded not in fields at points, but in nets of local algebras

Nothing has been proven here.
But something precise has been put on the table.

10.5 Beyond Physics

Although this work has remained firmly within physics, the structural ideas involved—plurality, nondomination, embeddability, and admissibility—are not unique to physical systems.

They also appear wherever:

local agents act with partial information,

global states cannot be reconstructed uniquely,

and legitimacy depends on consistency rather than coercion.

This suggests possible extensions to information systems, distributed computation, and institutional processes. These are not claims of identity, but of shared structure. Whether such extensions are fruitful remains to be seen.

10.6 Closing Perspective

The contribution of this work is intentionally restrained.

It does not tell us what the laws of physics are.
It tells us how the laws we already have decide what is real.

The result can be summarized in one sentence:

Physical reality is not defined by what equations permit, but by the shape of the space in which those equations live—and by how that shape structurally allows, or forbids, global consistency.

Everything else follows from that—quietly, and without drama.
