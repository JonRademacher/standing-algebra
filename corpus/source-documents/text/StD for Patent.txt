Title: Geometric Existence-Based Governance Architecture

Inventor: Jonathan Rademacher

Purpose of this Document

This document provides an architecture-level summary of the invention and is intended to serve as a concise orientation to the broader technical disclosures contained in the associated Patent Packet and Patent Addendum. It focuses on the common architectural stack, distinguishing features, and representative embodiments rather than exhaustive implementation detail.

1. Technical Field

This invention relates to systems for controlling admissibility, execution, and system effects in stateful computing environments. More specifically, it concerns:

constraint-based and geometry-based computation

execution mediation and system governance

security architectures for data, devices, services, and AI systems

compression properties in computational, physical, hardware, and data environments; and

diagnostic properties relating to state-space transitions in physical, hardware, and computational spaces

The invention applies across single-device, distributed, and federated computing systems, as well as physical and hybrid digital-physical systems whose states, relations, and transitions can be represented or governed computationally.

2. Background / Problem

Modern computing systems are inherently stateful, maintaining persistent data, configuration, and execution context. Many conventional security and governance approaches rely primarily on one or more of the following categories:

Identity-based systems (authentication/authorization)

Policy-based systems (ACLs, RBAC, ABAC)

Detection-based systems (anomaly detection, intrusion prevention)

These approaches may share limitations relevant to the present invention:

They rely on explicit decision logic (allow/deny)

They require persistent representations (identity, credentials, policies)

They introduce attack surfaces (credential theft, policy bypass, leakage)

They allow authorized but structurally unsafe actions

They operate after or alongside execution, not as a structural constraint

As a result, the systems described may remain vulnerable to:

unintended data exposure

unauthorized state modification

inference and aggregation attacks

leakage through scoring, ranking, or classification

The approaches described above do not, by themselves, provide the unified cross-domain structural mechanism proposed in this disclosure. The proposed architecture may provide opportunities for structural reconstruction, compression, controlled realization, state-space diagnostics, and security-related effects through a common admissibility architecture. In applicable embodiments, reconstruction-relevant structure may be retained in place of redundant realizations, potentially reducing storage and communication requirements. Likewise, admissibility may be determined through existence of a constraint-satisfying context rather than through continuous evaluation of identities, permissions, or policies.

Because inadmissible effects need not possess a realizable pathway within the admissible geometry, certain classes of attacks, misconfigurations, and unauthorized transitions may be reduced or prevented structurally. The specific security properties achieved depend upon the defining constraints and the realization mechanism employed.

3. Summary of the Invention

The invention provides a computational architecture in which relational geometry is combined with existence-based admissibility so that the realizability of a computational effect depends upon the existence of a valid context satisfying invariant relational constraints. The invention is directed to the amalgamation of the following architectural elements:

invariant relational constraints defining the required relations among data, system components, states, processes, or computational objects;

a geometric representation of the configuration space produced by those constraints;

an admissibility landscape consisting of the configurations, pathways, or states that survive the imposed constraints;

an existence condition under which a computational effect is realizable if and only if at least one admissible context exists; and

realization through the surviving admissible context or contexts rather than through a separate permission decision imposed upon an otherwise unrestricted runtime.

This amalgamation forms an existence-based geometric admissibility stack. The stack may be expressed in its general architectural form as follows:

Invariant relational constraints → geometric state-space formation → admissibility filtering → surviving admissible context or landscape → existence or non-existence of the corresponding computational effect

Where at least one context satisfies the invariant relational constraints, the corresponding execution, reconstruction, transition, or other system effect is structurally realizable. Where no context satisfies those constraints, the effect is not realizable because no valid computational context exists through which it can occur. Accordingly:

Existence of at least one admissible context → the corresponding effect is realizable

Non-existence of every admissible context → the corresponding effect is not realizable

The absence of an admissible context is not merely an explicit denial produced by a conventional allow-or-deny mechanism. It is the structural absence of a runtime, pathway, reconstruction, state transition, or relational configuration capable of producing the relevant effect. The invention therefore changes the location at which admissibility is determined. Rather than first presuming that an operation is computationally available and then deciding whether permission should be granted, the architecture constructs or evaluates the state space so that only operations having a valid admissible context are realizable within that space.

The invention should not be understood as a collection of unrelated security, encryption, compression, reconstruction, or diagnostic techniques. These properties arise from the same underlying architectural stack. They are characteristic consequences and technical signifiers of combining invariant relational geometry with existence-based admissibility. Particular implementations may emphasize one or more of these properties, but the underlying invention is the integrated architecture from which those properties are produced.

The architecture is not dependent upon any single mathematical notation, data structure, hardware design, software language, cryptographic primitive, storage medium, decision model, or physical realization mechanism. It may be realized through software, firmware, circuitry, hardware-assisted computation, graph structures, relational stores, distributed systems, cryptographic components, sensor environments, artificial-intelligence systems, or combinations thereof. These are possible realization mechanisms of the architecture and do not independently define its architectural identity.

3.1 The Existence-Based Geometric Admissibility Stack

The existence-based geometric admissibility stack begins with a set of invariant relational constraints. These constraints specify the relations that must hold for a particular data structure, state, transition, execution pathway, reconstruction, or system effect to be valid.

The constraints collectively define the shape of the applicable state space. Candidate configurations are evaluated according to whether they satisfy the declared relations. Configurations that fail one or more necessary constraints are eliminated. Configurations satisfying all applicable constraints survive as members of the admissible landscape. The resulting process may be represented as follows:

Candidate configuration or state landscape → invariant relational constraints → geometric admissibility envelope → elimination of incompatible configurations → surviving admissible landscape → realizable computational effects

The geometry is therefore not used merely as a visual description of decisions made elsewhere. It determines the structural envelope within which realization is possible. Existence within that envelope constitutes admissibility for the corresponding system effect.

3.3 Structural Reconstruction, Compression, and Encryption

Structural reconstruction provides a common architectural connection among compression, encryption, diagnostics, and recovery. The invention may represent data, system states, or computational objects through the invariant relations required to preserve their reconstruction-relevant properties. In such implementations, the primary reconstruction object need not consist of a complete stored copy of every realized object. It may instead consist of a relational representation specifying the constraints under which one or more valid realizations can exist. The general reconstruction chain is:

Source data, system state, or computational object → identification or declaration of reconstruction-relevant invariants → geometric representation of the required relations → filtering of incompatible reconstructions → surviving admissible reconstruction landscape

Compression may arise where the relational representation requires less storage, transmission, or redundancy than storing each complete realization independently. Repeated components or relations may be retained once, while multiple objects or states are represented through their relationships to those retained elements. Such reduction occurs where the retained relational representation and reconstruction requirements require fewer resources than the independently stored realizations they replace.

Encryption or controlled reconstruction may arise where the protected data, object, state, or effect can be recovered only through a relational configuration satisfying the applicable invariants. Individual components may be distributed, separated, encoded, transformed, or rendered structurally incomplete outside the admissible geometry. Possession of an isolated datum, token, component, or partial relation is therefore insufficient where the complete relational structure required for reconstruction does not exist. The encryption and controlled-reconstruction chain may be represented as follows:

Protected information or system effect → relational decomposition or constraint-based encoding → structural incompleteness outside the admissible geometry → formation of a constraint-satisfying reconstruction context → realization of the protected information or effect

3.4 Structural Security and Runtime Formation

The stack produces a structural security property by conditioning execution upon the existence of an admissible runtime or computational context. In a conventional permission architecture, an operation may already exist as an available capability, after which a separate mechanism determines whether a user, process, or object may invoke it. In the present architecture, the operation is realizable only where the required relational context exists. Where the context does not exist, the pathway required to produce the operation is absent from the applicable state space.

The architecture does not eliminate every possible vulnerability merely by being geometric or existence-based. Its specific security effect is that operations falling outside the geometrically admissible state space are structurally non-realizable through that state space. The security boundary is therefore established through the construction and preservation of admissible relational configurations rather than solely through an externally imposed ruleset.

3.5 State-Space Diagnostics and Failure Geometry

The same stack may be used to diagnose physical hardware, software, embedded systems, distributed systems, sensor environments, data environments, artificial-intelligence systems, and other state-bearing systems. For a monitored system, measurements, inferred variables, temporal changes, component dependencies, and operational relations may be represented as a state at a time t. Known operational, degraded, transitional, and failure conditions may be characterized by the respective invariant constraints associated with those conditions. The diagnostic architecture may be expressed as follows:

Measured or inferred system state at time t → relational state-space representation → geometric localization relative to retained constraints → elimination of incompatible state characterizations → surviving operational, degraded, transitional, failure, or recovery landscape

Where a known failure corresponds to a particular state-space region or transition geometry, the invariant relations characterizing that failure may be retained for future analysis. A later system state may then be evaluated against the retained failure geometry.

This produces a diagnostic capability that is not limited to detecting a single threshold violation or matching an isolated sensor value. A relevant condition may be latent in the relations among multiple measurements, temporal changes, dependencies, transitions, or remaining realization pathways. The architecture may therefore identify a failure condition, emerging degradation, transition boundary, obstruction, or recovery opportunity that is not independently represented by any single direct measurement. A detected obstruction does not automatically establish failure of the system’s overall objective. The architecture distinguishes among:

obstruction of one realization pathway;

exhaustion of all pathways leading to a declared admissible state;

entry into a retained failure geometry;

movement toward a failure boundary;

persistence within a degraded but recoverable region; and

continued availability of alternative recovery pathways.

The diagnostic use of the stack may support quality management, predictive maintenance, fault localization, product-state evaluation, resilience analysis, transition monitoring, recovery planning, and evaluation of future reachable states.

A digital state space may consist of a deliberately manufactured computational environment whose available runtimes, transformations, and state transitions are established according to the invariant relational constraints. Such manufactured environments may include, without limitation:

a null runtime;

a non-dominating runtime;

a restricted execution environment;

a secure reconstruction environment;

a hardware-mediated admissibility context;

a distributed constraint environment; or

another environment in which only constraint-satisfying effects possess a realizable pathway.

The formation of a manufactured state space may be represented as follows:

Declared invariant relations → construction of the state-space geometry → formation of admissible runtime or reconstruction contexts → existence only of compatible realizations within that state space

The environment therefore does not merely receive a ruleset instructing an otherwise unrestricted runtime to deny prohibited effects. Rather, the geometry of the environment determines which runtimes, pathways, transitions, and reconstructions can exist. This distinction permits the constraints to be carried intrinsically by the structure of the computational environment. A ruleset may still be used as an implementation tool, but the inventive architecture lies in the formation or evaluation of realizability through the existence-based geometric admissibility stack

4. Core Technical Concept

4.1 Invariant Relational Constraint Architecture

The invention begins with a collection of invariant relational constraints governing the admissibility of computational states, transitions, reconstructions, executions, and system effects. The constraints may include, without limitation:

equality and inequality relationships;

logical dependencies;

temporal relationships;

contextual constraints;

state-transition requirements;

topological relationships;

structural invariants;

reconstruction requirements; and

combinations thereof.

The constraints collectively define the admissible relational structure of a system. Rather than operating as a collection of independently evaluated rules, the constraints are treated as a unified relational object whose satisfaction determines the existence of admissible system configurations. The resulting relational object induces a geometric representation of the state space in which compatible configurations form an admissible region and incompatible configurations are excluded from realization. Accordingly:

Invariant Relational Constraints → Geometric State-Space Formation → Admissible Relational Structure

The geometry is therefore not merely descriptive. It is the operational representation through which realizability is evaluated.

4.2 Geometric Admissibility Landscape Formation

The induced geometry produces an admissibility landscape consisting of all configurations, states, pathways, reconstructions, or computational contexts satisfying the required constraints. The admissibility determination process consists of filtering incompatible configurations rather than selecting preferred configurations. The process may be represented as:

Candidate Landscape → Constraint Evaluation → Elimination of Incompatible Configurations → Surviving Admissible Landscape

The resulting landscape may contain:

no surviving configurations;

a unique surviving configuration; or

multiple surviving configurations.

The architecture does not assume that a single surviving configuration must exist. Where multiple configurations survive, each remains admissible. The admissibility process therefore preserves the complete surviving landscape rather than performing winner selection, optimization, ranking, or preference assignment. Selection among multiple admissible survivors, where desired, may be performed by a separate mechanism and does not constitute the admissibility determination itself.

4.3 Existence-Based Realization

A computational effect is realizable if and only if there exists at least one admissible context within the admissibility landscape capable of producing that effect.

The effect may include, without limitation:

execution of a process;

data access;

state transition;

reconstruction of information;

generation of output;

authorization of an operation;

creation of a communication pathway; or

another computational consequence.

The realization condition may be represented as:

Existence of an Admissible Context → Realizability of the Effect

Non-Existence of an Admissible Context → Non-Realizability of the Effect

Importantly, non-realizability is not produced by a denial decision. Instead, the computational pathway necessary to realize the effect does not exist within the admissible geometry. The invention therefore shifts control from permission-based evaluation to existence-based realization.

4.4 Structural Reconstruction Principle

The invention treats computational effects as consequences of relational structure rather than isolated objects. Data, states, executions, permissions, and system effects may be represented through the invariant relations required for their realization. Consequently, realization becomes a reconstruction process governed by admissibility.

The general form is:

Relational Representation → Constraint Satisfaction → Admissible Reconstruction Context → Realized Computational Effect

This reconstruction principle provides a common architectural foundation for multiple technical consequences of the invention. For example:

compression may arise through reconstruction from retained relational structure;

encryption may arise through controlled reconstruction requiring satisfaction of particular relational configurations;

diagnostics may arise through reconstruction of latent system state from retained constraint relationships;

recovery analysis may arise through reconstruction of surviving admissible pathways following obstruction of prior pathways.

These consequences are inherited from the reconstruction architecture and are not independent architectural components.

4.5 Non-Representational Admissibility Determination

Admissibility is determined through structural compatibility rather than representational attributes. In applicable embodiments, admissibility may therefore be established without requiring:

identity representation;

credential representation;

policy representation;

user classification;

object classification;

ranking systems;

optimization systems;

probability scoring systems;

similarity scoring systems; or

anomaly detection systems.

The invention does not require that all such mechanisms be absent from every implementation. Where present, they operate as auxiliary realization components rather than as the primary determinant of admissibility. The primary determinant remains the existence of a constraint-satisfying relational configuration. Accordingly, admissibility may be evaluated independent of participant identity and instead based upon whether the required relational structure exists.

4.6 Continuous Constraint Preservation

The admissibility geometry is maintained throughout the lifetime of the relevant computation, interaction, reconstruction, execution, state transition, or system process. Constraint satisfaction is therefore not limited to an initial admission event. Instead, admissibility may be continuously preserved through the evolution of the state space. As system states change over time, the geometry is correspondingly updated through the applicable invariant relations. This continuous preservation ensures that realized effects remain supported by an admissible context throughout their existence. The process may be represented as:

Current State → Constraint Preservation → Admissible Successor State → Constraint Preservation → Admissible Successor State

Because admissibility is tied to the maintained relational structure, transitions toward incompatible configurations become structurally constrained by the geometry itself. Accordingly, the architecture may prevent, detect, localize, or terminate transitions that would otherwise move the system outside the admissible state space.

4.7 Geometry-Derived Runtime Formation

In conventional computational architectures, a runtime environment is first constructed with a set of available operations, state transitions, interfaces, communication pathways, and execution capabilities. Constraints are then introduced into or applied to that preexisting environment through policies, permission systems, validation procedures, monitors, filters, or enforcement mechanisms.

The conventional sequence may be represented as follows:

Preexisting runtime environment → introduction of externally represented constraints → evaluation of attempted operations → permission, denial, correction, or termination

Under this sequence, an inadmissible operation may remain representable and computationally expressible within the underlying environment even when a subsequent mechanism prevents its successful completion. The constraint regulates an already formed possibility space. The present invention permits the order of construction to be reversed. The invariant relational constraints are introduced before the operational possibility space of the runtime has been finalized. The constraints are converted into a geometric admissibility structure, and that structure is then used to determine the forming elements from which the runtime is constructed. The geometry-derived formation sequence may be represented as follows:

Declared invariant relational constraints → formal constraint representation → derivation of the admissible geometric state space → extraction of admissible runtime-forming elements → construction or configuration of the runtime from those elements → execution within the resulting geometry-derived environment

The runtime is therefore not formed independently and subsequently restricted. Its operations, interfaces, pathways, and state transitions are derived from, generated according to, or instantiated within the admissibility geometry.

4.7.1 Formalization of the Defining Constraints

Runtime formation begins by receiving or constructing a set of invariant relational constraints specifying the conditions that must hold among the relevant participants, objects, resources, states, processes, or computational effects. The constraints may define, without limitation:

which entities or state variables may participate in a computational relation;

which relations may exist among those entities or variables;

which initial states are admissible;

which transformations may be performed;

which state transitions preserve admissibility;

which information may be available to particular processes or contexts;

which dependencies must exist before an effect can be realized;

which relations must remain invariant throughout execution;

which configurations must remain unavailable; and

which terminal states or endpoint geometries satisfy the declared objective.

These constraints are formalized in a machine-operable representation. Depending upon the realization mechanism, the representation may take the form of equations, inequalities, logical expressions, graphs, relations, types, schemas, transition systems, dependency structures, circuit conditions, protocol specifications, intermediate representations, or combinations thereof. At this stage, the constraints are not yet rules applied to a completed runtime. They constitute the input from which the runtime’s admissible possibility space will be derived.

4.7.2 Formation of the Admissible Geometry

The formalized constraints are amalgamated to determine the set of states, relations, pathways, and transformations that jointly satisfy them. Let C denote the complete set of applicable invariant relational constraints and let X denote the candidate configuration space from which the runtime may be formed. The admissible geometry A may be represented generally as:

A(C) = {x ∈ X | x satisfies every applicable constraint in C}

The resulting geometry identifies not only admissible individual states but also the permitted relations and transitions among those states. Accordingly, the geometry may contain:

admissible starting configurations;

admissible runtime components;

admissible compositions of those components;

admissible interfaces and communication pathways;

admissible transformations;

admissible successor states;

admissible reconstruction pathways;

admissible terminal configurations; and

admissibility-preserving execution trajectories.

Candidate elements that cannot participate in any constraint-satisfying configuration are removed from the runtime-forming landscape. Candidate transitions that would necessarily leave the admissible geometry are likewise excluded, disabled, left uninstantiated, or made unavailable through the selected realization mechanism.

4.7.3 Extraction of Runtime-Forming Elements

The admissible geometry is then translated into a runtime specification by extracting the elements required to realize that geometry computationally. The extracted runtime-forming elements may include:

executable operations and primitives;

data structures and object relations;

interfaces and communication channels;

state-transition and composition rules;

initialization, persistence, update, and termination conditions; and

reconstruction and admissibility-preservation requirements.

An operation is included as a runtime-forming element only where at least one admissible context exists in which that operation can be realized while preserving the applicable constraints. A transition is included only where it connects an admissible source state to an admissible successor state in accordance with the defining relations. An interface is formed only with the capabilities and relational exposures admitted by the geometry. The extraction process may be expressed generally as:

Admissible geometry → admissible operations + admissible interfaces + admissible state transitions + admissible compositions + admissible persistence relations = geometry-derived runtime specification

The runtime specification therefore carries forward the structure of the admissible geometry. It does not merely contain a textual copy of the original constraint set.

4.7.4 Construction of the Executable Environment

The geometry-derived runtime specification is realized through the applicable implementation mechanism. For example, it may be used to generate, select, configure, or assemble:

executable code or intermediate representations;

virtual-machine, interpreter, container, type, or capability configurations;

graph, database, state-machine, or distributed-protocol structures;

firmware, hardware logic, circuits, or communication topologies; and

hybrid software-hardware environments.

The selected mechanism instantiates the operations, transitions, interfaces, and relations extracted from the admissible geometry. The resulting environment possesses those elements as its available computational structure. The complete formation chain is therefore:

Constraint declaration → constraint amalgamation → admissible geometry → admissible runtime-forming elements → runtime specification → executable environment → admissibility-preserving execution

4.7.5 Intrinsic Realization of the Constraints

Once the runtime has been formed, the original constraints need not remain present only as a separate policy layer that evaluates every attempted operation. Their operative consequences have been incorporated into the runtime’s available components, relations, interfaces, and transitions. This distinction may be expressed as follows:

Externally constrained runtime

Runtime possibility space + external constraints = regulated environment

Geometry-derived runtime

Constraint geometry → runtime possibility space = intrinsically shaped environment

In the externally constrained architecture, the environment may contain both admissible and inadmissible pathways, with an enforcement mechanism distinguishing between them. In the geometry-derived architecture, the environment is formed so that its realizable pathways correspond to the admissible geometry. The effect is not merely labeled inadmissible. The runtime lacks an admissible structural path through which it can be realized.

4.7.6 Continuous Geometric Preservation

Where the runtime evolves during operation, the geometry may govern not only its initial formation but also the formation of subsequent states. For a current admissible state xₜ, the runtime exposes or constructs only those successor states xₜ₊₁ for which the applicable transition relation preserves the defining constraints:

xₜ ∈ A and xₜ → xₜ₊₁ is an admissibility-preserving transition therefore xₜ₊₁ ∈ A

If changing data, components, participants, or external conditions alter the applicable state space, the runtime-forming process may be repeated or incrementally updated. The revised constraints produce a revised admissible geometry, from which revised operations, interfaces, pathways, or transitions may be instantiated. Continuous enforcement is therefore more precisely characterized as continuous preservation or regeneration of the admissible geometry. The architecture need not repeatedly ask whether an independently available operation should be permitted. It may instead maintain the existence of admissible successor contexts and withhold, remove, deactivate, or decline to form pathways that no longer possess such a context.

4.7.7 Non-Domination Runtime Example

A non-domination runtime illustrates how a declared property may become inherent to a computational environment rather than being assigned to the environment after its formation. In a policy-oriented implementation, a general-purpose environment may first be constructed with operations capable of acquiring unilateral control, overriding another participant’s operative frame, removing viable exit, or establishing unreviewable authority. A subsequent policy layer then classifies or prohibits some uses of those capabilities. The geometry-derived approach begins instead with relational constraints defining the conditions under which domination-producing configurations cannot belong to the admissible state space. Depending upon the intended implementation, such constraints may require, for example:

preservation of each participant’s protected operative frame;

absence of unilateral external authority over another participant’s protected frame;

preservation of a legitimate and viable exit pathway;

localization and limitation of delegated authority;

reviewability of changes affecting protected relations;

revocability of delegated capabilities where required;

continuity of provenance and accountability;

prevention of unauthorized transfer or concentration of control; and

preservation of admissible recovery pathways where an interaction becomes obstructed.

These constraints are amalgamated into a non-domination admissibility geometry. The geometry determines which authority relations, interfaces, capability transfers, state transitions, and interaction pathways may be included in the runtime. The resulting formation chain is:

Non-domination relational constraints → non-domination admissibility geometry → extraction of compatible authority relations, interfaces, capabilities, transitions, and exit pathways → construction of the runtime from those elements → computational environment in which domination-producing configurations lack an admissible realization pathway

In such an environment, non-domination is not merely stored as a label, declared as an objective, or assigned as an evaluated property. The runtime is formed from a state-space geometry that excludes the relations required to realize domination as defined by the applicable constraints. The environment possesses non-domination as an inherited structural property because:

the defining constraints shape the geometry; the geometry determines the runtime-forming elements; and the runtime-forming elements determine the realizable interactions.

This does not mean that the word “non-domination” itself performs the construction or that every possible conception of non-domination is automatically captured. The relevant non-domination conditions must be translated into explicit invariant relational constraints. The inherited property is only as complete as the constraints, dependencies, and transition conditions used to form the geometry.

5. Key Distinguishing Features

Compared to conventional computational architectures, the invention introduces a distinct admissibility architecture characterized by the following features:

Determines admissibility through existence rather than permission

Represents constraints as a geometric admissibility structure

Uses admissibility filtering rather than winner-selection

Constructs or governs runtimes from admissibility geometry

Does not require identity as the primary determinant of admissibility

Does not require credential-based authorization

Does not require policy-engine-based decision making

Does not require ranking, scoring, optimization, or similarity comparison to establish admissibility

Does not require detection or classification as the primary enforcement mechanism

Preserves the complete admissible landscape where multiple admissible realizations exist

Provides structural rather than reactive security

Supports reconstruction, encryption, compression, and diagnostics through a common architectural stack

Permits computational environments whose defining properties are inherited from their construction geometry

Separates admissibility determination from subsequent optimization or selection

Maintains realization-mechanism neutrality across software, hardware, firmware, distributed, and hybrid implementations

Importantly, the invention does not claim the mathematical observation that systems of constraints may induce or correspond to geometric structures. That capability is already present within established mathematical frameworks. Algebraic-geometric results, including the Nullstellensatz and related constraint-to-geometry constructions, demonstrate that systems of constraints can be represented through geometric objects and corresponding solution structures. The distinguishing feature of the invention lies not in establishing that constraints can become geometry, but in utilizing such geometry as an operational object for admissibility determination, existence-based realization, runtime formation, runtime governance, and the production of inherited computational properties. It is this integrated architectural stack, rather than any individual embodiment, that constitutes the primary technical distinction of the invention.

A current observed state may therefore be compared against:

6.9 Predictive and Retrospective Failure-Trajectory Analysis

In one embodiment, the invention evaluates trajectories through a state space rather than evaluating only isolated states. A current state may be analyzed relative to known transition geometries to determine the admissible failure trajectories that remain reachable. Likewise, an observed failure state may be analyzed retroactively to determine the admissible pathways most consistent with the observed outcome. The architecture therefore supports both:

Predictive Analysis

Current State → Reachable Failure Regions → Reachable Recovery Regions → Future Trajectory Landscape

Retrospective Analysis

Observed Failure State → Compatible Prior States → Compatible Transition Paths → Reconstructed Failure Trajectory Landscape

This permits investigation not only of what failed, but also how the failure became reachable and which recovery opportunities may previously have existed.

6.10 Geometry-Derived Runtime Environments

In one embodiment, constraints are utilized prior to runtime formation to generate the geometry from which the runtime is constructed. Rather than creating an unrestricted environment and subsequently applying policies, the invention forms the runtime from the admissibility structure itself. The resulting runtime inherits its defining properties from the admissible geometry from which it was constructed rather than from rules imposed after formation.

8. Terminology

Unless otherwise indicated, the following terms are used throughout this document according to the definitions below.

8.1 Invariant Relational Constraint

A condition, dependency, relation, or requirement that must remain satisfied for a state, transition, reconstruction, execution, computational context, or system effect to be considered admissible.

8.2 Constraint Geometry

The geometric representation induced by one or more invariant relational constraints. Constraint geometry represents the admissible structure, states, relations, pathways, transitions, and configurations that satisfy the applicable constraints. Constraint geometry may be derived from any suitable mathematical representation and is not limited to a particular realization mechanism, notation, or mathematical framework.

8.3 Admissibility

The condition under which at least one context, configuration, pathway, state, transition, or reconstruction satisfies all applicable invariant relational constraints. A corresponding computational or physical effect is realizable where such an admissible context exists.

8.4 Admissible Context

A state, configuration, execution pathway, reconstruction pathway, runtime condition, or relational structure satisfying the applicable invariant relational constraints. An admissible context provides a realizable pathway through which a computational effect may occur.

8.5 Admissibility Landscape

The complete collection of admissible contexts surviving evaluation against the applicable constraints. Unless otherwise specified, the invention preserves the complete surviving admissibility landscape rather than selecting a preferred survivor.

8.6 Geometric Envelope

The region of a state space defined by the admissible configurations, pathways, relations, and transitions satisfying the applicable constraints. The terms geometric envelope, admissible region, and admissibility geometry may be used interchangeably where context permits.

8.7 Existence-Based Realization

A realization principle in which a computational effect becomes possible if and only if there exists an admissible context capable of producing that effect. The absence of such a context results in non-realizability rather than a required denial decision.

8.8 Filtering

The elimination of configurations, states, transitions, reconstructions, or computational contexts that fail to satisfy one or more required constraints. Filtering determines the admissibility landscape. Filtering does not imply ranking, optimization, scoring, or winner selection.

8.9 Runtime Formation

The process by which a computational environment, execution environment, state machine, protocol, software system, hardware system, or hybrid system is constructed, configured, or governed from an admissibility geometry. In a geometry-derived runtime, the available operations, transitions, interfaces, or pathways are determined by the admissibility structure rather than solely by post-formation enforcement mechanisms.

8.10 Structural Reconstruction

The realization or recovery of data, states, configurations, outputs, or system effects from retained relational structure and applicable constraints. Compression, controlled reconstruction, encryption, diagnostics, and recovery analysis may be realized through this architectural principle.

8.11 Failure Geometry

A geometric representation of states, transitions, or relational structures associated with a known degradation condition, obstruction state, fault condition, or failure mode. Failure geometries may be retained and subsequently utilized for diagnostic analysis, trajectory evaluation, failure localization, predictive assessment, and recovery analysis.

8.12 State-Space Localization

The process of determining the location of an observed configuration, system state, transition, or trajectory relative to an admissibility geometry, operational geometry, degradation geometry, recovery geometry, or failure geometry.

8.13 Inherited Property

A property arising from the operation of the admissibility architecture rather than from a separately imposed rule, policy, classification, or optimization mechanism.

8.14 Realization Mechanism

The specific software, hardware, firmware, protocol, cryptographic, mathematical, database, networking, sensing, or computational mechanism used to implement an embodiment of the invention. Realization mechanisms are implementation-dependent and are distinct from the underlying admissibility architecture.

8.15 Realization-Mechanism Neutrality

The principle that the defining architecture of the invention is determined by the relations among constraints, geometry, admissibility, existence-based realization, and runtime formation rather than by any specific implementation technology. Equivalent architectural structures remain within the scope of the invention even when implemented through different realization mechanisms.

All terms are intended to be understood in their formal operational sense within the context of the invention. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, references to geometry, admissibility, runtime formation, reconstruction, and related concepts refer to mathematical, computational, relational, or state-space structures and should not be interpreted as requiring any particular physical realization, mathematical formalism, or implementation technology.

11. Summary

The invention introduces an existence-based geometric admissibility architecture in which invariant relational constraints are utilized to form a geometric admissibility structure that governs realizability within computational, physical, and hybrid systems. Rather than determining admissibility through identities, credentials, policies, classifications, rankings, or post-hoc enforcement mechanisms, the invention determines whether a constraint-satisfying context exists through which a requested state, transition, reconstruction, execution, interaction, or system effect may be realized. The architecture operates through the following relationship:

Invariant Relational Constraints → Geometric Admissibility Structure → Admissibility Landscape Formation → Existence-Based Realization → Runtime, State-Space, or Operational-Environment Behavior

Computational, digital, physical, and hybrid environments may therefore be constructed, configured, governed, evaluated, or diagnosed according to the admissibility geometry derived from the defining constraints. The invention does not claim the mathematical observation that constraints may be represented geometrically. Rather, it utilizes constraint-derived geometry as an operational object for admissibility determination, runtime formation, state-space evaluation, reconstruction, and realization.

Through this architecture, properties including security, controlled reconstruction, encryption, compression, runtime governance, state-space diagnostics, failure localization, recovery analysis, and trajectory evaluation may arise as inherited consequences of a common architectural stack rather than as independent mechanisms. The invention therefore resides primarily in the operational amalgamation of constraint-derived geometry and existence-based admissibility rather than in any individual implementation, realization mechanism, or inherited property considered in isolation.
