Why Being Right Isn’t Enough

(A Conceptual Companion to On Relevance)

How Truth Loses Authority—and What Still Holds Us Together

By Jonathan Rademacher

Opening: A Familiar Frustration

Most of the conflicts that define modern life feel like they should be solvable.

Someone has the facts.
Someone has the moral high ground.
Someone has the science, the law, the scripture, or the numbers.

And yet—nothing moves.

People talk past each other.
Institutions stall or overreach.
Communities fracture, radicalize, or harden into silence.

If you’ve ever felt the urge to say, “I’m right—why isn’t that enough?”, this short book is for you.

The uncomfortable answer is that being right has never been enough—not to bind others, not to coordinate action, and not to hold plural societies together without force.

This book explains why.

A Note on Limits

This framework does not resolve moral disagreement, eliminate conflict, or guarantee just outcomes. It cannot tell us which values are correct or which goals should prevail. It specifies only the structural conditions under which claims may legitimately bind others in shared space.

Where those conditions cannot be met, disagreement may persist. That persistence is not always a failure. In pluralist societies, it is sometimes the cost of avoiding domination.

What Success Looks Like (So You Know What This Is For)

You haven’t “failed” as a reader if you disagree with this framework.

Success looks like being able to:

notice when a claim is being used to bind others rather than persuade them

ask who bears the risk if the claim is wrong

recognize when “correctness” is being used as a permission slip for coercion

distinguish coordination from domination

identify when the right move is repair—and when it is restraint

If this book helps you do those things more reliably, it has done its work.

1. The Mistake We Keep Making

Most arguments—political, moral, religious, scientific—share the same hidden assumption:

If a claim is correct, it deserves authority.

This feels intuitive. If something is true, good, or necessary, why shouldn’t it guide action?

But history is crowded with examples where correctness—real or perceived—became a justification for harm:

“God wills it.”

“The science is settled.”

“The law requires it.”

“The majority decided.”

“History demands it.”

Each of these claims may contain truth.
Each has also been used to excuse domination.

The mistake is not believing things.

The mistake is assuming that belief alone creates standing over others.

2. The Question We Rarely Ask

Most debates focus on what should be done.

This book asks a different question:

Who gets to bind whom—and under what conditions?

This question comes before morality, politics, or belief.
It applies even when everyone involved is sincere and acting in good faith.

You can be right—and still lack standing.
You can be wrong—and still act legitimately.

That may feel counterintuitive, but it’s the difference between persuasion and coercion, between coordination and control.

3. Coordination Is Not Agreement

Pluralist societies are not held together by harmony.
They are held together by coordination under disagreement.

Coordination does not require:

shared values

common beliefs

moral unity

consensus

It requires something thinner and more fragile:

the ability to affect one another’s actions without collapsing agency.

That means:

people can say no

mistakes can be corrected

power can be challenged

exit is possible without annihilation

When those conditions disappear, coordination doesn’t become more effective.

It becomes domination.

4. Domination Still “Works”—That’s the Problem

It’s important to be honest:

Domination does coordinate behavior.

People comply.
Orders are followed.
Systems move.

But domination coordinates by removing agency, not by preserving it.

That’s why domination can appear stable in the short term and catastrophic in the long term. It suppresses correction, learning, and dissent—the very mechanisms that allow societies to adapt without violence.

This framework does not claim domination is “evil” as a moral label.
It claims domination is structurally incompatible with pluralist society—because pluralism requires durable disagreement without annihilation.

Domination can coordinate behavior.
It cannot coordinate pluralism.

5. Why Final Authority Keeps Failing Us

Throughout history, humans have tried to solve coordination problems by appealing to final authority:

divine command

absolute morality

unquestionable expertise

unchallengeable institutions

inevitability (“this will happen anyway”)

Final authority promises certainty.
It promises relief from disagreement.

But final authority has a hidden cost:

It suspends accountability.

Once a claim is treated as final, questioning it becomes illegitimate. Correction becomes rebellion. Exit becomes treason.

This is true whether the authority is religious, secular, scientific, or political.

The problem is not which authority we choose.

The problem is assuming that any authority should be final in a plural world.

6. Interaction Is Where Authority Lives—or Dies

Nothing binds people unless it interacts with them.

Beliefs that never affect others do not require justification.
Beliefs that shape laws, norms, or enforcement do.

The moment a claim interacts with human lives, it enters shared space—and in shared space, it must meet shared conditions.

Those conditions are simple but nonnegotiable:

Accountability — Who answers if harm occurs?

Exit — Can someone refuse without being destroyed?

Corrigibility — Can the rule change if it’s wrong?

Nondomination — Does this prevent arbitrary control?

If a claim cannot meet these conditions, it may still be believed.
But it cannot legitimately bind others.

7. Example: Workplace Policy (Correctness Without Standing)

Consider a workplace policy introduced to improve productivity.

The policy is supported by data.

It reflects best practices.

Leadership sincerely believes it will benefit everyone.

Employees raise concerns:

burnout

loss of flexibility

increased attrition

Management responds with correctness:

the metrics are clear

the research is solid

the decision is final

Nothing about this requires bad faith.
The policy may even be right.

But conflict escalates anyway.

Why?

Because the question being answered is the wrong one.

The issue is no longer whether the policy is correct.
It is whether those enforcing it have standing to bind others under the imposed conditions.

If employees cannot refuse without penalty,
if feedback cannot alter the policy,
if accountability for harm is diffuse or denied,
then what is occurring is no longer coordination—it is domination.

The breakdown is structural, not moral.

8. Example: Public Health (When Correctness Meets Exit Collapse)

Consider a public health intervention during a crisis.

Authorities may have strong evidence that an intervention reduces harm. Even if correct, the legitimacy question remains:

Who bears the risk if the evidence is wrong or incomplete?

Are those most constrained given meaningful standing or representation?

Can policies be revised quickly as evidence changes?

Is there a safe “offramp” when conditions shift?

Does enforcement punish refusal in ways that collapse exit (loss of livelihood, exclusion from essential services, irreversible social penalty)?

The point is not that interventions are always illegitimate.
The point is that correctness does not settle standing.

When a policy is treated as unchallengeable and uncorrectable, even truth becomes a lever for domination.

Plural legitimacy requires that:

error remains correctable,

coercion remains proportional,

and exit does not become annihilation.

9. Example: Platform Moderation (Authority Without Corrigibility)

Consider an online platform moderating harmful content.

A platform may be correct about:

what content is harmful

what content drives harassment

what content destabilizes discourse

But legitimacy depends on structure:

Can users contest decisions meaningfully?

Are rules transparent and auditable?

Are enforcement actions proportional?

Are there clear review triggers and rollback mechanisms?

Can users exit without being effectively erased from public participation (exit collapse)?

When rules are opaque, enforcement is unappealable, and penalties are irreversible, coordination becomes domination—even when the goal is harm reduction.

Here too, the issue is not “is moderation good?”
The issue is whether enforcement preserves the conditions that prevent arbitrary control.

10. Example: Housing and Zoning (Structural Coercion and “Voluntary” Outcomes)

Consider a community where housing is scarce.

Market outcomes are often defended as “voluntary”:

people choose where to live

prices reflect supply and demand

But if:

housing is survivalgated

mobility is constrained

debt and time binding lock people in

alternatives are infeasible
then “voluntary choice” becomes structurally coercive.

Correctness claims like:

“the market is efficient”

“property rights are fundamental”
do not solve the standing problem.

When exit collapses, market outcomes stop being mere signals and become constraint-bearing structures.
Legitimacy questions come first.

11. Why This Isn’t an Attack on Belief

This framework does not tell anyone what to believe.

It does not deny God, morality, truth, or meaning.

It draws a single boundary:

Belief does not become authority simply by being believed.

You are free to follow any truth for yourself.

Binding others requires something more.

That “something more” is not correctness—it is standing under pluralist conditions.

12. Standing: The Missing Concept

Standing is the conditional permission to constrain others’ actions under shared justification.

It is not:

power

force

popularity

righteousness

sincerity

certainty

Standing exists only where coordination remains possible after disagreement.

This is why:

good intentions can still harm

correct policies can still fail

moral certainty can still destroy communities

Standing is fragile.

That fragility is not a flaw.
It is what keeps plural societies alive.

13. What This Framework Does—and Doesn’t—Do

This companion does not:

settle moral disputes

tell you what to value

decide who is right

prescribe policy outcomes

It does:

clarify when legitimacy questions precede moral debate

explain why coercion escalates

show how authority collapses structurally

point toward repair and restraint

Think of it as infrastructure for disagreement, not a solution to disagreement.

14. How This Changes the Way We Argue (and When We Stop Arguing)

Seeing the difference between being right and having standing does not tell us what to believe, but it changes how we engage with others.

When correctness is no longer assumed to confer authority, several shifts follow:

First: arguments stop being about winning

They become about identifying where coordination is failing.

Instead of asking, “How do I prove this?” ask:

Who bears the risk if this is wrong?

Instead of asking, “Why won’t they listen?” ask:

What condition for standing has collapsed?

Second: it becomes easier to recognize when debate is no longer productive

Some conflicts persist not because evidence is lacking or intentions are bad, but because no available claim can meet the conditions required to bind others.

In such cases, escalation does not resolve disagreement.
It converts coordination into domination.

Third: disengagement becomes a legitimate option

Where exit is preserved, refusal is not sabotage.

It is a signal that standing has not been established.

Knowing when to step away can prevent harm that certainty would otherwise justify.

Finally: responsibility returns to where it belongs

Not to who is most correct.

To who is asking others to comply—and under what conditions.

This framework does not make argument easier.
It makes it more honest.

From Insight to Use

Some readers will recognize these patterns repeatedly—in institutions, policies, debates, or personal interactions.

For those readers, conceptual clarity may not be enough.

The larger project (On Relevance) is supported by additional tools designed for practical application, including a flowchart and a quick reference guide.

These tools do not provide answers or prescriptions.
They help identify when legitimacy questions precede moral, factual, or political debate—and where coordination has collapsed into coercion.

They are intended to be used selectively, under pressure, and without requiring agreement with any particular belief system.

Closing: Why Being Right Isn’t Enough

Being right feels powerful.

But power without standing breaks coordination.
And coordination without accountability becomes domination.

Pluralist societies survive not because they are correct, but because they are repairable.

This book offers no final answers.

It offers something rarer:

a way to tell when answers—however correct—can no longer hold us together.
