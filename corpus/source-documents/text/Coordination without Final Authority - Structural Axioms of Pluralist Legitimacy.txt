Coordination without Final Authority: Structural Axioms of Pluralist Legitimacy

Abstract

Contemporary pluralistic societies face a distinctive problem: without a universally accepted final authority—whether divine, moral, or epistemic—on what basis can we justify the exercise of power or the resolution of fundamental disagreements? This paper proposes a structural approach to legitimacy in pluralist societies, grounded not in moral consensus but in the inherent requirements for stable, non-dominating coordination. Building on the concept of standing—the capacity of an agent or institution to have its directives bind others—we introduce two foundational axioms for pluralist legitimacy. The Axiom of Pluralistic Inclusion formalizes the principle that no agent can wield authority without first being included in the community of mutual recognition. The Axiom of Symmetry holds that social coordination is structurally stable only when all agents enjoy an equal standing in shared decision-making; any persistent asymmetry in standing generates systemic fragility—heightened resistance, feedback failures, and tendencies toward breakdown. We articulate these axioms in dialogue with classical and contemporary ideas of consent, equality, and non-domination (from social contract theory to Pettit’s republicanism), while moving beyond moral and procedural approaches to legitimacy. Instead, we outline a “social physics” of legitimacy: a set of invariant structural conditions that any pluralistic social order must satisfy to remain stable and avoid domination. These structural conditions are argued to be necessary conditions for a claim to political or moral authority to be considered legitimate in a deeply diverse society.

Keywords: Pluralism; Legitimacy; Standing; Social Coordination; Political Authority

Introduction

Modern pluralist societies are characterized by deep diversity in moral, religious, and ideological commitments. In the absence of a single, overarching source of ultimate authority—whether divine command, moral truth, or absolute sovereign—such societies struggle with the question of legitimacy: what gives any law, norm, or decision the right to bind those who do not already agree with it? Classical liberal philosophy responded by positing consent or social contracts as the foundation of political authority. From John Locke and Thomas Jefferson to Jean-Jacques Rousseau, theorists argued that governments derive their just powers only from the consent of the governed (as famously stated in the United States’ Declaration of Independence in 1776). Rousseau, for example, wrote that “no man has a natural authority over his fellows” and that force alone cannot create right, implying that legitimate authority must be founded on convention or agreement (Rousseau 1762). In more recent thought, John Rawls and Jürgen Habermas have sought legitimacy through public reason and fair procedures, proposing that political power is justified only when exercised in ways that all citizens can, in principle, accept as free and equal participants (Rawls 1993; Habermas 1996). Similarly, republican theorists such as Philip Pettit emphasize freedom as non-domination, insisting that social and political institutions must be arranged so that no person is at the mercy of another’s unchecked will (Pettit 1997). Despite these rich traditions, a core challenge remains: How can we coordinate social life and resolve fundamental disagreements without appealing to any shared, transcendent authority, and without permitting domination?

This paper offers a new approach to that question by examining the structural conditions for stable, non-dominating coordination in a pluralist society. Rather than grounding legitimacy in a specific moral value or the will of the majority (which may be absent or contested under conditions of pluralism), this approach derives formal constraints on social systems that seek to avoid domination. The guiding insight is that coordinating action in the absence of a final arbiter faces constraints analogous to those in physical or computational systems. Certain structural features (such as feedback loops, equilibrium conditions, and inclusive participation) determine whether a social order can maintain stability and adaptiveness over time in the face of internal diversity. Crucially, if these structural conditions are violated, a society will tend toward fragility, conflict, and collapse, regardless of the righteousness or popularity of its ruling ideologies. In other words, stability in a pluralist society is not simply a matter of moral consensus or charismatic authority, but of meeting the hard requirements for sustainable coordination among agents who do not share a common ultimate authority.

In what follows, we develop this structural framework of legitimacy step by step. We begin by clarifying the concept of standing as the basis of legitimate authority under pluralism. We then propose two foundational axioms: (1) the Axiom of Pluralistic Inclusion, which stipulates that everyone subject to social coordination must have a route out of total exclusion (null standing) into the community of recognized participants; and (2) the Axiom of Symmetry, which requires that interactions among agents preserve an equality of standing (no one is allowed to permanently dominate or be dominated). We discuss how these axioms relate to, yet crucially extend, existing ideas in philosophy and social theory, and we illustrate their dynamic implications with a simplified model of social coordination. Finally, we consider how this structural perspective can inform debates on legitimacy, rights, and justice, suggesting that it provides a stern check on power: even widely accepted or well-intentioned acts of governance will fail the test of legitimacy if they sidestep the structural axioms of inclusion and symmetry.

The Problem of Pluralism and Legitimacy

By a pluralist society, we mean one in which individuals and groups hold irreducibly diverse worldviews, religious beliefs, moral doctrines, and interests, such that no single perspective or authority is universally accepted. Modern liberal democracies are paradigmatic of this condition: they encompass multiple religions, ideologies, and cultural identities, none of which can claim final authority over everyone else. In these societies, the traditional sources of unquestioned authority—whether divine revelation, moral absolutes, or the commands of a monarch—can no longer serve as common foundations for political order. As Friedrich Nietzsche memorably observed, modernity is marked by the “death of God,” by which he meant the dissolution of any uncontested transcendent source of truth or legitimacy (Nietzsche 1882). In a sense, pluralism represents the institutional and social manifestation of this condition: we must live together without a guaranteed consensus on ultimate truths. This presents a profound coordination problem: how can a community govern itself and resolve conflicts without resorting to sheer coercion, when its members fundamentally disagree about why any particular decision would be justified?

Liberal theorists have long grappled with this problem. The social contract tradition (from the 17th–18th century writings of Locke and Rousseau to modern interpretations) held that political legitimacy arises from a kind of rational agreement or consent among individuals to form a governing authority (Rousseau 1762). In the twentieth century, John Rawls proposed that legitimacy in a pluralistic democracy must be based on public reason, meaning that the exercise of political power should be justifiable to all citizens using common reasoning and values that do not depend on any single sectarian doctrine (Rawls 1993). Jürgen Habermas offered a related formulation with his theory of communicative action: legitimate norms, he argued, are those that could be accepted by all affected parties in an ideal discourse free from coercion (Habermas 1996). These approaches underscore a critical point: in a pluralist society, legitimacy must rest on inclusion (everyone’s interests are taken into account) and equality (each person’s voice or vote counts in some fundamental way), rather than on any external theological or metaphysical truth. It is no coincidence that democratic maxims like one-person-one-vote and the consent of the governed have become cornerstones of modern notions of legitimacy (U.S. Declaration of Independence, 1776).

Yet, traditional theories often assume what they set out to guarantee: they presuppose a framework of fairness or equality (e.g. a foundational commitment to treating persons as free and equal) in order to justify political outcomes. What remains less examined is the possibility that these egalitarian preconditions are not merely ethical ideals but structural necessities for any stable coordination in the absence of a final arbiter. In this paper, we make that case. We argue that the requirements of inclusion and symmetrical standing function like conservation laws or equilibrium conditions in a social system: violate them, and the system inevitably generates internal friction, resistance, and pathologies that undermine its own stability. In this perspective, familiar political values like non-domination, accountability, and the “consent of the governed” can be reinterpreted not only as moral imperatives but as preconditions for the very possibility of a pluralist social order. This approach shifts our focus from the content of particular laws or policies to the underlying structure of our shared institutions. Regardless of what specific policies a society adopts, if the structural axioms of legitimacy are not met, those policies—ahead of any moral evaluation—lack a stable right to command obedience.

Standing as the Basis of Legitimacy

To ground these ideas, we first examine the notion of standing. In everyday terms, to “have standing” means to have the right to participate or be heard in a certain decision or debate. In legal contexts, for example, having standing means one is recognized as a proper party before the court, with a right to bring a case. In our broader political and moral context, standing refers to an agent’s recognized capacity to make binding claims or demands within a shared normative order. It is a kind of legitimating status. To possess standing is to count as a valid participant in social coordination, someone whose interests, voice, and agency must be included in the decision-making calculus (otherwise that person is, in Arendt’s term, a rightless person outside the community) (Arendt 1951).

Under conditions of pluralism, the concept of standing becomes crucial. If no one’s moral or religious vision is universally accepted, then no command is self-justifying; any claim to authority must clear an initial hurdle of being recognized as having standing by those expected to comply. Note that standing is distinct from mere power or de facto control. A dictator or ruling party might have the coercive power to enforce its will, but it lacks standing in a pluralist sense if its directives cannot be justified without assuming a privileged ideology or silencing dissent. Standing is also not equivalent to being correct or morally right: even if one faction is convinced of the truth or righteousness of its cause, imposing that cause on others who reasonably reject it would fail the standing test unless certain structural conditions are satisfied. Thus, standing provides a formal threshold test for legitimacy: before we even debate whether a law is just or a policy beneficial, we ask whether the process by which it was adopted and imposed respects the basic inclusion and equality of all participants. If not, the outcome is illegitimate regardless of its intrinsic merits, because it arises from a tainted process of coordination (one that, as we argue, cannot sustain stability in any case).

We can visualize an abstract model of social coordination centered on standing. Imagine a coordination mechanism at the heart of a society’s functioning. In any domain—whether politics, law, morality, or knowledge production—individual actions and claims feed into this mechanism, which then produces collective decisions or norms that guide behavior. The inputs to the mechanism are the web of interactions and impacts among individuals: who affects whom, what issues become relevant to the public, and how each person’s autonomy is at stake. The coordination mechanism evaluates these inputs in terms of standing: essentially, it filters which claims or demands are recognized as legitimate constraints on others, and which are not. A claim that passes the standing test can become a binding norm—a decision or rule that others are rightfully expected to follow. A claim that fails the test must instead be rejected or revised, and the imbalance it created calls for repair—actions to address the exclusion or asymmetry and restore legitimate order. This abstract picture will guide our development of two key structural axioms for pluralist legitimacy.

Axiom 1: Pluralistic Inclusion (The Successor Axiom)

The first axiom addresses the requirement of inclusion. It states that no member of society should remain a nullity in the scheme of social coordination. Formally, let (U) be the set of all individuals (or agents) subject to a given social order. Each agent (i \in U) has an associated standing value (\sigma_i) representing their weight or status as a participant in collective decision-making. If (\sigma_i = 0), agent i has no standing at all—their interests and voice have no recognized role in the social coordination mechanism. The Axiom of Pluralistic Inclusion declares that such a condition is inadmissible in a legitimate pluralist system: for every agent (i) in the system, there must be a possible transition from (\sigma_i = 0) (no standing) to (\sigma_i > 0) (some standing). In other words, every person must be in principle includable; no one can be consigned permanently to utter political irrelevance.

We can express this axiom with a metaphor drawn from mathematics: a function (S) that grants successor standing. If initially an individual has zero standing, applying this function yields the first unit of standing:
[ S(0) = 0 + 1. ] This simple expression symbolizes the granting of the first increment of recognition to someone who was previously excluded. That “+1” is the atomic act of inclusion—an irreducible step that moves a human being from non-personhood in the eyes of the system into the circle of recognized agents. In the context of political rights, this captures the idea behind extending the franchise or basic rights protections to those who lacked them: it’s the initial acknowledgment of a person as a bearer of claims. Hannah Arendt described something similar when she wrote about the importance of having a recognized place in the world – a “right to have rights” that stateless persons are cruelly denied (Arendt 1951). The Successor Axiom gives formal structure to that intuition: a society must never create or tolerate a class of people who are, in effect, outside the scope of its moral and political concern.

Several corollaries follow from the inclusion axiom. First, each additional agent introduced via a “+1” is unique and indivisible: the new unit of standing attaches to a specific person and cannot be treated as a fungible quantity shared or transferred among individuals. Second, adding a new person with standing does not dilute or reduce the standing of others; inclusion is not a zero-sum resource to be allocated, but rather an expansion of the community of participation. Third, what we might call the Law of the First Increment states that a social system’s integrity can be measured by its commitment to universal inclusion – its willingness and ability to apply the successor function to every person within its scope. If an institution or society knowingly keeps some individuals at (\sigma = 0) (for example, by maintaining a subclass of rightless persons, disenfranchised citizens, or silenced minorities), then that entire system fails a fundamental legitimacy test. It becomes structurally coercive, since those with no standing are, by definition, governed without any route to having their say. In a robustly pluralist system, therefore, “everyone counts” in the sense that for each person there exists a pathway from non-recognition to full membership in the deliberative community. Excluding individuals entirely is not only a moral injustice; on this view, it is a structural flaw that undermines the very possibility of legitimate and stable governance.

This axiom echoes and strengthens prior insights from liberal thought. Denying basic recognition to a category of people—whether through slavery, caste systems, or permanent alienage—has long been condemned as unjust; our framework adds that it also renders the system incapable of achieving legitimate stability. Social contract theories required that all those governed be considered parties to the hypothetical contract (at least implicitly); similarly, Arendt’s “right to have rights” insisted on a baseline status of belonging as a precondition for any specific rights. The Successor Axiom recasts these intuitions as a stringent structural requirement. It tells us, for instance, that a polity in which certain people (say, a disfavored minority or non-citizen residents) have no path to political representation is not merely lacking in justice by some external standard—it is internally unstable and illegitimate by its very structure. No amount of benevolence or efficiency by the rulers can compensate for that foundational omission.

Axiom 2: Symmetry of Standing (The Equality Axiom)

The second axiom of our framework is the Axiom of Symmetry, which holds that any coordination among agents of equal fundamental status must preserve their equality of standing. In plainer terms, when individuals or groups coordinate without appealing to a shared higher authority, the only stable way to do so is to treat each participant’s agency as on an equal footing in deciding joint outcomes. If (A) and (B) must work out a common decision, symmetrical standing means that, structurally, the decision-making process does not inherently privilege one over the other. Each has an equal voice in proposing, influencing, and vetoing actions; each is equally empowered to demand justification from the other; and neither can unilaterally impose an irreversible choice on the other. Conversely, any sustained asymmetry—where (A > B) in standing (domination) or (B > A) (subjugation)—introduces instability into the coordination. The Axiom of Symmetry thus formalizes the core republican intuition of non-domination: that enduring hierarchy or unchecked authority is inconsistent with freedom and will inevitably provoke resistance and dysfunction (Pettit 1997). It also mirrors the egalitarian spirit of Rousseau’s social contract (which sought to institutionalize an equality of political right) and Rawls’s model of citizens as free and equal (Rousseau 1762; Rawls 1993). Here, however, equality of standing is not only a moral or legal principle, but a necessary condition for the system’s viability over time.

We can illustrate this by examining three stylized relationships between two agents, A and B, who need to coordinate (for example, two citizens, or two groups in society, or two branches of government). The Axiom of Symmetry encapsulates a simple proposition: durable, non-coercive coordination is possible only under conditions of equal standing (A = B), whereas any deviation from this equality (A > B or B > A) will inevitably generate forces that undermine the coordination. Notably, this is framed as a matter of systemic stability rather than merely moral preference. Domination is objectionable not only because it is unjust, but because it creates a one-sided decision process that ignites conflict and blinds the system to important information held by the subordinate party. Conversely, when agents share power equally, dissent and feedback can be expressed within the system (rather than by violent revolt or sabotage), allowing for continual adjustments and improvements to collective decisions.

It follows that maintaining legitimacy in a pluralist society is virtually synonymous with maintaining symmetry of standing. A law or institution is legitimate only if it embodies or preserves an equality of standing among those governed by it. Any claim or authority that puts some people above others, without recourse or accountability, effectively asks the system to tolerate its own progressive destabilization. For example, a legal system that formally privileges one ethnic or religious group over others, or a scientific establishment that silences dissenting voices through non-transparent gatekeeping, would be structurally illegitimate from the start. Even if such arrangements appear to “work” for a time, the Axiom of Symmetry predicts that they will incur an increasing legitimacy deficit—a buildup of social pressure, mistrust, non-compliance, and other pathologies—until reforms or collapse become inevitable.

Another implication of the Symmetry Axiom is that equality of standing must be transitive and scalable in a network of social relations. If A and B interact on equal terms, and B and C do so as well, then A and C should likewise enjoy equal standing in any joint context; otherwise an indirect hierarchy can emerge. This mirrors the logic that equality is a transitive relation and is analogous to the Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics in physics (if two systems are each in equilibrium with a third, they are in equilibrium with each other). In practice, it means that a just and stable social order cannot contain pockets of entrenched hierarchy that undercut the overall balance. For instance, even if two departments in an organization are internally egalitarian, if the relationship between those departments is hierarchical (one department’s members have systematically less say in joint decisions), the overall system violates symmetry and is liable to conflict and inefficiency.

Finally, the Symmetry Axiom implies a principle of directed repair: when inequalities of standing do arise (as they will in any real system, whether through abuses of power, economic inequalities, or historical injustices), there is a structural imperative to actively correct them. This can be understood as a kind of negative feedback mechanism that kicks in to restore balance. If one group has been unfairly marginalized or disempowered, simply ceasing the marginalization may not suffice; affirmative steps (e.g. compensatory rights, power-sharing agreements, reparations) might be required to bring that group back into equilibrium with others. The goal of repair, however, is not to invert domination (which would merely swap who is on top) but to re-establish A = B. This resonates with the insight that true peace is not achieved by one side’s victory but by restoration of equality. It also guards against the risk that efforts to correct a domination could themselves become a new form of domination in reverse—the aim is always to land on the stable plane of equal standing.

In sum, the Axiom of Symmetry enshrines equal standing as the only stable equilibrium for pluralist coordination. It provides a structural explanation for why domination and unchecked hierarchy are not only morally troubling but also practically self-defeating. By treating legitimacy as a property of systems in equilibrium, this axiom shifts the discussion from whether inequality is unfair to the deeper question of whether inequality is dynamically sustainable in a pluralist context. The answer it gives is no: any persistent asymmetry in standing will act as a kind of stress on the social fabric, a latent source of fragility that eventually demands redress.

Toward a “Social Physics” of Legitimacy

The two axioms—Pluralistic Inclusion and Symmetry of Standing—form the backbone of a proposed structural theory of legitimacy. They function akin to laws of social dynamics: just as certain principles in physics (like conservation of energy or entropy increase) constrain what physical systems can do, these axioms constrain what kinds of social coordination can endure in a pluralistic context. We might say that human societies have natural “attractors” and “failure modes” determined by these structural parameters. No pluralistic society can remain stable if it permanently excludes some members from participation, or if it builds lasting hierarchies of control among participants. Over time, such a society will expend more and more effort to manage conflicts and suppression, leaking energy and information until it either transforms or falls apart. By contrast, a society that hews to inclusion and symmetry will be more adaptive and robust, able to correct course and respond to crises without resorting to tyranny. These claims are supported not only by theoretical reasoning but also by historical and empirical evidence. We have repeatedly seen autocratic or exclusionary regimes face rebellion, stagnation, or collapse stemming from their internal strains, whereas more inclusive and egalitarian institutions tend to be more stable and prosperous over the long run (Acemoglu & Robinson 2012).

Crucially, this structural perspective complements, but also constrains, traditional normative frameworks. It does not attempt to resolve every ethical question or policy debate by itself. Instead, it operates as a gatekeeping condition: it identifies when a claim or decision fails the minimal test of legitimacy in a pluralist order. We need not all agree on what the good or the just is in order to reject certain kinds of authority as illegitimate. For example, consider a public policy justified by a particular religious doctrine that many citizens do not share. Traditional debates might focus on whether the doctrine is true or whether imposing it violates liberal neutrality. Our framework would ask a prior question: does enforcing this doctrine sideline some people’s standing (because they do not accept the doctrine and have no say in its authority)? If so, then regardless of the doctrine’s content, the imposition lacks structural legitimacy because it contravenes the inclusion axiom. Similarly, if an economic system yields extreme imbalances of power—say, a small group of wealthy individuals effectively controls political decisions—one might argue about the justice of redistribution. But our test would highlight that such a power imbalance is itself a source of instability and illegitimacy (violating symmetry), likely to generate social “heat” in the form of unrest or breakdown unless corrected. By 	providing a common structural standard, the framework can thereby cut through some ideological disputes: any proposal, whether couched in terms of efficiency, morality, tradition, or faith, must still include everyone affected and treat them as equals in power if it is to result in non-arbitrary, sustainable coordination. If it cannot meet those conditions, it fails the pluralist legitimacy test, even before we get to debating its merits.

One might ask whether this approach introduces its own kind of “final authority” in the form of these axioms. We emphasize that the structural axioms are presented not as metaphysical certainties but as pragmatic imperatives. The claim is conditional: if we wish to achieve a stable, non-oppressive social order under conditions of pluralism, then we must adhere to these principles of inclusion and symmetry. The justification for the axioms lies in their explanatory and predictive power. They help us understand why certain social arrangements repeatedly fail—why, for instance, societies that disenfranchise segments of their population or that allow unchecked concentrations of power experience chronic conflict, corruption, and crisis. These failures are not random; they reflect structural laws. Conversely, societies that come closest to universal inclusion and power-sharing tend to exhibit greater resilience, creativity, and peace, which suggests these principles tap into something fundamental about how independent agents can successfully coordinate. The perspective is akin to a scientific one: it invites us to consider legitimacy in terms of cause and effect (inputs and outputs of a social system) rather than in terms of ultimate justifications. In this way, it aligns with Philip Pettit’s insight that freedom from domination has instrumental as well as intrinsic value—the absence of domination is what makes constructive social cooperation possible at all (Pettit 1997).

Conclusion

We have argued that pluralist societies—communities without a shared ultimate authority—must satisfy two structural requirements to achieve legitimacy and stability: universal inclusion (every individual can gain standing as a participant), and symmetry of standing (no permanent hierarchies of domination). These twin requirements, cast here as the Axiom of Pluralistic Inclusion and the Axiom of Symmetry, can be seen as formalizing long-standing democratic intuitions: that everyone matters, and no one is above or below the law. But unlike traditional formulations, we ground these principles not merely in moral commitments or social contracts, but in the dynamics of coordination itself. A pluralist society that violates inclusion or symmetry is, on this view, not only unjust but also courting its own failure: it will generate hidden costs, “resistance” pressures, and information pathologies that ultimately cause breakdown. Thus, inclusion and symmetry emerge as the non-negotiable parameters of a free and enduring pluralist order.

This structural perspective provides a clarifying lens on prominent philosophical debates. It gives us a way to reconcile liberal and republican insights: the demand for consent and public reason (as in Rawls) and the demand for robust non-domination (as in Pettit) are both upheld, but as consequences of deeper requirements for systemic equilibrium (Rawls 1993; Pettit 1997). It also echoes Hannah Arendt’s concern that a just political community must guarantee every person a place where their voice counts (Arendt 1951). By showing that these are not merely high ideals but practical conditions for societal survival, the framework sidesteps perennial worries about relativism or moral disagreement under pluralism. We do not need a unanimous vision of the good; we need inclusive structures and feedback-rich processes that allow us to navigate our disagreements without oppression. This resonates with John Rawls’s later observation that modern societies must discover “terms of cooperation [that] all those citizens… might endorse,” and that these terms must remain stable over time despite our varied convictions (Rawls 1999). The structural axioms can be seen as prerequisites for any such terms of cooperation to take root and persist.

Much work remains to be done in fleshing out this framework. Future inquiries might explore how these axioms apply in complex real-world scenarios involving multinational institutions, economic inequality, or historical injustices, or how they intersect with concepts like human rights and democratic representation. Additionally, while our focus has been on necessary conditions for legitimacy, one could ask what more (beyond inclusion and symmetry) is required for a society to be not just stable but substantively just. Nonetheless, by illuminating the structural “laws” of pluralist coordination, we provide a foundation on which these further normative questions can be more securely built. We suggest that any political or social theory that hopes to guide pluralistic societies must, at minimum, respect the social-physical constraints identified here—much as any bridge must respect the laws of physics. In a world of deep pluralism, the only way to remain many while acting as one is by ensuring that none of us is ever rendered nobody, and none of us is allowed to dominate everybody.

Summary: This paper develops a structural theory of legitimacy tailored to pluralist societies, where no ultimate authority is universally accepted. It introduces two key axioms: Pluralistic Inclusion, which demands that every individual can obtain standing in collective decision-making (no one is permanently excluded from having a voice); and Symmetry of Standing, which requires that all participants have equal standing (no one is permitted to dominate others). By treating these principles as necessary structural conditions—akin to laws of social dynamics—the paper argues that any social order violating inclusion or symmetry will generate internal instabilities (“social friction,” loss of feedback, illegitimate coercion) and eventually undermine itself. The framework thus provides a non-moral, systemic basis for the norms of democratic inclusion and equality, explaining why they are indispensable for a durable, non-oppressive pluralist society. In dialogue with thinkers like Arendt, Rawls, Habermas, and Pettit, the paper shows that freedom as non-domination and the “right to have rights” can be understood as emergent requirements of stable coordination. This structural perspective offers a unifying lens for debates on political authority, justice, and rights, suggesting that even in a world of divergent beliefs, legitimacy is governed by invariant principles of inclusion and power symmetry.
