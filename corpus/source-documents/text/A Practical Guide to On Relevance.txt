A Practical Guide to NonDominating Coordination

Using the Relevance Framework in Pluralist Communities

What This Guide Is (and Is Not)

This guide is:

A diagnostic tool for collective decisionmaking under disagreement

A way to identify when coordination becomes domination

A shared language for discussing harm, standing, and legitimacy

A framework for repair when coordination fails

This guide is not:

A moral code

A political ideology

A consensusbuilding method

A way to determine who is “right”

A requirement anyone must accept

Participation is voluntary. Exit is always permitted.

When to Use This Framework

Use this guide only when a group or institution claims the right to:

bind others,

restrict behavior,

allocate risk,

impose costs,

or enforce rules

If no one is being bound or harmed, this framework has nothing to say.

The Core Principle (Plain Language)

Disagreement does not justify harm.
Authority does not excuse harm.
Coordination is legitimate only under specific structural conditions.

Everything below operationalizes that principle.

The Diagnostic Flow (OnePage Overview)

When a collective claim is made (“we must do X”), ask the following in order:

What is being coordinated?

Who is being constrained or harmed?

Who has standing to make this claim?

Who bears the risk and cost?

Is exit preserved?

Is the constraint proportional and repairable?

If the claim fails at any step, it cannot be justified as nondominating coordination.

Step 1: Identify the Coordination Claim

Ask:

What action is being required, restricted, or enforced?

What happens to people who refuse or dissent?

Is this a request, a norm, or a mandate?

Key distinction:
Persuasion is not coordination.
Coordination begins where refusal carries consequences.

Step 2: Identify the Impact on Autonomy

Ask:

Who loses options if this claim is enforced?

What freedoms are reduced?

What choices become unavailable?

If no one’s autonomy is reduced, domination is not at issue.

If autonomy is reduced, continue.

Step 3: Determine Standing

Standing is not about truth, expertise, sincerity, or power.

Standing depends on risk inheritance.

Ask:

Who bears the cost if this coordination fails?

Who is exposed to harm?

Who cannot opt out safely?

Rule of thumb:
Those who bear the risk must have standing in the decision.

Claims imposed by those insulated from risk lack standing.

Step 4: Examine Risk Allocation

Ask:

Are harms evenly distributed?

Are costs imposed on dissenters?

Are vulnerable parties disproportionately exposed?

If risks are asymmetric and imposed without consent, domination risk is high.

Step 5: Check for Exit

Exit is the strongest nondomination safeguard.

Ask:

Can individuals or groups opt out?

Is exit meaningful, or merely formal?

Does exit carry punishment, stigma, or deprivation?

If exit collapses, coordination becomes coercive.

Step 6: Test Proportionality and Repair

Ask:

Is the constraint the leastharmful option?

Is it reversible?

Is there a clear repair path if harm occurs?

Can the policy be revised based on feedback?

Irreversible, totalizing, or unrepairable constraints are dominationprone.

Interpreting Outcomes

Legitimate Coordination

All conditions satisfied:

Standing is grounded in risk

Exit is preserved

Harm is minimized and repairable

Disagreement may remain, but domination is avoided.

Contested Coordination

Some conditions partially met:

Standing unclear

Exit fragile

Risk uneven

Proceed only with safeguards and review mechanisms.

Domination

Any of the following apply:

Standing is asserted by authority alone

Risk is imposed on others without consent

Exit is blocked or punished

Harm is justified by belief, truth, or necessity

The claim fails structurally, regardless of intention or correctness.

Repair Pathways (When Coordination Fails)

When domination is diagnosed, the response is repair, not blame.

Possible repairs include:

Redistributing risk

Adding exit options

Narrowing scope of enforcement

Introducing sunset clauses

Shifting from mandate to voluntary participation

Creating parallel coordination pathways

If repair is impossible, the coordination claim must be withdrawn.

What This Framework Cannot Do

Be explicit about limits:

It cannot guarantee harmony

It cannot resolve all conflicts

It cannot prevent tragedy

It cannot replace moral judgment

It cannot force good faith

It only constrains justifications for harm.

A Minimal Facilitation Script (Optional)

When conflict arises, a facilitator can ask:

“What harm are we trying to prevent?”

“Who bears the cost of this decision?”

“Can people exit without punishment?”

“How would we repair harm if we’re wrong?”

“What would make this less coercive?”

If the group cannot answer these, coordination should pause.

How This Helps Pluralist Communities

This framework:

Prevents authority escalation

Stops moral shortcuts

Keeps disagreement survivable

Preserves diversity without paralysis

Enables collective action without moral permission to harm

It does not require shared values—only shared constraints.

Final Reminder

This framework does not tell communities what to do.
It tells them what they cannot justify doing to each other.

Use it when harm is at stake.
Ignore it when it is not.

That restraint is the point.

Concept Inquiry: On Relevance

Structural Constraints on Standing and Authority Across Domains

Dear Professor Endicott,

I am writing to inquire whether Oxford University Press might be interested in a manuscript that addresses a persistent problem in legal and political philosophy: under what structural conditions do authority claims acquire standing to bind others under pluralism without collapsing into domination?

The manuscript, On Relevance: Structural Constraints on Standing and Authority Across Domains, is not a moral theory, a political program, or an account of truth. Instead, it offers a structural framework for evaluating authority claims prior to questions of correctness, justice, or value, with particular relevance to jurisprudence, public authority, and institutional legitimacy.

The Problem

Pluralist societies cannot rely on final authority—moral, epistemic, religious, or democratic—without coercion. Yet the absence of final authority does not eliminate the need for coordination, nor does it prevent claims from continuing to bind others through law, institutions, and social practices. Contemporary approaches tend either to reintroduce final authority implicitly (through moral realism, public reason, or procedural consensus), or to treat authority as reducible to power.

The result is a recurring legitimacy failure: correctness is mistaken for standing, and disagreement is converted into domination rather than coordinated accommodation.

The Contribution

The manuscript proposes a structural framework for evaluating standing based on interactional conditions—accountability, exit, corrigibility, and nondomination—that must be satisfied for authority claims to bind others under pluralism. The framework does not adjudicate outcomes, determine values, or prescribe legal or political arrangements. It specifies when authority claims remain compatible with pluralist coordination and when they collapse into domination.

Importantly, the framework is explicitly nonfinal. If adopted, it does not function as a new authority, moral or legal. It does not determine what ought to be done. Questions of right and wrong, justice and injustice, remain open to other theories. The framework instead clarifies the structural conditions under which such theories may legitimately bind others at all.

Position in the Literature

Methodologically, the project is closest to work that addresses conditions of validity and legitimacy rather than substantive justification. It is informed by, and in conversation with:

H.L.A. Hart’s account of legal validity and secondary rules,

jurisprudential work on coercion and authority,

Hannah Arendt’s distinction between authority and force, and

empirical work on nondominating coordination and institutional robustness.

The manuscript differs from existing approaches by generalizing the legitimacy problem across domains, rather than confining it to law or democratic institutions, while maintaining a jurisprudential focus on standing, coercion, and constraint.

Scope

The manuscript is a singleauthor monograph of approximately [136,000] words. It moves from foundational clarification to crossdomain analysis (law, politics, morality, institutions, technology), without offering prescriptive conclusions. Its intended audience includes scholars in legal philosophy, jurisprudence, and political theory concerned with authority, legitimacy, and pluralism.

Given its focus on the nature of authority and standing rather than normative adjudication, I believe it may be a suitable fit for Oxford Legal Philosophy. If the project seems potentially appropriate, I would be glad to share a formal proposal, table of contents, or sample chapters.

Thank you for your time and consideration.
