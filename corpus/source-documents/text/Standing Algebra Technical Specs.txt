Abstract

This paper introduces Standing Algebra, a manysorted firstorder axiomatic system governing admissible transformations in plural systems. The theory is formulated over agents equipped with a numeric measure and a directed dependency relation, together with a class of operations constrained by invariantpreserving predicates. Unlike optimizationbased, ordertheoretic, or trajectorybased frameworks, Standing Algebra is deliberately nonselective: it filters impermissible transformations without inducing any preference, ordering, or choice among those that remain.

The axioms are organized into two tiers. Tier1 axioms define the algebraic and graphtheoretic structure of agents and measures. Tier2 axioms constrain operations via admissibility, symmetry, bounded effect, idempotence, and directed repair. From these axioms, the paper derives a collection of foundational lemmas establishing definability, nonemptiness, invariance under model isomorphism, noncollapse, and closure properties of legitimate operations.

A central result is that the legitimate operations in any model form a nonempty, invariant, noncollapsed intersection of definable constraint sets. This structure is shown to constitute an Interoperable Constraint Geometry: a constraint geometry on transformations rather than on states or trajectories, preserved under reinterpretation across models. The emergence of this geometry is proved as a theorem, not assumed.

Further results establish the necessity and conditional universality of the axioms, the independence of all axiom groups via countermodels, and a nondeterminacy theorem showing that legitimacy does not entail uniqueness or preference. Formal separation theorems demonstrate that Standing Algebra is not reducible to viability theory, optimization frameworks, ordertheoretic systems, or constraint satisfaction problems.

The paper presents a complete axiomatic specification, full proofs of all main results, explicit dependency structure, and exhaustive appendices, yielding a closed and auditable mathematical theory of nonselective, invariantpreserving transformation constraints.

1. Introduction

The mathematical analysis of constrained dynamical and multiagent systems has traditionally focused on identifying admissible trajectories within a prescribed state space. Prominent examples include differential inclusions, invariant set theory, and viability theory, where constraints are expressed as subsets of a state space and admissibility is defined relative to forward evolution under allowable dynamics.

This paper develops a formally distinct approach. Rather than characterizing admissible trajectories within a fixed geometric domain, we present an axiomatic framework that constrains the space of transformations themselves. The resulting structure gives rise to what may be described as an interoperable constraint geometry: a geometry not of states or trajectories, but of admissible operations acting on plural systems.

The framework, referred to as Standing Algebra and denoted is formulated as a manysorted firstorder theory. Its primary object of study is the set of transformations that preserve a collection of invariant structural constraints across interacting agents. These constraints are enforced axiomatically and are independent of any particular optimization objective, preference ordering, or equilibrium notion.

1.1 From StateSpace Constraints to TransformationSpace Geometry

In classical constrained systems theory, admissibility is typically defined pointwise or trajectorywise: a state or trajectory is viable if it remains within a constraint set under the system’s evolution. The underlying geometry is therefore a geometry of subsets of a state space, often endowed with topological, convex, or smooth structure.

Standing Algebra shifts the level of abstraction. The theory does not specify a privileged state space or a family of admissible trajectories. Instead, it axiomatizes conditions under which transformations—viewed abstractly as operations acting on agents or system configurations—are permitted or forbidden.

As a consequence, admissibility is no longer a property of points or paths, but of elements of an operation space. The axioms of carve out a subset of operations satisfying invariance, symmetry, idempotence, boundedness, and nonescalation constraints. The collection of all such operations, considered modulo definable equivalence, constitutes a geometric object in its own right.

This motivates the term interoperable constraint geometry: a constraint geometry defined on transformations rather than on states, and formulated so as to remain invariant under reinterpretation across domains.

1.2 Interoperable Constraint Geometry

The phrase interoperable constraint geometry is not used here to denote a previously established mathematical theory. Rather, it is introduced as a descriptive term for the structural phenomenon that emerges from the axioms of .

Formally, the geometry arises as follows:

The language of defines a class of structures with agents, dependency relations, and numeric measures.

Tier1 axioms constrain the algebraic and graphtheoretic structure of agents.

Tier2 axioms constrain the admissible operations acting on those structures.

The set of operations satisfying all Tier2 predicates forms a space of admissible transformations closed under rerunning and restricted by bounded drift and symmetry conditions.

What is geometric about this construction is not metric structure or smoothness, but constraint interaction: multiple invariant conditions intersect to define a feasible region in the space of operations. What makes the geometry interoperable is that the axioms do not encode domainspecific semantics. Any system that can be interpreted as a model of inherits the same admissibility geometry, regardless of whether the agents are computational processes, institutional actors, or abstract nodes in a dependency graph.

No claim is made that this geometry is unique or universal among all possible constraint frameworks. The claim is more modest and more precise: the axioms of define a nontrivial and reusable constraint geometry on transformations, one that is not reducible to existing statespace formulations.

1.3 Divergence from Viability Theory

Although the theory developed here shares a family resemblance with viability theory, the divergence is substantive and structural.

Viability theory studies the evolution of states under differential inclusions, with admissibility defined relative to a constraint set in state space. Even in abstract formulations, the central object remains a viability kernel: a subset of states from which admissible trajectories emanate.

By contrast, :

Does not presuppose a state space endowed with topology, convexity, or smooth structure;

Does not define admissibility in terms of forward trajectories;

Does not characterize kernels or maximal invariant sets;

Does not select or privilege particular evolutions.

Instead, admissibility is defined at the level of operations. The axioms constrain how transformations may alter agent measures and dependency relations, independently of any notion of temporal evolution or optimal control.

From a geometric standpoint, viability theory yields a geometry of reachable states under constraints. Standing Algebra yields a geometry of permissible transformations under invariants. These are formally orthogonal constructions. One may, in principle, study viable dynamics within a model of , but the viability constraints would be additional structure, not intrinsic to the theory.

1.4 NonSelectivity and Structural Filtering

A central design feature of is that it is filtering but nonselective. The axioms eliminate entire classes of transformations but do not induce any ordering, ranking, or preference among those that remain.

This is not a limitation but a theorembacked property of the formal system. As shown later, the language of is insufficient to define any binary relation that would order legitimate operations. Consequently, distinct admissible operations may coexist without the theory entailing a choice between them.

From the perspective of interoperable constraint geometry, this nonselectivity is essential. It ensures that the geometry describes the shape of the admissible region without collapsing it to a distinguished point.

1.5 Positioning and Scope

The contribution of this paper is therefore not a new solution concept, control method, or equilibrium notion. It is the explicit axiomatization of a transformationlevel constraint geometry that:

is independent of optimization and preference aggregation,

is compatible with multiple interpretations,

and diverges structurally from statespacebased constraint theories such as viability theory.

The remainder of the paper develops this framework rigorously, beginning with a precise modeltheoretic specification of the language and structures involved.

7.9 Scope and NonClaims of the Theory

This subsection records, explicitly and formally, the scope of the theory and the claims it does not make. The purpose of this statement is to delimit the logical consequences of the axioms and theorems established above and to prevent misinterpretation of the results proved in Sections 3–7.

All statements below concern nonentailments of the theory and are intended as formal clarifications rather than substantive additions.

7.9.1 Logical Scope

The theory is a manysorted firstorder axiomatic system whose primary objects are:

agents equipped with a numeric measure and a dependency relation, and

operations constrained by invariantpreserving predicates.

All results proved in this paper are consequences of the axioms explicitly stated in Sections 3–4 and Appendices A–B. No additional semantic, temporal, probabilistic, or topological structure is assumed or derived.

In particular, is a theory about admissibility and legitimacy of transformations, not about outcomes, trajectories, equilibria, or optimization.

7.9.2 NonClaims Concerning Selection and Optimality

The theory does not assert, entail, or encode:

the existence of a unique legitimate operation,

a preference ordering over legitimate operations,

an optimal, maximal, or minimal legitimate operation,

or any selection principle among legitimate operations.

This is not a limitation but a provable property of the theory (Theorem 6.7). Any selection, ranking, or optimization imposed on legitimate operations necessarily arises from structure external to and cannot be derived within its language.

7.9.3 NonClaims Concerning Dynamics and Time

The theory does not introduce:

a time parameter,

a notion of trajectory or evolution,

or a dynamical system in the classical sense.

Although operations may be iterated, iteration is constrained by idempotence (Lemma 5.5), which precludes trajectorybased semantics. Consequently, does not subsume, generalize, or reexpress dynamical systems theory, control theory, or viability theory (Theorem 7.6).

7.9.4 NonClaims Concerning Expressive Universality

The theory does not claim:

completeness with respect to all possible constraint theories,

universality across all mathematical models of coordination,

equivalence to categorytheoretic, ordertheoretic, or optimizationbased frameworks,

or reducibility of all constraint geometries to its axioms.

The universality results proved in Section 6 are conditional: they apply only to systems satisfying the structural conditions made explicit in Definition 6.4 and Theorems 6.5–6.6.

7.9.5 NonClaims Concerning Semantics and Interpretation

No semantic interpretation of agents, measures, dependencies, or operations is fixed by the theory. Any interpretation—computational, institutional, organizational, or otherwise—constitutes an external model of the axioms and does not alter their formal content.

In particular, the theory makes no claims concerning meaning, intent, or purpose of agents or operations beyond their formal properties.

7.9.6 Summary

The theory :

does define a nonselective, invariantpreserving constraint theory over transformations;

does induce an interoperable constraint geometry as a provable consequence of its axioms;

does not select, optimize, rank, or prioritize legitimate operations;

does not rederive existing mathematical frameworks;

does not rely on unstated assumptions or external semantics.

All claims made by the theory are exhaustively enumerated and formally justified in the preceding sections and appendices.

7.10 Explicit Comparison Boundary with Existing Frameworks

This subsection records, in formal terms, the boundaries separating the theory from other mathematical frameworks. These boundaries are not matters of interpretation or emphasis; they follow directly from the axioms and theorems established above.

7.10.1 Boundary with Optimization and OrderTheoretic Frameworks

Optimization theory and ordertheoretic frameworks presuppose the existence of a partial or total order on a space of candidates.

By Theorem 6.7 and Theorem 7.4, no definable ordering exists on the set of legitimate operations in . Consequently, no objective function, extremal principle, lattice structure, or dominance relation is definable within the theory.

Therefore, is not equivalent to, nor a reformulation of, any optimizationbased or ordertheoretic framework.

7.10.2 Boundary with Dynamical Systems and Viability Theory

Dynamical systems and viability theory are formulated over:

a timeindexed evolution,

trajectories or flows,

admissibility defined over paths in state space.

In :

no time parameter exists,

no notion of trajectory is definable,

admissibility is defined on operations rather than paths,

legitimate operations are idempotent (Lemma 5.5).

By Theorem 7.6, no structurepreserving mapping exists between legitimate operations and viable trajectories or kernels. Accordingly, is not a reformulation, extension, or abstraction of viability theory.

7.10.3 Boundary with Constraint Satisfaction Problems

Constraint satisfaction frameworks encode constraints whose solutions are selected or enumerated.

By Lemma 5.13 and Theorem 6.7, the admissible region of is noncollapsed and nonselective: it admits multiple legitimate operations without any distinguished solution.

By Theorem 7.8, no finite CSP formulation captures the structure of legitimate operations in .

7.10.4 Boundary with Categorical and Algebraic Selection Frameworks

Categorytheoretic constructions that yield universal or terminal objects presuppose a selection principle.

By Theorem 6.11 (maximal nonselectivity), any attempt to introduce such a principle within either collapses the admissible region or violates the axioms.

Thus, while categorical semantics may be imposed externally, is not internally categorical in this sense.

Concluding Boundary Statement

The distinctions above are established by formal impossibility results, not by informal comparison. They collectively demonstrate that occupies a distinct mathematical position: a nonselective, transformationlevel constraint theory whose properties are not inherited from existing frameworks.
